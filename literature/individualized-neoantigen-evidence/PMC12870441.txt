In this review, we first delineate the immunological and pathological hallmarks of mCRC metastases, highlighting organ-specific immune evasion mechanisms and actionable molecular targets. ## Introduction
Colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks as the third most commonly diagnosed malignancy and the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [ – ]. Approximately 15% of patients present with metastases at initial diagnosis, and nearly half eventually develop distant spread, most commonly to the liver and peritoneum [ ]. Although current chemotherapy, anti-EGFR/anti-VEGF biologics, and locoregional interventions have led to incremental gains, the five-year survival rate for metastatic CRC (mCRC) remains below 15%, indicating that durable benefit requires more than intensifying existing regimens. Immunotherapy offers conceptual promise, yet its success in CRC has largely been limited to the minority of mismatch-repair-deficient or microsatellite-instability-high tumors [ – ]. The majority of mCRC is microsatellite-stable and characterized by low intrinsic immunogenicity, inefficient effector T cell trafficking, and a dominantly suppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), all of which contribute to resistance against immune checkpoint blockade. Attempts to transform the cold tumor to hot tumor through chemotherapy, anti-angiogenic agents, radiotherapy, or oncolytic viruses have resulted in only modest and transient responses, suggesting that the true barrier lies not in how much the immune system is activated, but in site-specific immunosuppression and delivery failure within metastatic niches.
The metastatic TME in mCRC represents an active, niche-dependent ecosystem shaped by specific molecular circuits and immunologic pressures, many of which constitute actionable therapeutic targets [ ]. In the liver, fenestrated endothelium, Kupffer cells (KCs), and stellate cells generate a tolerogenic milieu rich in TGF-β, IL-10, and VEGF [ – ]. Liver sinusoidal endothelial cells and tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) upregulate scavenger and mannose receptors, while abnormal vasculature and a dense perivascular matrix hinder T cell infiltration and antigen-presenting cell (APC) priming. Chemokine axes, such as CXCL12-CXCR4 and CCL2-CCR2, in concert with the adenosine pathway (CD39, CD73, A2A receptor) and IDO1-kynurenine-AhR signaling, reinforce T cell exhaustion and myeloid dominance [ – ]. Additional stromal programs, including TGF-β–SMAD signaling, WNT/β-catenin-driven APC exclusion, and VEGF-mediated endothelial anergy, further restrict cytotoxic infiltration [ – ]. In the peritoneal cavity, a complex milieu of mesothelial and fibrin meshes, omental fat-associated milky spots, neutrophil extracellular traps, and immunosuppressive ascitic cytokines forms a “trap-and-tolerize” ecosystem. Carcinoma cells commonly retain EpCAM, MUC1, and other adhesion molecules that promote mesothelial attachment [ – ], while FAP-positive cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) and integrins (αvβ3, β5, β6) reinforce the stromal barrier [ ]. Across anatomical compartments, a spectrum of immune checkpoints (PD-1/PD-L1, CTLA-4, LAG-3, TIM-3, TIGIT), myeloid programs (CSF1R, CCR2/CCR5), “don’t-eat-me” signals (CD47-SIRPα), and metabolic suppressors (adenosine, tryptophan-kynurenine) act in parallel to exclude or disable effector cells [ – ].
Importantly, these liver-, lung-, and peritoneum-specific suppressive programs are not simply quantitative extensions of general tumor immune evasion but qualitatively different forms of immunological sequestration [ – ]. Soluble cytokines, antibodies, and systemically delivered agonists are rapidly cleared or neutralized by sinusoidal scavenger pathways, KCs uptake, extensive alveolar macrophage filtering, high baseline. Myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) influx, and peritoneal stromal trapping, leading to a fundamental mismatch between pharmacologic exposure and the anatomical sites that require modulation [ ]. This organ-defined barrier landscape explains why broad immune activation or standard immunotherapies often fail to recondition hepatic or peritoneal metastases, and why spatially resolved, niche-accessible delivery systems are required.
Despite this hostile terrain, mCRC maintains or acquires multiple actionable targets that can be harnessed for precision immunotherapy, contingent on the successful delivery of therapeutic payloads to the correct anatomical compartments. CRC cell antigens, such as CEA/CEACAM5, GPA33 (A33), EpCAM, MUC1, and HER2 in selected subsets, offer entry points for vaccines, bispecifics, or adoptive cell therapy [ – ]. Recurrent oncogenic mutations, including KRAS and BRAF V600E, yield neoepitopes suitable for personalized vaccines or TCR-based strategies [ , ]. Stromal and myeloid programs provide orthogonal targets for vascular and CAF normalization [ ]; CSF1R and CCR2 for TAM and MDSC reprogramming [ ]; CXCR4 blockade to disrupt chemokine exclusion [ ]; CD39/CD73/A2A and IDO1 inhibition to counter metabolic suppression [ ]; FAP and integrins as matrix “address tags” [ ]; and STING or TLR activation to restore functional antigen presentation [ , ]. Many of these targets are spatially compartmentalized, for instance, liver-specific scavenger receptors and lectin-binding motifs on sinusoidal endothelium and macrophages, versus peritoneal fibrin and ECM motifs, making delivery strategies inseparable from target selection.
Nano-immunotherapy provides a platform to make this target compartment landscape actionable [ – ]. Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs), polymeric nanoparticles (PNPs), inorganic materials, and membrane-coated biomimetic systems protect fragile cargos, such as mRNA, peptides, siRNA, cGAMP, CpG, cytokines, and checkpoint modulators [ , ]. These carriers improve pharmacokinetics, and they can also be engineered with antibodies, peptides, chemokine-binding domains, or matrix-binding ligands. Such modifications direct payloads to APCs, tumor beds, lymphoid tissues, or metastatic niches. In preclinical models of mCRC, mRNA-LNP vaccines encoding colorectal antigens or patient-specific neoepitopes enhance cross-presentation and diversify cytotoxic T cell responses [ ]. Polymeric co-delivery systems combining antigens with TLR or STING agonists overcome dendritic cell quiescence and myeloid suppression [ ]. MnO₂ nanomaterials alleviate hypoxia and amplify STING signaling [ ]. CXCR4- or FAP-targeted nanocarriers disrupt chemokine exclusion and stromal resistance [ ]. Early clinical studies underscore the translational potential of mRNA nanovaccines. BNT122 is a personalized mRNA-LNP vaccine that encodes up to 20 patient-specific neoantigens, and is currently being tested with atezolizumab after metastasectomy to consolidate systemic antitumor immunity (NCT04486378) [ ]. mRNA-5671/V941, in contrast, delivers mRNA encoding shared KRAS mutations (G12C, G12D, G12V, G13D) and is being evaluated with pembrolizumab in KRAS-mutant colorectal cancer and other solid tumors (NCT03948763) [ ].
In this review, we focus on mCRC as an immune-refractory malignancy shaped by organ-specific barriers and evolving immunosuppressive circuits. As shown in Fig.  , we first delineate the immunobiology of liver, peritoneal and lung metastases, and the associated therapeutic targets. Then, we summarize four strategic applications of nanomedicine to overcome these barriers, including nanoparticle-based tumor vaccination, delivery of immune checkpoint modulators and gene silencers, reprogramming of the TME, and induction of immunogenic cell death. We conclude by discussing translational challenges, ranging from delivery efficiency to immune escape, and potential solutions including stimulus-responsive materials, combinatorial regimens, and locoregional administration. Collectively, this framework highlights how rationally designed nano-immunotherapeutics may transform the treatment landscape of mCRC.

 

## Current landscape of metastatic colorectal cancer
mCRC represents a biologically and anatomically compartmentalized disease in which liver, peritoneal, and pulmonary metastases develop through distinct dissemination routes and give rise to unique immunosuppressive niches [ – ]. The liver, seeded via the portal circulation, exhibits sinusoidal tolerance and myeloid dominance shaped by TGF-β, VEGF, and chemokine axes. The peritoneal cavity, exposed through serosal breach and transcoelomic spread, forms a fibrin-rich and mesothelial interface where stromal barriers and immunosuppressive cytokines limit immune access. The lung, reached via systemic venous return, presents vascular traps and neutrophil-driven exclusion mechanisms. Across sites, shared suppressive circuits, checkpoint ligands, adenosine signaling, tryptophan metabolism, and stromal fibrosis, converge with site-specific features to form barriers to effector T cell infiltration, antigen presentation, and drug penetration [ – ]. Understanding this spatial immunopathology is essential for the rational design of nano-immunotherapeutic strategies capable of overcoming both immune resistance and delivery failure in mCRC.

## Liver metastasis of colorectal cancer
The liver is the most common site of colorectal cancer metastasis due to direct access through the portal circulation and its intrinsically tolerogenic immune environment. Liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSECs), KCs, and hepatic stellate cells form a specialized niche that favors immune deviation over clearance [ ]. TGF-β, IL-10, and VEGF are highly expressed, and inefficient cross-presentation by sinusoidal endothelium, combined with capillarization and matrix stiffening, progressively limits T cell infiltration and drug penetration [ , ]. LSECs express high levels of PD-L1, Stabilin-1/2, and mannose/scavenger receptors, enabling them to capture antigens but bias processing toward tolerance rather than immunogenic cross-presentation [ , ]. Their secretion of IL-10 and lack of co-stimulation promote CD8⁺ T cell deletion or exhaustion rather than effector differentiation. KCs, as the dominant intravascular phagocytes, rapidly clear nanoparticles, cytokines, and opsonized antibodies through MARCO, CLEC4F, and complement receptors, limiting exposure of metastases to systemic immunotherapies. KCs also release prostaglandins, IL-10, and TGF-β, skewing monocyte differentiation toward suppressive macrophages and restraining dendritic cell activation. MDSCs accumulate early in hepatic metastases and suppress cytotoxic immunity through arginase-1, iNOS, ROS production, and cystine sequestration, which deprives T cells of essential metabolites. They also express PD-L1 and generate peroxynitrite, nitrating the TCR complex and further impairing antigen recognition.
This suppressive microenvironment is consolidated by interconnected chemokine, metabolic, and signaling programs. CXCL12-CXCR4 and CCL2-CCR2 axes recruit and retain suppressive myeloid cells [ ]. The adenosine pathway, through CD39, CD73, and A2A signaling, inhibits TCR activation and NK cell function [ ]. IDO1 depletes tryptophan and produces kynurenine, which acts via AhR to promote T cell exhaustion [ ]. WNT-β-catenin signaling excludes conventional dendritic cells and impairs priming [ ]. VEGF and ANGPT2 sustain endothelial anergy, and constant exposure to microbial products via the portal vein further dampens innate immune activation [ ]. Despite this suppressive landscape, several therapeutic opportunities emerge [ , – ]. Vascular and stromal normalization through VEGF or TGF-β inhibition can improve perfusion, reduce hypoxia, and facilitate lymphocyte entry. Myeloid remodeling via CSF1R, CCR2, or CCR5 blockade restores antigen presentation and reduces suppressive macrophage populations. CXCR4 antagonists enhance T cell trafficking, and inhibition of CD39, CD73, A2A, or IDO1 can relieve metabolic constraints. Tumor antigens remain expressed in liver metastases and can support vaccine or cellular strategies. Intrahepatic T cells often co-express LAG-3, TIM-3, and TIGIT, which become targetable once trafficking and priming are restored.
Challenges to therapy are equally distinct. Nanoparticles and proteins are rapidly cleared by sinusoidal scavenger and lectin receptors, forming protein coronas that divert payloads to the reticuloendothelial system [ , ]. First-pass hepatic uptake increases off-target deposition, and fibrotic narrowing of sinusoids limits penetration of carriers larger than ~ 100 nm. Complement activation and neutrophil extracellular traps further impede delivery, while systemic administration of innate agonists risks hepatotoxicity and anti-carrier immunity with repeated dosing.
Effective strategies must therefore integrate vascular normalization, myeloid reprogramming, and metabolic relief before introducing antigen-targeted and checkpoint-based immunotherapy. Delivery systems should be optimized for reduced Kupffer cell clearance and adapted to the fenestration size of remodeled sinusoids, using targeting ligands such as GalNAc or apolipoprotein motifs [ ]. Locoregional approaches, including hepatic artery infusion, perilesional depots, or hydrogels, can provide sustained immune activation while minimizing systemic exposure [ ]. Together, these principles support liver-specific, mechanism-driven design for nano-immunotherapy in mCRC.

## Peritoneal metastasis of colorectal cancer
Colorectal cancer cells disseminate into the peritoneal cavity through serosal breaches, where they survive in ascitic fluid and adhere to the mesothelium before invading the submesothelial layer [ , ]. This cavity is a dynamic immune and stromal environment. Mesothelial injury and tumor-derived cues initiate a wound healing response characterized by fibrin deposition, matrix exposure, and upregulation of adhesion molecules that capture exfoliated tumor clusters. Mesothelial cells upregulate ICAM-1, VCAM-1, P-selectin, and mesothelin under inflammatory cytokines, enhancing attachment of EpCAM⁺ or MUC1⁺ tumor cells and promoting transmesothelial migration [ ]. Omental immune niches, or milky spots, contribute myeloid cells that often support rather than clear implants. These milky spots enrich CD163⁺ macrophages, CCR2⁺ monocytes, and granulocytic MDSCs, which secrete IL-10, TGF-β, and arginase-1 to block dendritic maturation, inhibit T cell recruitment, and expand regulatory phenotypes. Ascites is enriched in VEGF, TGF-β, and IL-6, which suppress dendritic maturation, promote angiogenesis, and activate fibroblasts.
The evolving tumor stroma is dense and fibrotic, dominated by FAP-positive fibroblasts that stiffen the matrix and restrict T cell infiltration. Fibroblasts in the peritoneum also express CXCL12, which creates exclusion zones that prevent T cells from entering tumor nests [ ]. MDSCs and M2-like macrophages accumulate under the influence of CXCL12-CXCR4 and CCL2-CCR2 chemokine gradients. MDSCs deplete arginine via arginase-1, generate ROS and peroxynitrite, and nitrify TCR complexes, rendering CD8⁺ T cells refractory to antigen. M2-like macrophages produce IL-10, TGF-β, and PD-L1, directly suppressing cytotoxicity and inhibiting NK cell activation. T cells are sparse and often exhausted, marked by checkpoint expression and impaired cytotoxicity. Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) facilitate cell capture and reimplantation [ , ]. NETs expose DNA-histone scaffolds decorated with integrins and proteases that trap circulating tumor cells, increase mesothelial adhesiveness, and generate local oxidative stress that impairs T cell survival. Tumor cells express EpCAM, MUC1, mesothelin, and claudins, which promote mesothelial adhesion, while integrins such as αvβ3, αvβ5, and αvβ6 enhance invasion.
Immune suppression is reinforced by metabolic and signaling barriers [ , ]. CD39 and CD73 drive adenosine accumulation, impairing TCR signaling and NK function. IDO1-mediated tryptophan depletion leads to kynurenine production and T cell exhaustion via AhR. TGF-β from multiple sources sustains fibrosis and suppresses antigen presentation. These signals converge to form an adhesive, immune-silent microenvironment permissive to tumor expansion.
Therapeutic delivery in the peritoneal cavity faces distinct obstacles [ – ]. Poor vascularization limits systemic drug exposure, while intraperitoneal drugs are rapidly diluted or cleared. Dense collagen and fibrin impede diffusion, and particles may be sequestered by mesothelial or macrophage uptake. Repeated dosing risks serositis or ileus. These barriers favor local strategies. Nanocarriers for intraperitoneal use should balance penetration and retention, incorporate matrix- or mesothelium-binding motifs, and respond to proteases or acidity in the tumor bed. Hydrogels or in situ depots can prolong antigen and adjuvant exposure. Coordinated delivery of antigen, innate agonists, and checkpoint or metabolic inhibitors may help overcome the multilayered immunosuppression of peritoneal metastases.

## Lung metastasis of colorectal cancer
Colorectal cancer cells metastasize to the lung through capillary arrest, where they encounter a unique immunological and biophysical interface shaped by endothelial adhesion, alveolar immune tolerance, and surfactant biology [ ]. Pulmonary endothelial cells express selectins and adhesion molecules in response to systemic inflammation, mediating tumor tethering and extravasation. High endothelial expression of E-selectin, VCAM-1, and ICAM-1 under TNF-α and IL-1β exposure facilitates rolling and firm adhesion of circulating tumor cells (CTCs) [ ]. Alveolar macrophages (AMs), tuned for tolerance to inhaled antigens, secrete TGF-β and PGE₂ to dampen inflammation, while neutrophils deploy extracellular traps that entangle tumor cells and promote seeding [ ]. AMs constitutively express PD-L1, CD200R, and MARCO, enabling them to silence dendritic cell priming and suppress T cell activation. AM-derived PGE₂ signals through EP2/EP4 to impair NK cytotoxicity and inhibit antigen cross-presentation. Platelets cloak circulating tumor cells, shielding them from NK surveillance and presenting adhesive ligands [ ]. Surfactant components alter nanoparticle corona formation and inhibit dendritic maturation, adding further complexity to immune activation. Surfactant proteins A/D bind to microbial- and tumor-associated ligands and modulate local immune thresholds, limiting dendritic activation and promoting tolerogenic macrophage phenotypes.
The pulmonary metastatic niche is further shaped by chemokine and metabolic circuits. CXCL1 and CXCL2 attract neutrophils via CXCR2, while CXCL12-CXCR4 signaling retains tumor cells and recruits suppressive myeloid cells [ – ]. CXCR2⁺ neutrophils generate ROS and NETs containing MPO, NE, and histones that damage epithelial barriers and entrap tumor cells, facilitating micrometastatic outgrowth. COX-2-driven PGE₂ production skews macrophages toward M2 polarization and suppresses cytotoxic lymphocytes. M2-like TAMs express arginase-1, iNOS, and PD-L1; they deplete L-arginine, nitrify TCR complexes, and inhibit IL-12-dependent NK activation. Immunosuppressive pathways in the metastatic niche are multifaceted, involving growth factor signaling that drives epithelial-mesenchymal transition and immune exclusion, metabolic checkpoints that suppress TCR activity, and catabolic enzymes that enforce T-cell exhaustion. Yet despite these barriers, tumor-associated antigens and cell-surface markers remain stably expressed, and recurrent oncogenic mutations generate neoepitopes that can be harnessed for vaccine- or TCR-based immunotherapy.
Within the metastatic microenvironment, multiple layers of immunosuppression converge to blunt effective T cell responses. Immunoregulatory cytokines such as TGF-β directly restrain cytotoxic programs of CD8⁺ T cells and promote regulatory T cell differentiation, while metabolic reprogramming mechanisms deplete essential amino acids and generate suppressive metabolites that reinforce T cell exhaustion [ ]. In parallel, stromal components [ ], including myeloid-derived suppressor cells and tumor-associated macrophages, secrete high levels of suppressive mediators and foster an immune-privileged niche. Hypoxia and altered extracellular adenosine further reinforce this dysfunctional state by attenuating TCR signaling [ ]. Despite these obstacles, tumor-associated antigens and mutation-derived neoantigens in colorectal cancer remain exploitable targets, offering opportunities for vaccine design and T cell-based immunotherapy,
Delivery challenges in the lung are distinct from other metastatic sites [ , ]. High pulmonary flow and shear reduce particle residence time, while nonspecific trapping and opsonization by alveolar phagocytes limit targeting efficiency. Surfactant adsorption alters nanoparticle fate and may inactivate immune adjuvants. Inhaled formulations enhance local deposition but face mucociliary clearance and biophysical barriers; particle size and elasticity critically influence distribution and uptake. Systemic innate agonists risk pneumonitis, and repeated exposure may induce anti-carrier immunity.
These barriers provide design principles for lung-directed nano-immunotherapy. Nanocarriers should be small, neutral or zwitterionic, and protein-repellent to avoid premature clearance [ ]. Leukocyte- or platelet-mimicking coatings and ligands for inflamed endothelium may enhance targeting while reducing alveolar macrophage uptake [ ]. For coordinated immunotherapy, co-delivery of antigens, innate agonists, and metabolic or checkpoint inhibitors can integrate priming with exhaustion reversal. Locoregional options such as aerosolizable particles or surfactant-stable hydrogels that release cargo in response to protease or pH cues may sustain exposure without triggering toxicity [ , ]. Gradual dose escalation and biomarker-guided strategies, targeting CXCR2, adenosine, or checkpoint profiles, are essential to balance efficacy with pulmonary safety.

## Other metastasis
In addition to the liver, peritoneum, and lung, mCRC can spread to bone, brain, ovary, lymph nodes, adrenal glands, and other organs, each posing distinct immunologic and physical challenges [ , , ]. Bone metastases reside in marrow niches dominated by myeloid cells and osteolytic remodeling, where RANKL and CXCL12 drive homing and tolerance is reinforced by TGF-β and CSF1R signaling. Brain metastases encounter restrictive vascular barriers and profound immunosuppression shaped by microglia, adenosine accumulation, and poor dendritic activation, with the CD73-A2A and IDO1 pathways playing central roles. Ovarian metastases emerge within the peritoneal continuum and share many features with peritoneal spread but exhibit particularly dense stroma, VEGF-rich ascites, and strong mesothelial and integrin signaling. Metastatic lymph nodes reflect local tolerization through regulatory T cells and dendritic cell conditioning, creating barriers to effective antigen presentation and trafficking. Less frequent sites such as the adrenal gland are marked by high vascularity and low immune surveillance, limiting both therapeutic delivery and response durability.
Across metastatic sites, colorectal cancer encounters a series of immunologic and biophysical filters that limit the effectiveness of systemic therapies. As outlined above and summarized in Table  , these include tolerogenic organ environments, such as the liver, physical encapsulation and fibrin scaffolds in the peritoneal cavity, chemokine exclusion and metabolic suppression in the lung, and niche-specific barriers in bone, brain, and lymph nodes. Myeloid-dominant immunosuppression, impaired antigen presentation, stromal resistance, and vascular access limitations converge to blunt T cell recruitment, activation, and effector function. These constraints help explain why current immunotherapies yield only modest and short-lived benefits in most mCRC patients, even when intensified or combined.
Instead of further escalating immune activation, effective intervention may require strategies that are spatially matched to the metastatic niche. By tailoring antigen choice, immune modulators, and delivery design to site-specific barriers, nanomedicine can enable precise reprogramming of local immunity [ , ]. This includes co-delivery of tumor antigens with STING or toll-like receptor agonists to initiate innate activation, incorporation of checkpoint or metabolic inhibitors to reverse T cell exhaustion, and use of macrophage or vessel-modulating agents to reshape the stromal context. Nanoparticles can be optimized for biodistribution and retention using hepatotropic ligands, mesothelium-binding surfaces, or selectin-targeted coatings, while locoregional routes such as hepatic artery infusion or intraperitoneal depots offer further spatial control when systemic exposure is insufficient.

 

## Nanotechnology improves mCRC precise delivery
Immunotherapy for mCRC faces fundamental challenges of effective delivery across diverse anatomical sites. Nanotechnology offers a versatile solution by enhancing the stability, circulation time, and localization of immune payloads [ – ]. Advanced nanocarriers protect nucleic acids and proteins from enzymatic degradation, improve pharmacokinetics, and facilitate endosomal escape [ , ], thereby increasing their accumulation in metastatic lesions. By incorporating stimuli-responsive components, such as pH-, redox-, enzyme-, or hypoxia-sensitive elements, these systems can further achieve controlled release within the tumor microenvironment [ , ]. Functionalization with targeting ligands allows preferential recognition of receptors enriched in metastatic niches, while integration with imaging modalities supports real-time monitoring of distribution and immune responses.
A variety of nanocarrier systems have been developed to meet the distinct delivery requirements of mCRC (Fig. ). LNPs exhibit strong hepatic tropism and are widely used for the delivery of nucleic acids to liver metastases, enabling local gene modulation and vaccination [ ]. Biodegradable polymeric carriers, including PLGA-based particles and micelles, support co-delivery of antigens and innate immune agonists, helping to reverse myeloid suppression and stimulate T-cell priming [ ]. Inorganic materials such as superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles, manganese-based systems, and gold-based scaffolds can remodel hypoxia and induce immunogenic cell death while offering imaging capabilities [ , ]. Biomimetic vesicles, including membrane-coated or leukocyte-derived carriers, enhancing accumulation in metastatic tissue through reduced clearance and improved homing [ ]. For lesions with poor vascular access, such as those in the peritoneum or at resection margins, injectable hydrogels provide sustained local exposure [ ]. Across these platforms, ligand engineering, using molecules, such as apolipoproteins, GalNAc, RGD peptides, or fibroblast-activation protein (FAP) binders, further improves specificity and tissue penetration [ ]. In the following sections, we provide a detailed overview of the major nanocarrier platforms and their immunological applications in mCRC. Each is discussed in terms of delivery efficiency, immunomodulatory potential, compatibility with systemic or local administration, and translational readiness.

 

## Lipid nanoparticles
LNPs are the leading non-viral carriers for nucleic acid delivery and have shown strong potential for metastatic colorectal cancer due to their ability to protect cargo from degradation, promote cytosolic release, and achieve reliable exposure in key metastatic organs such as the liver, lung, or bone. Canonical LNPs comprise ionizable lipids, helper phospholipids, cholesterol, and PEG-lipids [ ]. The ionizable lipid facilitates RNA encapsulation and promotes endosomal escape upon acidification. Cholesterol and phospholipids stabilize the structure, while PEG-lipids extend circulation and reduce opsonization. These components can be tuned to adjust size, pKa, PEG density, and tissue tropism, and further functionalized with ligands for organ- or cell-specific delivery [ ].
In mCRC, LNPs are particularly useful in two contexts, mRNA vaccination and local immune modulation. Liver metastasis is the most common pattern, and intravenously administered LNPs reliably reach hepatic sinusoids. Incorporating ApoE-recruiting motifs or GalNAc groups further enhances uptake at metastatic borders [ , ]. In peritoneal disease, intraperitoneal injection of mildly PEGylated LNPs achieves broad serosal distribution, supporting cavity-based delivery [ ]. Preclinical studies demonstrate that mRNA-LNP vaccines encoding CEA or neoantigens expand intratumoral CD8⁺ T cells and enhance checkpoint response in liver metastasis models [ ]. LNPs carrying siRNA targeting PD-L1 or CXCR4 improve T-cell infiltration and delay tumor growth, while intraperitoneal mRNA-LNPs increase dendritic maturation and CD8⁺ infiltration in serosal nodules [ – ].
Clinically, LNPs are under active investigation in mCRC. Personalized mRNA-LNP vaccines are being tested post-metastasectomy in combination with PD-1 blockade [ ]. A KRAS-mutant mRNA-LNP vaccine is also undergoing early trials [ ]. These efforts benefit from mature manufacturing platforms established for approved LNP therapeutics. However, issues such as infusion reactions, anti-PEG immunity, and batch-to-batch consistency remain important considerations. Despite these challenges, LNPs offer a versatile and scalable platform for both systemic and locoregional immunotherapy in mCRC, enabling precise immune activation and gene regulation across metastatic sites.

## Polymer-based nanoparticles
Polymer-based nanoparticles offer a modular and biodegradable platform for precise delivery in mCRC. Systems such as PLGA particles, PEG-PLGA micelles, polypeptide micelles, dendrimers, and block-copolymer vesicles can encapsulate peptides, nucleic acids, and small molecules with high efficiency, protecting them from enzymatic degradation and enabling stimuli-responsive release [ – ]. Sizes can be tuned (typically 50–150 nm) to optimize lymphatic transport and tissue penetration, and surfaces accommodate ligands, including antibodies, chemokine mimetics, and glycan motifs, for improved targeting. Imaging tracers like near-infrared dyes, PET chelators, and MR-active metals can be incorporated to monitor biodistribution and lesion coverage [ ].
In mCRC, polymeric carriers can be tailored to overcome site-specific barriers and immunologic failure modes [ , ]. Liver metastases with sinusoidal remodeling, hypoxia, and myeloid dominance benefit from co-delivery strategies that combine antigen, innate agonists, and local suppressive modulators (e.g., COX2 or IDO inhibitors). In peritoneal lesions with limited perfusion, slightly mucoadhesive micelles improve retention and nodular spread following intraperitoneal dosing. For pulmonary implants, stealth micelles with neutral or zwitterionic coronas help resist opsonization and reduce clearance. These design features enable synchronized initiation of innate sensing, antigen presentation, and T-cell reinvigoration within constrained metastatic sites.
Polymeric nanoparticles have shown promise in reshaping the immune landscape of colorectal cancer metastases. In preclinical liver models, PLGA-based carriers co-loaded with CEA peptides and CpG increased intratumoral CD8⁺ infiltration and reduced suppressive cell populations, with Cy7 imaging confirming preferential accumulation within lesions [ , ]. PEG-PLGA micelles micelles delivering PD-L1 siRNA together with an IDO inhibitor achieved dual checkpoint and metabolic modulation in hepatic models, while intravital microscopy visualized their ability to traverse sinusoidal barriers. In the peritoneal setting, AMD3100-decorated particles distributed uniformly after intraperitoneal dosing, and rim-to-core fluorescence correlated with enhanced cytotoxic activity. Theranostic versions bearing 64Cu or 89Zr chelators enabled positron emission tomography (PET) quantification of lesion exposure and informed dose and schedule without serial biopsies. Clinically, polymer platforms benefit from regulatory familiarity, scalable fabrication, and adaptable linker chemistries to match immune activation kinetics [ ]. Nonetheless, important hurdles remain. Precise control over particle size, the risk of anti-PEG immunity, and variable vascular permeability across human metastases all complicate translation [ ]. Addressing these challenges will require integration of imaging-guided verification and ligand tuning to ensure delivery fidelity. Taken together, polymeric nanoparticles represent adaptable and traceable systems for nano-immunotherapy, with potential to be deployed across the metastatic spectrum of colorectal cancer.

## Inorganic nanoparticles
Inorganic nanomaterials offer two key advantages for nano-immunotherapy in mCRC. They provide built-in imaging capabilities and can modulate the tumor microenvironment while delivering immunologically active payloads [ , ]. Among these, superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPIO) are the most clinically validated. SPIO-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has been used to improve the detection and characterization of colorectal liver metastases, as shown in comparative and prospective clinical studies [ ]. These results support their relevance for mCRC given the liver-dominant metastatic pattern.
Beyond diagnosis, SPIO nanoparticles have shown therapeutic potential in preclinical mCRC models. When functionalized with toll-like receptor (TLR) 9 agonists, SPIO particles selectively accumulate in hepatic metastases, promote M1-like macrophage polarization, and enable noninvasive tracking of biodistribution through T2-weighted MRI [ ]. These multifunctional properties allow simultaneous monitoring of lesion exposure and modulation of immunosuppressive myeloid niches. Manganese-based nanostructures, such as MnO₂ nanoparticles, further address the hypoxic and acidic environment of colorectal liver or lung metastases [ , ]. These particles release oxygen by reacting with local hydrogen peroxide, alleviating hypoxia while simultaneously producing Mn²⁺ ions for T1-weighted MRI signal enhancement. When combined with the STING agonist cGAMP, they enhance innate immune activation through cGAS-STING signaling, boosting dendritic cell activation and type I interferon production in immunologically cold liver lesions. Theranostic gold nanorods coated with gadolinium or loaded with radionuclides have been used to guide catheter-directed therapy for colorectal liver metastases [ ]. These systems support MRI or photoacoustic imaging to verify localization and enable photothermal ablation of lesions. When combined with immune adjuvants, photothermal injury increases the release of tumor-associated antigens and promotes cross-priming of cytotoxic T cells. For peritoneal metastases, mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs) show strong retention within collagen-rich extracellular matrices and achieve broad coverage of serosal nodules following intraperitoneal administration [ ].
Taken together, inorganic nanoparticles provide a rational scaffold for immune intervention in metastatic niches. Their ability to combine image-guided delivery with microenvironment remodeling is particularly advantageous for liver and peritoneal metastases. Moving toward clinical translation will require minimizing off-target toxicity and complement activation, while leveraging real-time MRI or PET feedback to refine dosing and ensure pharmacodynamic precision.

## Biomimetic and cell membrane-coated nanoparticles
Biomimetic carriers enhance nanoparticle delivery to mCRC lesions by cloaking synthetic cores with natural membranes or membrane-mimetic shells [ – ]. These coatings inherit homing and adhesion properties from source cells while shielding immune payloads from opsonization and clearance. For mCRC, this strategy directly addresses two key delivery bottlenecks of rapid hepatic clearance by the reticuloendothelial system and poor localization to metastatic interfaces in the liver, peritoneum, and lung. By presenting endogenous ligands such as selectin-binding proteins, integrins, chemokine receptors, and the self-marker CD47, membrane-coated particles achieve prolonged circulation, accumulate at inflamed endothelium, and engage matrix components that promote lesion anchoring and immune engagement [ – ].
Platelet membrane coatings are particularly advantageous for targeting liver and lung metastases. Platelets express P-selectin, GPIb/IX/V, and integrins that bind activated endothelium and exposed collagen. Nanoparticles coated with platelet membranes and loaded with immune agonists such as STING or TLR ligands can roll, adhere, and transmigrate across sinusoidal or pulmonary microvessels, releasing their cargo into the perivascular immune niche [ , ]. Intravital imaging confirms that platelet-coated particles follow the full adhesion cascade and correlate with increased type I interferon and NF-κB activation at metastatic sites. Radiolabeled or near-infrared–tagged platelet-coated nanoparticles further demonstrate selective localization to metastatic lesions with limited background signal in uninvolved organs.
Cancer-cell membrane coatings leverage homotypic binding to metastatic colorectal nodules [ , , ]. Membranes derived from CEA- or EpCAM-positive colorectal cancer cells display tumor-associated antigens and adhesion molecules that facilitate binding to hepatic or peritoneal implants. When used to coat cores encapsulating tumor antigens and immune adjuvants such as CpG or cGAMP, these systems combine physical targeting with enhanced cross-presentation. In peritoneal metastasis models, CRC-membrane-coated nanovaccines achieve sustained cavity retention [ ], strong coverage of serosal lesions, and accumulation in antigen-presenting cells, leading to robust CD8⁺ T-cell activation.
Leukocyte-derived membranes extend targeting to additional metastatic environments [ – ]. Neutrophil coatings exploit L-selectin and CXCRs to navigate inflamed pulmonary vasculature and extracellular trap rich regions, both of which are relevant to lung metastasis. Monocyte or macrophage membranes enhance accumulation in myeloid-rich perivascular zones of hepatic metastases by displaying scavenger and chemokine receptors. These biomimetic layers suppress complement activation and reduce nonspecific uptake, allowing lower doses to achieve therapeutic exposure. Red blood cell membranes, while lacking active targeting, carry CD47 and a neutral glycocalyx that prolong systemic circulation. When combined with integrin-targeting ligands such as cyclic RGD, they achieve meaningful peritoneal and pulmonary localization with minimal KCs uptake.
The translational appeal of membrane-coated nanoparticles lies in their ability to improve metastatic lesion docking without aggressive surface chemistry. They can be fabricated using clinically familiar cores such as PLGA or lipid nanoparticles [ , ]. However, challenges remain. The source material must be screened for safety and standardized for protein content and ligand presentation [ ]. Recombinant or synthetic mimetics of critical ligands may help overcome variability. Repeated dosing risks immunogenicity, especially if donor-specific antigens or PEG moieties are present [ , ]. Techniques such as gentle membrane processing and the addition of defined lipid supplements can reduce these risks. Real-time imaging with near-infrared dyes, radiometals, or magnetic cores should be incorporated to track localization and link exposure to pharmacodynamic outcomes, such as dendritic cell activation and T-cell expansion. With appropriate controls in place, biomimetic nanoparticles offer a robust platform for targeting and activating immune responses in mCRC metastases across hepatic, peritoneal, and pulmonary compartments.

## Hydrogel depots and locoregional formulations
Hydrogel depots provide a localized approach to enhance immune stimulation at mCRC sites, particularly in the liver and peritoneum where systemic delivery often fails [ – ]. These injectable systems form gels in situ and gradually release nanocarriers loaded with immunomodulators, such as STING or TLR agonists, at the tumor-host interface, boosting antigen presentation and innate immune activation while minimizing systemic toxicity.
In hepatic metastasis models, post-ablation application of hydrogels carrying cGAMP or tumor antigens has maintained sustained exposure at surgical margins, enhancing dendritic cell activation in draining lymph nodes and promoting cytotoxic T-cell infiltration in adjacent liver tissue [ , ]. For peritoneal disease, sprayable or conformal gels extend retention of nanovaccines on serosal surfaces, enabling persistent immune stimulation within dispersed nodules [ – ]. Hyaluronic acid, chitosan, and PEG-based matrices with pH- or enzyme-responsive linkers align drug release with the acidic and protease-rich microenvironment of mCRC metastases.
Embedding nanoparticles within the gel matrix adds spatial and temporal control, enabling sequential immune activation across multiple cycles. Optical or PET imaging tracers can be co-loaded to verify localization and guide dosing [ , ]. Catheter-based administration into peritumoral hepatic regions also allows for minimally invasive deployment in interventional radiology settings.
Clinically, these materials benefit from regulatory familiarity, surgical compatibility, and tunable mechanical and degradation profiles. When designed with appropriate rheology, payload release, and imaging features, hydrogel-based locoregional systems transform metastatic niches into immunologically responsive zones, offering a practical complement to systemic nano-immunotherapy in mCRC.
A range of nanotechnology platforms have been tailored to address the anatomical and immunological barriers of mCRC metastases. Table  summarizes representative systems—spanning lipid, polymeric, inorganic, and biomimetic carriers with their core features, niche preferences (liver/peritoneum/lung), typical immune cargos, and supporting data. Each platform offers distinct advantages: from mRNA or siRNA delivery and local immunomodulation, to MRI-visible tracking or targeted depot formation. Collectively, these nanoplatforms demonstrate the versatility and translational potential of nano-immunotherapy in the mCRC setting.

 

## Nano-immunotherapy strategies for mCRC
In metastatic colorectal cancer, immune resistance is not solely due to a lack of immune stimulation but is primarily driven by organ-specific barriers that impair antigen presentation, hinder T-cell trafficking, and induce immune dysfunction. The liver, peritoneum, and lung each present distinct microenvironmental challenges that limit the efficacy of systemic immunotherapies. Nano-immunotherapy aims to overcome these barriers by integrating multiple functions into a single platform [ – ]. These include enhancing local antigen exposure, modulating stromal and chemokine pathways to facilitate immune cell infiltration, reversing myeloid and metabolic suppression, and prolonging immune activation within target niches. Building upon the delivery strategies outlined in Sect. , this section categorizes the major mechanistic strategies employed in nano-immunotherapy for mCRC and highlights representative preclinical advances that illustrate their therapeutic potential.

## Nanoparticle-based tumor vaccines for antigen presentation
In mCRC, impaired antigen priming remains a fundamental barrier, especially in microsatellite-stable tumors. Nanoparticle-based vaccines are engineered to address this limitation by stabilizing tumor antigens, facilitating their delivery to dendritic cells, and co-delivering immunostimulatory signals that promote efficient cross-presentation [ , ]. Compared with soluble formulations, nanoparticles enhance lymph node trafficking, increase uptake by professional antigen-presenting cells, and improve endosomal escape. These properties are especially valuable in mCRC, where systemic immune therapies often fail due to weak T cell initiation rather than a dominant checkpoint.
Tailoring the physicochemical properties of nanocarriers enables precise adaptation to metastatic niches. For instance, ligand modification can enhance hepatic accumulation or favor retention within peritoneal implants, while imaging reporters allow in vivo verification of vaccine biodistribution. A unifying design principle of effective nanovaccines is the co-delivery of antigen and adjuvant within the same particle, ensuring that both innate and adaptive cues converge on the same dendritic cell. This spatial coordination promotes efficient cross-presentation via MHC-I and supports type I interferon signaling. Recent studies highlight nucleic acid vaccination using lipid or hybrid nanoparticles to deliver mRNA encoding tumor antigens, ranging from shared targets such as CEA or GPA33 to personalized neoantigens [ , , ]. In particular, co-administration of MHC-I- and MHC-II-restricted neoantigen mRNAs has been shown to amplify antigen-specific CD8⁺ T cell responses, reduce recurrence after surgery, and synergize with checkpoint blockade in colorectal cancer models. Consistent with these principles, experimental evidence demonstrates that MHC-I and MHC-II mRNA vaccines each induce distinct immune responses [ ]. As shown in Fig. A, MHC-I antigens elicited modest IFN-γ secretion, whereas several MHC-II antigens triggered stronger responses. Flow cytometry (Fig. B) confirmed that MHC-I vaccines mainly activated CD8⁺ T cells, while MHC-II vaccines induced CD4⁺ T cell cytokine production. Importantly, co-administration of both vaccines significantly improved tumor control (Fig. C, D), with complete regression observed in some mice. Functional assays further revealed that combined vaccination markedly enhanced IFN-γ and TNF-α secretion, especially dual-positive CD8⁺ T cells (Fig. E), underscoring the critical role of CD4⁺ T cell help in sustaining potent CD8⁺ T cell immunity.
Beyond anatomical targeting, nanovaccine performance is strongly dictated by particle size, surface charge, and antigen conjugation chemistry, each of which modulates dendritic cell uptake and subsequent MHC presentation [ , ]. Nanoparticles in the 20–50 nm range efficiently drain to lymph nodes, whereas 50–200 nm particles are preferentially internalized by tissue-resident dendritic cells through macropinocytosis and receptor-mediated endocytosis. Surface charge also shapes uptake, such as mildly anionic or near-neutral particles facilitate lymphatic transport, while cationic particles enhance electrostatic interaction with dendritic cell membranes but may promote lysosomal retention. Antigen conjugation chemistry exerts an additional layer of control. Covalent, protease-labile linkers allow controlled antigen release in endosomes, increasing the probability of loading onto MHC-II, while pH-sensitive or reducible linkers favor cytosolic escape and cross-presentation through the MHC-I pathway. Dense antigen display through multivalent or repetitive patterns can further enhance B cell receptor engagement and augment CD4⁺ T cell help. Together, these physicochemical parameters determine not only how efficiently dendritic cells internalize nanovaccines but also which antigen-processing pathways are preferentially engaged.
Cell membrane-derived nanovesicles (CMNVs) have emerged as versatile platforms in mCRC immunotherapy, owing to their inherent biocompatibility, ability to present tumor or immune cell-derived membrane proteins, and capacity for homotypic targeting [ ]. By mimicking natural cell-cell interactions, CMNVs can facilitate efficient antigen delivery, enhance immune activation, and improve tumor accumulation, making them promising carriers for vaccine design. Building on this concept, bioengineered outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) derived from ClearColi™ have been explored as potent adjuvants in colon cancer vaccination [ ]. In a preventive CT26 mouse model, recombinant OMVs (rOMVs) displaying a CT26 polytope containing multiple neoepitopes markedly enhanced vaccine immunogenicity compared to alum. rOMV-polytope combinations elicited stronger Th1-biased IgG2a responses, elevated IFN-γ, TNF-α, and granzyme B production, and conferred superior protection by reducing tumor burden. These findings highlight the potential of rOMVs to amplify multi-epitope cancer vaccines and provide a versatile platform for personalized immunotherapy in tumors with high mutational heterogeneity.
Cancer cell membrane nanodiscs (CCNDs) also represent an innovative nanovaccine platform that integrates the natural multi-antigenic repertoire of tumor membranes with the immune-potentiating effect of adjuvants. Guo et al. developed CCNDs incorporated with the TLR4 agonist monophosphoryl lipid A (MPLA) as a nanovaccine platform. these nanodiscs achieve efficient lymph node drainage, dendritic cell uptake, and synchronous delivery of antigen and adjuvant [ ]. CCND/MPLA vaccination promotes robust dendritic cell maturation, enhances CD8⁺ T cell responses, and synergizes with PD-L1 blockade to suppress tumor growth. Importantly, beyond primary tumor control, CCNDs also reduce metastatic burden, underscoring their potential in preventing mCRC progression. Another strategy employs engineered probiotics that secrete antigen-loaded nanovesicles as an oral nanovaccine (Fig. F) [ ]. This approach bypasses invasive injection routes and directly engages the gut-associated lymphoid tissue, thereby eliciting both mucosal IgA responses and systemic T cell immunity. In colorectal cancer models, probiotic-secreted nanovesicles carrying tumor antigens not only inhibited primary tumor growth but also suppressed lymph node metastasis, while generating durable immune memory that prevented recurrence. The ability of genetically modified probiotics to continuously release nanovesicles in situ provides a safe, sustainable, and patient-friendly vaccination modality, highlighting the potential of oral nanovaccines as an innovative avenue for mCRC immunotherapy.
Another strategy employs nanocarriers for the co-delivery of defined tumor peptides or proteins together with potent adjuvants, such as CpG, Poly(I: C), or STING agonists [ – ]. This combination ensures that antigens are processed in a highly inflammatory context rather than a tolerogenic one, which is particularly important for immune-privileged sites like the liver. In preclinical models, intraperitoneal administration of such formulations has been shown to transform the peritoneal cavity into an immunization field, where metastatic nodules are simultaneously exposed to both antigen and innate immune stimuli, thereby enhancing local antigen presentation, dendritic cell maturation, and effector T cell infiltration. Moreover, integrating additional payloads, such as IDO inhibitors to prevent T cell exhaustion or chemokine antagonists to redirect suppressive myeloid cells [ ], further supports the persistence, trafficking, and functional activity of tumor-reactive T cells.
Overall, nanovaccines constitute a versatile and modular platform for addressing the priming deficiencies that limit mCRC immunotherapy. By synchronizing the delivery of antigens and immune adjuvants, and by adapting to different routes of administration, including intravenous, intraperitoneal, or locoregional injection, they provide multiple opportunities to reprogram distinct metastatic niches. Their capacity to integrate immunomodulators alongside antigens allows fine-tuning of both the magnitude and quality of T cell responses, underscoring the potential of nanovaccines to transform immune-cold mCRC into an immunologically responsive disease.

 

## Nanoparticle-mediated checkpoint modulation and gene Silencing
In mCRC, immune checkpoint therapies have yielded limited clinical success not simply due to the lack of tumor antigens, but because immunosuppression is structurally and transcriptionally embedded in the metastatic niches. Liver and peritoneal environments impose constitutive inhibitory cues on tumor and myeloid cells, while their physical architecture restricts antibody penetration. Nanoparticles reshape this context by selectively enriching checkpoint blockade and immunomodulatory molecules within metastatic lesions, where they reprogram the tumor microenvironment and potentiate the effects of T cell-based therapies [ ]. When applied following or alongside antigen priming strategies, these nanotherapies enhance local T cell activation and reduce systemic toxicity.
Nanocarriers used for checkpoint modulation in mCRC deliver three principal classes of agents, including antibodies or receptor-mimetic decoys, RNAi species (siRNA, shRNA, and miRNA), and small-molecule inhibitors, and they target distinct but complementary checkpoint pathways [ ]. Antibody- or decoy-based formulations predominantly block PD-1/PD-L1 and LAG-3/FGL-1 interactions, with emerging systems extending to CTLA-4 and TIGIT to broaden effector reinvigoration. RNAi-loaded nanoparticles silence PD-L1, PD-1, or upstream checkpoint regulators such as STAT3 and NF-κB, thereby concurrently suppressing multiple inhibitory ligands including CTLA-4, LAG-3, TIGIT, and TIM-3. Small-molecule payloads, such as A2A receptor or IDO1 inhibitors, relieve metabolic checkpoints that indirectly constrain TCR signaling. These categories differ in mechanism, encompassing receptor blockade, ligand depletion, and pathway-level reprogramming, and together provide a modular toolkit for reshaping dysfunctional immunity in metastatic colorectal cancer.
A primary strategy centers on delivering checkpoint blockade agents directly to the tumor. Nanoparticles that carry PD-L1 traps or surface-anchored antibodies localize the blockade effect to the tumor-stromal interface, improving receptor occupancy while minimizing peripheral exposure. For example, engineered hybrid cell membrane nanovesicles (NVs) co-displaying PD-1 and LAG-3 receptors were constructed to simultaneously block the PD-L1/PD-1 and FGL-1/LAG-3 axes (Fig. A) [ ]. In CT26 tumor–bearing mice, treatment with PD-1/LAG-3 NVs produced the strongest tumor growth inhibition (Fig. B), and this effect correlated with significantly higher TNF-α secretion (Fig. C). Flow cytometry showing an increased proportion of CD8⁺ T cells (~ 25%) and elevated granzyme B⁺ CD8⁺ T cells (~ 15%), alongside reduced frequencies of Tregs and MDSCs (Fig. D). Together, these data demonstrate that dual-checkpoint NVs more effectively reinvigorate cytotoxic T cell responses and remodel the immunosuppressive microenvironment than single-target approaches, highlighting their potential in potentiating mCRC immunotherapy. An alternative approach encodes checkpoint inhibitors as nucleic acids. LNPs delivering mRNA for transient checkpoint antagonists enable lesion-specific blockade that decays with time, reducing the risk of immune-related adverse events such as autoimmune hepatitis.
A second layer removes immunosuppressive ligands by RNA interference. siRNA or miRNA against PD-L1 or other checkpoints, delivered by lipid or polymeric nanoparticles, leads to sustained knockdown within both tumor and myeloid compartments. This enhances immunological synapse formation and restores cytotoxic responsiveness. In the study by Wang et al., PCIF1, a 5′ cap N6-methyladenosine (m6Am) methyltransferase, was found to be upregulated in colorectal cancer and to promote tumor growth via stabilization of FOS mRNA and induction of TGF-β signaling [ ]. Silencing PCIF1 with LNP-formulated siRNAs significantly reduced tumor burden in vivo: immunohistochemistry revealed elevated PCIF1 expression in CRC compared to adjacent tissues (Fig. E), wound healing assays showed markedly reduced migration after PCIF1 knockdown (Fig. F), and LNP-siRNA treatment decreased PCIF1 mRNA levels in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. G). Functionally, tumor volumes in mice treated with LNP–PCIF1 siRNAs were reduced by more than half compared to controls (Fig. H). Mechanistically, loss of PCIF1 decreased intratumoral TGF-β while enhancing IFN-γ and TNF-α secretion and NK cell infiltration, thereby sensitizing tumors to anti-PD-1 therapy (Fig. I). Also, another study showed that in MC38 colorectal models, PLGA nanoparticles delivering PD-L1 siRNA systemically were shown to accumulate preferentially in tumors, silencing PD-L1 expression, increasing CD8⁺ infiltration, and sensitizing tumors to T cell cytotoxicity without the need for systemic antibodies. Therefore, RNAi-based nanotherapy can reprogram the immunosuppressive microenvironment of mCRC by targeting epitranscriptomic regulators, opening a synergistic avenue with checkpoint blockade.

 
Checkpoint ligands are not isolated molecules but components of broader transcriptional programs driven by upstream regulators including STAT3, NF-κB, and HIF-1α [ – ]. Consequently, targeting these master regulators provides a more comprehensive strategy to relieve immune paralysis than blocking individual checkpoint pathways alone. Compared with direct blockade of checkpoint ligands, this upstream intervention has several advantages: it simultaneously downregulates multiple inhibitory ligands, prevents compensatory upregulation of alternative checkpoints, and exerts effects across both tumor cells and stromal compartments. Nanoparticles further enhance this approach by enabling co-delivery of siRNA with adjuvants or immunomodulators, ensuring coordinated remodeling of the tumor microenvironment into one more permissive for T cell priming and effector function [ – ]. For instance, nanoparticle-mediated silencing of STAT3 has been shown to restore dendritic cell antigen-presenting capacity, reprogram M2-like macrophages toward a proinflammatory phenotype, and suppress the secretion of immunosuppressive cytokines such as IL-6 and VEGF [ ].
Emerging evidence underscores the importance of LRG1-STAT3 signaling in colorectal cancer metastasis and its potential as a therapeutic target. Transcriptomic analyses comparing primary tumors with matched liver metastases identified LRG1 as one of the most consistently upregulated genes, a finding corroborated by immunohistochemistry, which revealed significantly higher protein levels in metastatic lesions (Fig. A, B) [ ]. Functionally, LRG1 promoted migratory capacity in colorectal cancer cell lines, while shRNA-mediated knockdown impaired cell migration (Fig. C) and markedly reduced the formation of hepatic metastatic nodules in vivo (Fig. D). Together, these findings position LRG1 as both a biomarker and functional driver of metastatic colonization, tightly coupled to STAT3 activity. Leveraging this mechanistic insight, nanoparticle-mediated STAT3 silencing has been developed as an immunotherapeutic strategy to dismantle immunosuppressive networks in the metastatic niche. AIRISE-02, a lipid-based nanoparticle co-delivering STAT3 siRNA and CpG adjuvant, exemplifies this approach [ ]. By simultaneously targeting tumor-intrinsic STAT3 signaling and reprogramming myeloid compartments, AIRISE-02 enhanced dendritic cell activation, reduced MDSC-mediated suppression, and fostered robust CD8⁺ T cell priming (Fig. E). Notably, in murine colorectal tumor models, AIRISE-02 achieved not only regression of local, treated tumors but also systemic control of distant, untreated lesions, reflecting an abscopal-like effect mediated by vaccine-induced immunity (Fig. F). The combination of AIRISE-02 with PD-1 and CTLA-4 blockade further potentiated these responses, resulting in durable tumor clearance and establishment of long-term T cell memory.
Each metastatic site requires a tailored design. In the liver, moderate PEGylation and hepatotropic ligands such as GalNAc or ApoE-mimics guide delivery to tumor-bearing regions while limiting KCs uptake [ ]. In the peritoneum, muco-adhesive polymers or gel depots extend retention on serosal surfaces, supporting complex payloads without systemic cytokine release [ , ]. In the lung, stealth particles with controlled release prevent off-target inflammation [ , , ]. Imaging labels such as near-infrared dyes or PET tracers should be incorporated whenever possible to confirm lesion-specific exposure and correlate pharmacokinetics with immune outcomes.
Taken together, nanoparticle-enabled checkpoint modulation in mCRC allows spatially confined and transcriptionally integrated immune reprogramming. When layered with effective vaccination and trafficking strategies, this approach transforms immune deserts into responsive microenvironments and extends the clinical utility of immunotherapy in traditionally unresponsive colorectal metastases.

 

## Tumor microenvironment reprogramming
mCRC persists not due to the absence of antitumor T cells, but because metastatic niches impose layered immunologic and structural barriers. These include aberrant vasculature, stromal fibrosis, myeloid skewing, chemokine-driven exclusion, and metabolic suppression [ , – ], all of which impede priming, block trafficking, and exhaust infiltrating effectors. Among them, tolerance programs shaped by TGF-β, VEGF, CXCL12-CXCR4, and adenosine or kynurenine metabolism are particularly dominant. Durable immunity in mCRC requires not only antigen recognition but active dismantling of these suppressive layers. Nanocarriers make this feasible by delivering modulators in spatiotemporally controllable pulses, enabling co-packaging of synergistic agents, and, when imageable, confirming on-target exposure before escalation. A central advantage is the ability to directly manipulate the cellular, cytokine, and metabolic architecture of the metastatic microenvironment, including macrophage polarization, chemokine gradients, and hypoxic stress.
Vascular and stromal access is the initial checkpoint. In CRLM, VEGF-driven endothelial anergy impairs leukocyte adhesion, while TGF-β-conditioned fibroblasts generate dense extracellular matrix that physically excludes T cells and suppresses APC function [ , ]. Nanoparticles carrying anti-VEGF or TGF-β-modulating payloads can normalize perfusion and remodel stroma when delivered as local, short-lived pulses, reducing systemic toxicity compared to free agents [ , ]. This transient normalization improves lymphocyte infiltration and enhances drug penetration. Mechanistic syntheses of CRLM consistently highlight these pathways as key gatekeepers of immune exclusion and resistance to therapy. A complementary example highlights how stromal-derived chemokine networks can be therapeutically rewired. In SMAD4-deficient CRC, aberrant TGF-β signaling induces high CCL9 expression, which engages CCR1⁺ myeloid cells to establish a prometastatic niche. To intercept this axis, engineered chemotactic nanovesicles carrying CCR1 antagonists were designed to preferentially trap TGF-β within metastatic foci [ ]. In mouse models, this intervention suppressed recruitment of CCR1⁺ macrophages and neutrophils, thereby attenuating liver colonization (Fig. A–D). This reduction in CCR1-driven myeloid influx directly shifts the cytokine field by lowering IL-6 and VEGF levels, demonstrating how nanocarriers modulate chemokine-guided cytokine gradients in situ. By directly modulating chemokine-driven myeloid trafficking, this approach exemplifies how nanoparticles can dismantle stromal-immune cross-talk that underpins metastatic fitness.
Beyond stromal remodeling, myeloid dominance remains a persistent suppressive force. TAMs and CCR2⁺ monocytes in liver metastases maintain IL-6- and VEGF-rich environments that suppress dendritic cells and blunt T cell activation. Nanocarrier-based delivery of CSF1R or CCR2 inhibitors can deplete these cells or reprogram them toward antigen-presenting phenotypes [ , ]. Iron oxide-based particles, including ferumoxytol and mannosylated SPIONs, not only induce M1-like polarization via NF-κB activation but also offer MRI visibility to monitor macrophage reprogramming [ ]. In these settings, nanoparticle-treated TAMs downregulate IL-10 and TGF-β while upregulating IL-12 and TNF-α, reshaping the cytokine milieu toward one that supports dendritic-cell activation and cytotoxic T-cell entry.
Another line of work demonstrates that reprogramming TAMs at the post-transcriptional level can remodel the suppressive landscape of colorectal liver metastases. Dicer, a ribonuclease critical for miRNA maturation, was identified as a central driver of M2 polarization in TAMs [ ]. Selective knockdown of Dicer using M2-targeted lipid nanoparticles carrying siRNA (M2pep-LNPs) repolarized TAMs toward an M1 phenotype, elevating IL-12 and TNF-α while reducing IL-10 and TGF-β production. In vivo, systemic administration of these nanoparticles not only curtailed liver metastatic outgrowth but also improved survival, with marked increases in intratumoral CD8⁺ T cell infiltration and effector cytokine production (Fig. E–L). Macrophage depletion studies confirmed that the therapeutic efficacy depended on TAM reprogramming rather than non-specific effects. These data highlight that nanoparticle-mediated macrophage polarization directly rewires local cytokine gradients from immunosuppressive to immunostimulatory states.

 
Additional strategies focus on simultaneously modulating tumor hypoxia and innate immune sensing to overcome metabolic suppression. Biomineralized MnO₂ nanoplatforms represent a particularly elegant design, as they decompose endogenous H₂O₂ to generate oxygen, thereby alleviating hypoxia-induced radioresistance and improving effector T cell persistence [ ]. This oxygenation reduces HIF-1α–dependent immunosuppression and weakens adenosine accumulation, both of which contribute to T-cell dysfunction. Under the acidic conditions of the tumor microenvironment, MnO₂ degrades to release Mn²⁺, which amplifies cGAS-STING signaling and promotes type I interferon secretion, leading to dendritic cell maturation and enhanced antigen presentation. When formulated as αPDL1@MnO₂, this platform further co-delivers immune checkpoint inhibitors, enabling simultaneous reversal of PD-1/PD-L1–mediated suppression (Fig. A-E). In bilateral CT26 models, αPDL1@MnO₂ combined with X-ray irradiation not only inhibited local tumor growth but also induced a potent abscopal effect, markedly delaying distant tumor progression and prolonging survival compared with monotherapies. Mechanistically, this synergy was associated with increased maturation of CD80⁺CD86⁺ dendritic cells, elevated infiltration of CD8⁺ and CD4⁺ T cells, polarization of TAMs from M2 to M1 phenotype, and upregulation of IFN-β, TNF-α, and IFN-γ in both primary and metastatic lesions (Fig. F and G). These multimodal effects collectively demonstrate that hypoxia relief, STING activation, and macrophage repolarization act in concert to reverse TME suppression. In parallel, Peng and colleagues developed a CuS/MnO₂ nanosystem loaded with the STING agonist diAMP (diAMP-BCM) to selectively boost antitumor inflammation while limiting protumor cytokines [ ]. By generating ROS and releasing Mn²⁺, diAMP-BCM reinforced STING/IRF7/CXCL10 signaling and type I interferon production without excessive NF-κB–IL-6 activation. In MC38 and CT26 colorectal cancer models, it induced cancer cell apoptosis/pyroptosis, recruited proinflammatory monocytes, and improved CD8⁺ T cell infiltration. When combined with anti-PD-1 therapy, diAMP-BCM turned “cold” tumors into “hot” ones, enhanced TLS formation, reduced T cell exhaustion, and strongly inhibited both local and distant tumors. Furthermore, adenosine and kynurenine-mediated suppression remain a critical bottleneck. Nanocarriers concentrating A2A antagonists or delivering CD39/73 and IDO1 inhibitors restore TCR signaling and reverse tryptophan starvation [ ]. These metabolic interventions become more effective when hypoxia is relieved, as oxygenation dampens adenosine signaling and synergizes with IDO1 blockade to restore effector function.
In hepatic and peritoneal metastases, hypoxia is not only immunosuppressive but also a detectable biomarker of disease progression [ ]. MnO₂-based nanozymes exploit this vulnerability by decomposing endogenous H₂O₂ into oxygen, thereby relieving hypoxia, while releasing Mn²⁺ to activate STING signaling and simultaneously provide T1-weighted MRI contrast for theranostic monitoring. A macrophage membrane-camouflaged hybrid nanoplatform (M@MnO₂-Au-SiO₂@CDDP) exemplifies this approach [ ]. The MnO₂ core relieved hypoxia and activated STING, the Au-SiO₂ shell provided structural stability and drug loading, and the CDDP payload induced DNA damage and immunogenic cell death. Importantly, macrophage-membrane cloaking facilitated tumor homing and immune evasion. In a colorectal peritoneal metastasis model, this construct markedly suppressed tumor burden compared with Au-SiO₂ or MnO₂-Au-SiO₂ controls (Fig. I–J). Mechanistically, treatment enhanced dendritic-cell recruitment and maturation, increased infiltration of CD8⁺ T cells, and elevated systemic IFN-β, TNF-α, and IL-6 levels, collectively remodeling the peritoneal niche into an immune-responsive state. Beyond therapy, the real-time imaging capacity of Mn²⁺ release offers a translational advantage in metastatic liver or peritoneal lesions, enabling confirmation of on-target exposure to support treatment escalation with reduced systemic risk.
Therefore, reprogramming the metastatic microenvironment is the essential scaffold on which nano-immunotherapy must operate. The most robust trajectory layers vascular and stromal normalization with myeloid re-education and CXCR4 disinhibition, then couples metabolic relief with hypoxia correction and innate ignition [ , ]. Nanocarriers make this orchestration practical by co-packaging synergistic payloads, positioning them at lesion rims with temporal control, and minimizing systemic spillover. Liver- and peritoneum-aware dosing, supported by image-guided confirmation, enables delivery to become a measurable and adjustable parameter. Remaining hurdles include interpatient variability, risks of cytokine flare with repeat administration, and the largely preclinical status of some components. Nonetheless, aligning vaccination with coordinated microenvironmental reprogramming offers a coherent framework to convert immune-cold metastatic niches into effector-permissive tissues and achieve durable disease control in mCRC.

 

## Controlled induction of Immunogenic cell death
A defining opportunity for nano-immunotherapy in mCRC is to convert metastatic deposits into in situ vaccines by inducing immunogenic cell death (ICD) in a site-adapted, controlled fashion [ , ]. True ICD is not generic cytotoxicity, it is a regulated process that releases a coordinated set of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), including pre-apoptotic calreticulin (CRT) exposure, ATP release, HMGB1 translocation, and type-I interferon signaling. These signals allow dying tumor cells to instruct antigen-presenting cells (APCs), especially dendritic cells, to cross-present tumor antigens and prime durable CD8⁺ T-cell responses. This distinction is particularly critical in tolerogenic organs such as the liver and fluid compartments like the peritoneal cavity, where non-immunogenic or excessive damage may aggravate suppression rather than relieve it [ ]. Nanoparticles enable ICD not simply by delivering cytotoxic agents, but by localizing their effect to metastatic rims, tuning the intensity and duration of cell death, and co-delivering innate immune agonists so that antigen release and danger signaling converge within the same APCs [ ]. Practical biomarkers, such as calreticulin exposure, extracellular HMGB1, ATP release, and type-I interferon transcription, have been consolidated in recent syntheses and serve as readouts in both preclinical and translational studies.
Chemotherapy-induced ICD with enhanced delivery precision, chemotherapeutics remains a prototypical ICD inducer in colorectal cancer [ , ]. Its anti-tumor efficacy depends in part on endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress-mediated ICD, and both clinical and preclinical studies link therapeutic response to DAMP release. Nanocarriers that enrich chemotherapeutics, such as doxirubicin, oxaliplatin or chemo-prodrugs, within liver, lung or peritoneal metastases increase local exposure without dose-limiting systemic toxicity. For example, Zhao and colleagues designed self-delivery nano-PROTACs (DdLD NPs) that integrate doxorubicin with the BRD4 degrader dBET57 into a single nanostructure [ ]. This construct not only retained the classical ICD-inducing features of doxorubicin but markedly amplified them, enhancing CRT exposure, extracellular HMGB1 release, and ATP secretion, which together promoted efficient dendritic-cell maturation and cross-priming of CD8⁺ T cells. At the same time, BRD4 degradation reduced glycolytic flux and suppressed PD-L1 expression, further reinforcing the immunogenic state of tumor cells (Fig. A). In CT26 models, DdLD NPs exhibited potent antitumor activity in CT26 models. Compared with DOX or dBET57 alone, the nano-PROTACs significantly reduced primary tumor burden and reshaped the intratumoral immune landscape, increasing the CD8⁺/Treg ratio and enhancing effector T-cell infiltration (Fig. B). Importantly, DdLD NPs produced a marked anti-metastatic effect, almost completely eliminating pulmonary metastatic nodules and restoring normal lung architecture, as shown by gross examination and H&E staining (Fig. C). Treated mice displayed elevated IFN-γ levels and expanded cytotoxic T-cell populations in peripheral lymphoid organs, consistent with systemic activation of adaptive immunity. these results demonstrate that coupling cytotoxic chemotherapy with targeted protein degradation within a unified nanoplatform can amplify ICD, reprogram tumor metabolism and immune evasion pathways, and generate robust systemic antitumor immunity capable of controlling both primary and metastatic disease. When co-formulated with innate immune activators (e.g., TLR agonists, STING agonists) or metabolic modulators (e.g., IDO inhibitors), these platforms sustain the immunogenic phenotype of dying tumor cells and enhance downstream T-cell priming [ , ]. Thus, chemotherapy-induced ICD, when harnessed by nanocarriers, provides a powerful foundation for mCRC nano-immunotherapy by converting conventional cytotoxic agents into dual-function platforms that not only debulk tumors but also generate durable, systemic antitumor immunity.
Exogenous stimulation, such as photothermal, photodynamic, or sonodynamic, inducing ICD under imaging guidance, photonic energy-thermal or ROS-based-provides an alternative means to induce ICD within spatially confined lesions [ , ]. Plasmonic nanoparticles (e.g., gold nanorods, nanostars) or silica-based photosensitizers convert light into local stress, producing ICD without damaging surrounding healthy tissue. These platforms effectively trigger calreticulin exposure, ATP and HMGB1 release, and systemic antitumor immunity in CT26 and MC38 models when the irradiance and temperature remain within the “immunogenic window”-sub-ablative to mildly ablative. Their imaging traceability via photoacoustic, optical enables real-time monitoring of lesion coverage and precise energy titration. In CT26-bearing mice, low-temperature photothermal therapy combined with either dendritic cell vaccination or anti-PD-L1 checkpoint blockade restored responsiveness to immunotherapy, highlighting the synergy of ICD with immune priming. A representative example of this strategy is the recent development of an oral sonodynamic nanomedicine (Ce6/R837@Lp127NPs), in which chlorin e6 and the TLR7 agonist R837 were co-encapsulated within a silk fibroin-plant lipid hybrid nanoparticle [ ]. Upon oral administration, these particles penetrated the colonic mucus and preferentially accumulated in orthotopic colorectal tumors under ultrasound guidance. Sonodynamic activation induced robust ICD, as evidenced by marked CRT exposure and HMGB1 release, while R837 enhanced dendritic cell maturation and antigen presentation. This in situ vaccination effect was further reinforced by systemic immune activation, characterized by elevated serum TNF-α/IFN-γ, increased mature DCs, and expansion of CD8⁺ T cells. Most strikingly, in combination with anti-PD-L1 therapy, this approach not only suppressed orthotopic tumor growth but also eradicated distant metastatic tumors (Fig. D-K).
Matrix-aware ICD strategies for peritoneal metastases. Peritoneal metastases present unique delivery challenges due to fluid turnover and diffuse nodule distribution. Mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs) are particularly suited for this environment: they adhere to collagen-rich metastatic surfaces, resist rapid clearance, and can co-load oxaliplatin, photosensitizers, or TLR agonists [ ]. Intraperitoneally administered MSNs transform the peritoneal cavity into a distributed ICD field with minimal systemic cytokine surge. Theranostic versions incorporate NIR dyes or PET labels to visualize distribution and guide downstream checkpoint modulation. Design studies emphasize that tumor-ECM adhesion and residence time, rather than dose alone, are key determinants of effective peritoneal nanoICD.
Controlled induction of ICD represents a feasible and potent method to convert immunologically “cold” metastases into functional vaccine sites. The success of this approach depends on precise tuning of the ICD stimulus, whether oxaliplatin, photonic energy, or hypoxia relief, and the spatial coordination of danger signals and antigen presentation [ , , ]. Nanoparticles make such coordination possible through lesion-specific release, co-delivery of immune activators, and built-in imaging surrogates to titrate dosing. These effects are best deployed in a rational sequence: first, a priming step that recruits dendritic cells; then, ICD to load them with antigen and danger signals; and finally, checkpoint relief (e.g., PD-L1 silencing or trapping) to sustain T-cell effector function.

 
To operationalize nano-immunotherapy for mCRC, four strategic modules have emerged, each addressing a distinct immunological bottleneck. As summarized in Table , nanoparticle-based tumor vaccines aim to restore high-quality priming and CD8⁺ T-cell breadth by protecting labile antigens and co-delivering innate agonists within a lymph-node- or lesion-targeted scaffold. Checkpoint modulation and gene silencing strategies concentrate PD-(L)1 blockade or STAT3 suppression within tumors to reduce systemic toxicity while unshackling local effectors, often in tandem with vaccination [ ]. A third module enforces innate ignition via co-delivery of STING or TLR agonists with antigen, crucial for overcoming hepatic/peritoneal tolerance and establishing a type-I interferon tone. Finally, stromal reprogramming approaches target myeloid and CAF dominance, particularly CSF1R/CCR2 circuits, to relieve exclusion and enhance APC function. Across strategies, the co-localization of immunologic triggers, route tuning (e.g., intravenous vs. intraperitoneal), and integration with imaging-readouts constitute shared design principles that underpin therapeutic success.

 

## Clinical application of nano-immunotherapy in mCRC
Nano-immunotherapy has demonstrated substantial potential in preclinical models of metastatic colorectal cancer, particularly in addressing the immune resistance associated with microsatellite-stable subtypes. However, its clinical application remains in the early stages. This section reviews the progress of ongoing clinical trials, the translational barriers that remain, and the strategic considerations for moving nano-immunotherapy toward routine clinical use in mCRC.
Most clinical efforts in mCRC immunotherapy have focused on checkpoint inhibitors, primarily benefiting patients with MSI-H or dMMR tumors. Nano-immunotherapy is now beginning to appear in clinical settings, especially in the form of lipid nanoparticle mRNA vaccines and other delivery platforms that enhance immunomodulation (Table  ).
The most advanced clinical program to date is autogene cevumeran, also known as RO7198457 [ ], developed by BioNTech and Genentech. This personalized mRNA vaccine, delivered via lipid nanoparticles, encodes up to 20 neoantigens tailored to each patient and is used in combination with PD-(L)1 inhibitors. A Phase 2 trial (NCT04486378) is enrolling patients with resected colorectal cancer who are positive for circulating tumor DNA [ ]. The goal is to determine whether personalized mRNA vaccination can eliminate minimal residual disease and extend disease-free survival. This adjuvant setting, characterized by low tumor burden, is well suited to vaccine-based interventions and compatible with the immunologic and tolerability constraints of the liver and peritoneal environments. Public sources confirm the ctDNA-guided design and the LNP formulation of the vaccine. In parallel, growing evidence supports ctDNA as a highly sensitive and actionable biomarker for risk stratification and trial endpoints in colorectal cancer [ ].
Another clinical-stage LNP vaccine, mRNA-5671 also known as V941 and co-developed by Moderna and Merck, targets recurrent KRAS mutations including G12D, G12V, G12C, and G13D. Its Phase 1 trial (NCT03948763) demonstrated the feasibility of administering mutation-specific mRNA vaccines in alignment with checkpoint inhibitor regimens [ ]. Although the trial has been completed and closed to accrual, it illustrates both the opportunities and attrition risks of early-generation cancer vaccines. It also highlights the importance of rational antigen selection and combination strategies in colorectal cancer. In newly diagnosed MSS/pMMR mCRC, an individualized heterologous vaccination strategy that combines a chimp adenoviral prime with a self-amplifying mRNA-LNP boost (GRANITE) is being tested as maintenance after FOLFOX/bevacizumab (NCT05141721). The randomized phase 2/3 design adds low-dose CTLA-4 and PD-L1 blockade on top of fluoropyrimidine/bevacizumab, with ctDNA molecular response in phase 2 and PFS in phase 3, explicitly aiming to generate neoantigen-specific CD8⁺ T cells in a context where such priming is typically absent. In the postoperative minimal-residual-disease setting, a lymph-node-targeted amphiphile nanovaccine against mutant KRAS (ELI-002) demonstrated high direct ex vivo immunogenicity and biomarker responses with a median relapse-free survival of 16.33 months in a phase I PDAC/CRC cohort (NCT04853017).
Beyond mRNA vaccines, other nano-facilitated immunotherapy strategies, such as the delivery of STING or TLR agonists, siRNA targeting PD-L1 or STAT3, and myeloid-modulating agents have not yet entered clinical trials specific to colorectal cancer. Nonetheless, experience from other indications is shaping translational principles. These include the importance of co-delivering antigen with innate immune stimulants, metering the intensity of innate signaling to avoid systemic cytokine spikes, and adopting route-specific strategies for regional delivery. Emerging nanoparticle systems that encapsulate cGAMP analogs or other cyclic dinucleotides are being developed to boost potency while minimizing systemic toxicity. These principles are directly relevant to colorectal cancer niches in the liver and peritoneum, even as CRC-specific trials are pending.
Overall, nano-immunotherapy in mCRC is at an early yet promising stage. The most immediate path forward lies in individualized mRNA vaccines delivered by lipid nanoparticles, particularly in patients with residual disease after metastasectomy and confirmed ctDNA positivity. As delivery systems become safer and more controllable, other agents such as innate agonists and gene silencers are likely to follow. Clinical success will depend on spatially optimized administration, reliable verification of nanoparticle exposure, and rational sequencing of immunotherapeutic components. Trials should incorporate both conventional endpoints such as safety and imaging response, and molecular measures such as immune activation and ctDNA clearance. With careful control of nanoparticle properties and dosing schedules, nano-immunotherapy has a credible pathway to durable clinical benefit in metastatic colorectal cancer.

 

## Challenges and outlooks
While the progress in nano-immunotherapy has demonstrated tremendous promise in preclinical models of mCRC, several critical challenges remain before its full translation into clinical practice can be realized. These obstacles can be broadly categorized into biological challenges inherent to mCRC pathology and the TME, engineering and delivery challenges associated with nanomaterial design, and broader strategic issues in therapy integration and personalization. Addressing these multidimensional barriers will be essential to unlock the therapeutic potential of nanotechnology in mCRC immunotherapy.

## Biological challenges

## Tumor heterogeneity and immune evasion
A major barrier to effective immunotherapy in mCRC lies in its pronounced intra- and intertumoral heterogeneity [ , ]. Genomic differences between primary and metastatic sites lead to variable neoantigen profiles, inconsistent immune cell infiltration, and fluctuating mutational burdens. Within a single lesion, the uneven expression of tumor-associated or neoantigens often enables immune escape, as selective immune pressure removes only sensitive clones while resistant subpopulations persist and expand. This diversity complicates antigen selection for nanovaccines, reducing the likelihood that a single or limited antigen repertoire will generate a broad or durable antitumor response. Although personalized neoantigen-based strategies offer a potential solution, they require comprehensive sequencing and bioinformatic analysis that may not be feasible for all patients. Compounding this challenge, some tumor clones may lose antigen-presenting capacity through downregulation of MHC-I molecules or transporter components, rendering them invisible to cytotoxic T cells even in the presence of effective vaccination.

## Immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment
The tumor microenvironment of microsatellite-stable colorectal cancer is dominated by dense stroma, hypoxia, and immunosuppressive cells including myeloid-derived suppressor cells, tumor-associated macrophages, and regulatory T cells [ ]. These elements collectively restrict T cell infiltration, reduce antigen presentation, and secrete inhibitory cytokines such as IL-10, TGF-β, and VEGF. While some nanoparticles are engineered to modulate these signals or reprogram immune cells, achieving sufficient accumulation and retention within these fibrotic and immunologically cold environments remains difficult. In particular, liver metastases often present with stromal barriers that limit nanoparticle diffusion and require locoregional delivery methods such as intra-arterial administration [ , ]. Furthermore, the immune composition of the tumor microenvironment is dynamic and can shift during treatment, necessitating delivery systems that are adaptive or capable of responding to environmental cues.

## Off-target effects and immunotoxicity
Systemic toxicity is another important concern in nano-immunotherapy, especially when delivering immune activators such as STING agonists, TLR ligands, or pro-inflammatory cytokines [ ]. Even with nanoparticle-mediated targeting, off-target immune activation may trigger inflammation in healthy tissues, leading to adverse events such as cytokine release syndrome or autoimmune-like symptoms. The carriers themselves may also induce toxicity. For example, cationic lipids in lipid nanoparticles are associated with hepatotoxicity, while inorganic components such as gold or iron oxide may accumulate in organs and raise long-term safety issues [ , ]. Additionally, repeated dosing of immunostimulatory nanomaterials may lead to immune desensitization or the formation of neutralizing antibodies, both of which can undermine therapeutic efficacy and complicate treatment schedules.
To mitigate these risks, biodegradable and immune-stealth nanocarriers are increasingly preferred. Materials such as PLGA, poly(β-amino esters), polythioethers, calcium phosphate, and lipid-like biodegradable polymers degrade into non-inflammatory metabolites, reducing chronic accumulation and minimizing long-term innate activation. Immune-stealth strategies, including PEGylation, zwitterionic coatings, and biomimetic membranes (e.g., RBC, platelet, or macrophage membranes), can further suppress complement activation, reduce opsonization, and avoid rapid clearance by KCs or splenic macrophages [ ]. These approaches directly lower the probability of activation–related pseudoallergy (CARPA)-like reactions and off-target cytokine release. For immune agonists, encapsulating TLR/STING ligands in degradable or stealth carriers also blunts systemic spillover, allowing agonist release to be spatially confined to metastatic lesions rather than healthy organs. As a result, local immune activation is preserved while systemic immunotoxicity is markedly attenuated.
Another related concern is the potential induction of cytokine storm when potent innate agonists leak systemically or are released too rapidly, leading to excessive IL-6, TNF-α, or type-I interferon signaling [ ]. Although uncommon with well-designed nanocarriers, this risk warrants attention in early-phase translation. Practical mitigation strategies include step-up dosing, slower infusion rates, controlled-release formulations, locoregional administration to limit systemic exposure, and early monitoring of inflammatory cytokines. In high-risk settings, short-term IL-6R blockade may also be considered. These approaches collectively help maintain strong antitumor immunity while preventing uncontrolled systemic inflammation.

## Engineering and delivery challenges

## Endosomal escape
Achieving efficient cytosolic delivery remains a critical challenge for nucleic acid-based nano-immunotherapy in mCRC. Following cellular uptake, nanoparticles are typically sequestered in endosomal compartments, where cargo degradation can occur before reaching the cytoplasm. This is especially problematic for therapeutics such as mRNA vaccines or siRNAs targeting immune checkpoints, which rely on intact cytoplasmic release for functional activity. Various design strategies have been proposed, including proton sponge mechanisms, pH-sensitive polymers, and membrane-disruptive peptides [ ]. However, most of these approaches demonstrate limited efficacy in vivo and may compromise membrane integrity or induce cytotoxicity. Striking a balance between efficient escape and cellular safety is particularly important when targeting antigen-presenting cells in secondary lymphoid organs, which are essential for priming robust immune responses.

## Targeting metastatic niches
mCRC frequently involves dissemination to the liver and peritoneal cavity, both of which impose distinct barriers to nanoparticle delivery. In the liver, fenestrated sinusoids and the activity of Kupffer cells lead to preferential nanoparticle uptake by non-tumor hepatic cells. While this hepatic tropism may be advantageous for targeting liver metastases, it also increases the risk of off-target effects and limits delivery precision. The peritoneal cavity presents additional difficulties, including poor vascularization and the presence of peritoneal fluid that dilutes nanoparticle concentration and hinders diffusion. To address these site-specific challenges, researchers have explored intraperitoneal or catheter-based local delivery approaches [ ]. However, such strategies require specialized infrastructure and may not be suitable for all clinical settings. Advances in ligand engineering and microenvironment-responsive designs, including nanoparticles that respond to acidic pH, matrix metalloproteinases, or elevated reactive oxygen species, offer potential to improve site-selective delivery.

## Controlled release kinetics
Precise control over the timing and location of immunomodulatory payload release is essential for maximizing therapeutic effect and minimizing systemic toxicity. If immune agonists are released too early in circulation, they may be rapidly cleared or cause widespread inflammation. Conversely, delayed or insufficient release at the tumor site can lead to suboptimal immune activation. To overcome these limitations, nanocarriers are increasingly designed with responsive release mechanisms, including pH-sensitive linkers, redox-cleavable bonds, and enzyme-triggered systems. Hydrogel scaffolds and polymeric platforms have shown promise in this context by offering sustained or localized release. Nonetheless, matching release profiles with the dynamic nature of immune activation and tumor progression in mCRC remains a key challenge, especially given the variability in metastatic burden and immune context across patients.

## Strategic perspectives

## Combination regimens
Combining nano‑immunotherapy with established cancer therapies offers a compelling route to surmount multiple resistance mechanisms in mCRC. A well‑designed combination can simultaneously enhance antigen priming, debulk tumor burden, and mitigate immunosuppressive barriers. For example, pairing a nanovaccine encoding tumor antigens with anti‑PD‑1 therapy can support expansion of antigen-specific T cells while reinvigorating their function in an otherwise suppressive microenvironment [ ].
Preclinical evidence supports this synergy. In a colorectal cancer model, researchers used nanoscale coordination polymer particles co-delivering oxaliplatin and dihydroartemisinin. The particles induced reactive oxygen species, triggered immunogenic cell death, and when combined with anti‑PD-L1 therapy, achieved complete tumor eradication and durable antitumor immunity [ ]. This approach elegantly links cytotoxic stress, antigen release, innate activation, and checkpoint inhibition. Similarly, nanomedicines combining gene therapy and chemotherapy (e.g. siRNA plus chemotherapeutic agents) have shown superior in vivo efficacy compared to either modality alone [ ], increasing apoptosis and reducing tumor volumes in gastrointestinal tumor models.
Beyond cytotoxic partners, anti-angiogenic agents represent a rational combinatorial axis [ , ]. VEGF and VEGFR2 blockade normalize tumor vasculature, reduce hypoxia, and enhance both immune-cell infiltration and nanoparticle penetration. Several studies demonstrate that pairing nano-immunotherapies, such as nanovaccines, STING/TLR carriers, or macrophage-reprogramming platforms, with anti-VEGF antibodies or VEGFR2 inhibitors produces stronger T-cell recruitment, more balanced myeloid composition, and deeper remodeling of metastatic niches. Such vascular-immune crosstalk is especially relevant in colorectal liver metastases, where endothelial anergy and hypoxia are dominant suppressive cues.
Yet crucial caveats must guide translation. The timing and sequence of interventions often determine synergy versus antagonism. If chemotherapy is administered too aggressively, it may deplete proliferating immune cells or damage draining lymphoid architecture, undermining vaccine priming. Conversely, suboptimal immunogenic death might produce tolerogenic signals or regulatory myeloid polarization. Dose, schedule, and spatial overlap must be finely optimized. In addition, biomarker-guided stratification is critical: patients with low baseline T cell infiltration or high myeloid burden may require myeloid reprogramming before vaccination. Finally, in clinical settings, cumulative toxicity and immunologic side effects may limit the permissible intensification of combination regimens.

## Smart and stimuli-responsive systems
Future nano‑immunotherapy platforms are expected to incorporate “intelligence” by sensing and responding to tumor-specific cues, thereby activating payload delivery only within metastatic microenvironments. Such systems are engineered to exploit biophysical or biochemical gradients typical of tumors, for example modest acidity, overexpressed enzymes, elevated ROS, and hypoxia, so that immunostimulatory agents are released precisely where needed and off-target exposure is minimized.
One class of examples is ROS‑responsive nanocarriers. These systems remain stable in circulation, but in regions of oxidative stress they undergo structural transformation or cleavage to release immune modulators. For instance, a ROS‑sensitive polymeric prodrug nanoparticle combining cisplatin and camptothecin was reported to trigger DNA damage and thereby activate the cGAS-STING pathway in situ [ , ], enhancing dendritic cell maturation and CD8⁺ T cell infiltration in colorectal cancer models. Another study designed a “nano‑bomb” by self‑assembly of HA‑modified IR780 and a ROS‑triggered prodrug core; upon encountering abundant ROS in tumors, the carrier bursts and releases payloads, leading to localized ICD and immune activation [ ].
pH‑responsive systems constitute another major branch. Tumors and endosomes often present mildly acidic pH compared to normal tissues. Nanoparticles built with acid‑cleavable bonds or pH‑labile crosslinks can carry immune drugs or adjuvants and release them upon encountering acidity. For example, nanogels have been used to deliver TLR7/8 agonists, remaining inert at physiological pH and degrading under endosomal acidification [ ], thus limiting systemic inflammation while achieving immune stimulation in lymphoid or tumor tissues.
Beyond ROS and pH, multifunctional nanogels and polymeric carriers responsive to enzymes, redox gradients, or light are under active development [ , ]. Smart nanogels that swell or collapse in response to tumor proteases or reducing environments allow tunable release kinetics of immunotherapies, improving the match between immune activation timing and T cell kinetics. Stimuli-sensitive hydrogels incorporating pH, photo, or magnetic triggers further extend the toolbox for locoregional deployment of immunomodulators.
These smart systems improve therapeutic index by restricting immune activators to malignant niches, synchronizing delivery with local cues, and reducing systemic inflammatory risk. They also allow adaptive treatment: for example, ROS generation by photodynamic or sonodynamic therapy can be harnessed as a trigger for subsequent payload release. In the metastatic colorectal cancer context, integrating smart responsive carriers with the niche-aware delivery strategies already discussed can raise the precision and safety of nano-immunotherapy.

## Artificial intelligence assisted neoantigen prediction and individualized nanovaccines
The development of personalized cancer vaccines relies heavily on the accurate identification of patient-specific neoantigens. Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) have enabled more efficient and scalable analysis of next-generation sequencing (NGS) data, allowing for robust prediction of peptide-MHC binding affinity, immunogenicity, and mutation clonality [ , ]. In particular, the integration of multi-omics profiling with immunopeptidomics has markedly improved prediction accuracy, as this approach combines genomic/transcriptomic mutation data with mass spectrometry-verified MHC-bound peptides to ensure that only truly presented neoepitopes are prioritized. This is especially important in avoiding false positives that arise from purely in silico predictions.
Incorporating these AI tools into the vaccine design pipeline enables rapid prioritization of neoantigen candidates, structural modeling of their MHC presentation, and prediction of TCR recognition likelihood [ ]. The resulting insights allow for the design of bespoke nanoparticle vaccines that co-deliver selected neoepitopes with potent adjuvants such as STING or TLR agonists. And effective pairing of AI-selected payloads with rationally engineered nanocarriers, optimized in size, surface properties, and release kinetics, has been shown to significantly enhance dendritic cell uptake and elicit durable cytotoxic T cell responses.
Furthermore, AI is increasingly used to guide nanocarrier engineering itself screening lipid compositions, PEG densities, and charge states, predicting serum protein corona formation, and modeling in vivo transport to optimize tumor-rim deposition [ , ]. These data-driven design loops shorten formulation development and enable patient- or lesion-specific tuning of delivery systems. Multi-omics data also inform patient stratification, identifying immune phenotypes, such as myeloid-dominant, T cell-excluded, or metabolically suppressed niches, that benefit from distinct nano-immunotherapy combinations. Integrating whole-exome sequencing, transcriptomics, and ctDNA kinetics with spatial or single-cell immune profiling enables selection of patients most likely to respond to nanovaccines, TLR/STING carriers, or macrophage-reprogramming platforms. In addition, deep immune profiling, including TCR repertoire analysis, myeloid signatures, and spatial mapping of immune exclusion, further supports personalized regimen design by matching the immunologic deficits of each metastatic lesion with specific nanocarriers and adjuvant strategies.
This synergy between AI-guided epitope discovery and nanotechnology is particularly promising for MSS mCRC, where traditional vaccines have largely failed due to poor antigenicity and T cell exclusion. Personalized nano-vaccines not only restore tumor antigen-specific immunity but also provide a flexible framework for incorporating patient-specific delivery requirements. As technologies mature, combining AI-predicted targets with multifunctional delivery systems is expected to streamline the clinical translation of personalized immunotherapy for otherwise refractory colorectal cancer.

## Translational challenges in human application
Nano-immunotherapy for mCRC is beginning to translate from concept to clinic, most tangibly through lipid-nanoparticle mRNA vaccines in post-metastasectomy, ctDNA-defined settings, with early signals of robust neoantigen-specific T cells and an emerging playbook that pairs vaccination with PD-(L)1 blockade, niche-aware delivery, and immune-pharmacodynamic readouts. Beyond vaccines, nano-enabled delivery of STING/TLR agonists, checkpoint/gene silencers, and myeloid- or stroma-directed agents remains preclinical but offers a clear path once human delivery and safety are de-risked.
Advancing these strategies into routine care still faces practical hurdles, but each has a workable path forward. Manufacturing and regulation require tight control of particle size, surface, and payload, so the best path is to standardize early by locking qualified excipients, building in-line process controls, and using modular CMC to keep even personalized products on a feasible timeline. Translation also suffers from a delivery gap between mice and people [ ], especially in fibrotic and tolerogenic sites such as colorectal liver and peritoneal metastases. Treat delivery as a measurable variable, embed PET, MR, or fluorescent surrogates or theranostic cores in early cohorts, favor locoregional routes when systemic exposure is unreliable, and tune ligands, PEG density, and size to bias deposition at metastatic rims [ ].
In parallel, the major immunotoxicity concerns specific to nanoparticle systems, particularly complement CARPA and cytokine release syndrome (CRS), must be anticipated in clinical translation [ ]. LNPs, liposomes, and some polymeric or inorganic carriers can trigger complement activation, leading to acute cardiopulmonary reactions that resemble pseudoallergy rather than IgE-mediated anaphylaxis. These events are formulation-dependent and more common at high first-dose exposure. Mitigation strategies include step-up dosing, slower infusion rates, premedication in high-risk settings, and the use of low-complement-activating PEGylation or zwitterionic surfaces.
Potent innate agonists (STING/TLR ligands) carry a different liability, such as dose-dependent cytokine surges. CRS risk can be reduced by confining agonists to the metastatic niche via hydrogels or locoregional infusion, using burst-limited release profiles, and monitoring IL-6/TNF-α kinetics in early human cohorts. Both CARPA and CRS tend to be predictable and manageable when their kinetics are characterized alongside imaging-verified biodistribution. Patient selection and endpoints for MSS-predominant disease also need sharpening. Concentrate trials in minimal-residual-disease cohorts identified by ctDNA, and pair standard safety readouts with immune pharmacodynamics such as antigen-specific T-cell responses, type-I interferon signatures, TCR clonality, and ctDNA kinetics [ ]. With manufacturable platforms, verified human delivery, metered immune activation, and mechanism-matched enrollment, nano-immunotherapy has a credible route from preclinical promise to durable benefit in mCRC.

## Conclusion
Nano-immunotherapy offers a promising strategy to address the longstanding challenge of treating mCRC, a subgroup largely unresponsive to current immunotherapies. Compared with melanoma or lung cancer, where nanomedicine has enabled effective lymph node targeting, efficient tumor infiltration, and robust responses, mCRC presents biological barriers that are fundamentally different. These include the immune-tolerant gut mucosal environment, the fibrotic and metabolically specialized liver metastatic niche, and the dense desmoplastic stroma that restricts nanoparticle penetration. These features make mCRC uniquely challenging, and also underscore why dedicated nano-immunotherapeutic strategies are required rather than simply adapting approaches successful in other cancers.
By leveraging the versatility of nanomaterials, this approach enables targeted delivery of tumor antigens, immunomodulators, and checkpoint inhibitors, while also modulating the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that nano-immunotherapy can enhance antigen presentation, promote T cell infiltration, and synergize with checkpoint blockade, leading to improved tumor control in mCRC models.
Although clinical translation remains in its early stages, ongoing trials, particularly those involving mRNA-lipid nanoparticle vaccines, signal growing interest and feasibility. Key challenges, including tumor heterogeneity, nanoparticle delivery to metastatic sites, and safety concerns, must be addressed to move this field forward. Cross-disciplinary collaboration, smart materials design, and biomarker-guided patient selection will be essential to realize the full potential of nano-immunotherapy. With continued innovation and clinical validation, nano-immunotherapy holds the potential to transform mCRC treatment and extend immunotherapeutic benefit to a broader patient population.
