## Abstract
While being a safe and effective precision therapy strategy, tumor immunotherapy still fails in many patients due to the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Emerging evidence has indicated that the targeted nano-drug delivery systems can accurately deliver therapeutic agents to potentiate the efficacy of immunotherapy. This review will outline recent advances in applying targeted nano-drug delivery systems in immunotherapy, with an emphasis on their crucial roles in regulating innate immune responses, adaptive immune responses, and immunogenic cell death. We will also discuss the current challenges and future opportunities for the clinical translation of targeted nano-drug delivery systems for tumor immunotherapy.
Keywords: Immunotherapy, Targeted nano-drug delivery systems, Tumor microenvironment, Immunogenic cell death, Innate immunity, Adaptive immunity

## Graphical abstract
There are two primary methodologies in targeted nano-drug delivery systems. Passive tumor targeting exploits the enhanced permeation and retention (EPR) effect, enabling nanocarriers to accumulate within tumor tissues. Alternatively, active targeting involves the specific internalization of nanoparticles into target cells through receptor-mediated endocytosis, which is activated by internalizing receptors.

•Targeted nano-drug delivery systems reshape immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment for enhanced immunotherapy.
•Targeted nano-drug delivery systems synergize innate and adaptive immunity to amplify anti-tumor immune responses.
•Immunogenic cell death triggered by targeted nano-drug delivery promotes tumor antigen presentation and T cell activation.
•Targeted nano-drug delivery systems achieve active tumor targeting of therapeutic agents through functional nanocarriers.

1.

## Introduction
Tumor microenvironment (TME) is a vascularized extracellular matrix (ECM) co-embedded with various types of cells and non-cellular components. Specifically, the immune cell populations in the TME play essential roles in regulating tumor initiation, progression, and resistance to treatment [ ]. During the past decades, emerging studies in this field have provided a new perspective for a deeper understanding of tumor biology, rendering the successful development of tumor immunotherapy. Through promoting immunogenic cell death (ICD), innate immune or adaptive immune responses to eliminate malignant cells, immunotherapy represents a cutting-edge therapeutic strategy for tumor patients [ , ]. Currently, several tumor immunotherapies have been approved for clinical use, including oncolytic virus therapy, dendritic cell (DC)-based vaccines, tumor whole-cell vaccines, cytokine therapy, adoptive cell transfer (ACT) therapy, monoclonal antibodies (mAbs), and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) [ ].
Innate immunity, as the body's first line of defense consisting of phagocytes, antimicrobial peptides, and complement pathways, plays an essential role in the early stages of pathogen invasion. Innate immunity can quickly initiate an inflammatory response after exposure to pathogens, thereby eliminating conserved microbial components of various pathogens [ ]. The adaptive immune system mediates a more sophisticated immune response, comprising immune organs, immune cells, and immune molecules responsible for executing adaptive immune functions [ ]. Notably, ICD, a form of regulated cell death (RCD), has shown an ability to activate adaptive immune responses in immunocompetent hosts [ ]. During this process, tumor antigens are presented on the cell surface through class I human leukocyte antigens (HLA-I). After being taken up by dendritic cells (DCs), these tumor antigens are cross-presented to CD8+ T cells to trigger an effective antitumor response [ ]. Innate immunity and adaptive immunity coordinate with each other to maintain the healthy status of the organism.
Nanomedicine is generally defined as the application of nanotechnology to create nanoscale particles from active pharmaceutical ingredients for the intervention of human diseases [ ]. Nanopreparations encompass diverse forms, such as inorganic nanomaterials (e.g., metal and metal oxide nanoparticles (NPs), carbon nanotubes, and black phosphorus NPs), dendrimers, polymers, micelles, liposomes, and nanocrystals [ ]. Nano-drug delivery systems, as a state-of-the-art technology in drug delivery, can achieve targeted delivery of therapeutic agents through functional nanocarriers [ ]. Advances in targeted nano-drug delivery systems, such as prolonging blood circulation time, improving bioavailability, increasing accumulation at tumor sites through the enhanced permeation and retention (EPR) effect, and minimizing adverse effects of antitumor drugs, have exhibited immense potential in tumor therapy [ ].

2.

## TME
TME is a supportive integrated system for tumor growth that consists of an intricate interplay of cellular and non-cellular components [ ] ( ). Within the cellular milieu, a broad spectrum of immune cell populations is presented, constituting the intricate and diverse TME. This complex system typically encompasses an array of immune cells, including T and B lymphocytes, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), DCs, natural killer (NK) cells, neutrophils, and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs). Additionally, stromal components such as cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), pericytes, and mesenchymal stromal cells further contribute to the richness and complexity of TME [ ]. Non-cellular components primarily encompass ECM and soluble signaling molecules, such as transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO), cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2), and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) [ ]. Importantly, tumor-derived metabolites generated through metabolic reprogramming, such as lactate, glutamine, and tryptophan, have also been identified as critical immunosuppressive factors within the TME [ ].
 In addition to the above components, the TME exhibits pathophysiological characteristics, including enhanced vascularization, hypoxia, decreased pH, and elevated interstitial fluid pressure [ ]. These complex components and properties jointly regulate the TME, forming an immunosuppressive TME (iTME) that supports tumor growth and metastasis by inhibiting the function of antigen-presenting cells (APCs) and infiltrating T cells. Moreover, iTME may contribute to tumor drug resistance, thus posing significant challenges to existing tumor immunotherapy strategies [ ].
2.1.

## Immunosuppressive mechanisms of pivotal immune cells in the TME
2.1.1.

## Cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs)
CD8-expressing CTLs represent a favored immune cell subset for targeting malignancies. During tumorigenesis and tumor progression, CTLs frequently undergo functional impairment and exhaustion due to the immune tolerance mechanisms and immunosuppressive factors prevalent within the TME. These conditions facilitate the development of adaptive immune resistance [ ]. CAFs, M2 macrophages, and regulatory T (Treg) cells collectively form a formidable immunologic barrier to impede CD8+ T cell-orchestrated antitumor immune responses [ ]. Consequently, in order to elicit sustained and potent antitumor immune responses, it is critical to prime and activate CD8+ T cells into effector CTLs through a process termed the tumor immunity cycle. This priming process fundamentally involves a synergistic interaction between innate immune cells (such as DCs and NK cells) and adaptive immune cells (such as CD4+ T cells). Upon activation, effector CTLs can migrate into the tumor's core or invasive frontier (termed the infiltrated-inflamed TME), thereby playing a pivotal role in cancer cell elimination [ ].
Rational immunotherapy strategies offer potential pathways for exogenous reactivation and/or priming of CD8+ T cells. One such strategy involves enhancing the ratio of costimulatory to coinhibitory molecules through immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) [ ]. Targeting the programmed death-1 receptor (PD-1) and its ligand programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1), as well as CTL-associated antigen 4 (CTLA-4), can alleviate CTL exhaustion and rejuvenate their priming respectively, thereby facilitating the elimination of antigen-presenting cancer cells [ , ]. Moreover, emerging studies highlight additional immune checkpoint receptors (ICRs) that orchestrate CTLs dysfunction through distinct mechanisms. These emerging ICRs include T-cell immunoglobulin and mucin domain-containing protein 3 (TIM-3), lymphocyte activation gene 3 (LAG-3), V-domain immunoglobulin suppressor of T cell activation (VISTA), and T-cell immunoreceptor with Ig and immunoglobulin and tyrosine-based inhibitory motif (ITIM) domains (TIGIT) [ ]. TIM-3 expression correlates with resistance to immunotherapy and poor clinical outcomes, which is mediated by galectin-9-triggered apoptosis in CTLs [ ]. LAG-3 and PD-1 cooperatively suppress CD8+ T cell function, promoting exhaustion and inhibiting interferon (IFN)-γ-dependent autocrine antitumor immunity [ ]. VISTA regulates CD8+ T cell development and functional homeostasis, and its blockage synergistically enhances the effector activity of tumor-specific CTLs when combined with a toll-like receptors (TLR) agonist vaccine [ ]. TIGIT promotes CD8+ T cell exhaustion through high-affinity interactions with its primary ligand cluster of differentiation 155 (CD155) [ ]. However, the main challenge of existing ICIs therapies is the low overall response rate [ ]. The development of novel combination therapies that optimally restore antitumor immunity may be a breakthrough in the future. Given the complex interplay between CTLs and Treg cells, modulating Treg cell activity may be necessary to augment CTL numbers and restore their functional capacity, leading to enhanced immunosensitivity of hepatocellular carcinoma cells.

2.1.2.

## CAFs
CAFs, the primary stromal cells in TME, play crucial roles in tumor immune evasion and drug resistance through intercellular contacts, secreting various regulatory molecules and extracellular vesicles, and remodeling the ECM. CAFs expressing major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class II molecules can present antigens to CD4+ T cells [ ]. However, recent studies have revealed the underappreciated functional expression of CAFs. For example, CAFs expressing programmed death ligand 2 (PD-L2) and Fas ligand (FasL) can sample, process, and cross-present antigens to kill CD8+ T cells in an antigen-dependent manner, thereby protecting tumor cells from immune attack [ ]. Given the high abundance of CAFs in various tumors and their significant tumor-promoting functions, CAFs have become important targets for cancer therapy. Currently, multiple drugs intervening the functions of CAFs have been extensively tested in preclinical studies [ ].

2.1.3.

## TAMs
TAMs, primarily derived from bone marrow monocytes, are closely linked to chemoresistance and associated with poor prognosis in multiple cancers [ ]. As the leading producers of proinflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin (IL)-1, IL-6, IL-12, or IL-23, etc.), TAMs play vital roles in initiating and maintaining tumor inflammation [ ]. In addition, TAMs can inhibit adaptive antitumor immunity. Many studies have revealed that TAMs can directly inhibit T-cell function [ ]. For instance, the high expression of mannose (MAN) receptor CD206 on TAMs can impair the cytotoxicity of CD8+ T cells by inhibiting CD45 phosphatase activity [ ]. Moreover, TAMs can inhibit the function of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells by recruiting natural regulatory T (nTreg) cells and promoting the generation and survival of induced regulatory T (iTreg) cells [ ].

2.1.4.

## MDSCs
MDSCs, constituting a pivotal element within the TME, exhibit robust immunosuppressive capabilities. MDSCs originate in the bone marrow and, upon the presence of tumors, relocate to peripheral lymphoid tissues and the tumor sites to reshape the TME [ ]. Compelling evidence has demonstrated a functional heterogeneity between MDSCs located in peripheral lymphoid organs and those resident within tumors. Specifically, MDSCs in peripheral lymphoid tissues are predominantly composed of polymorphonuclear MDSCs (PMN-MDSCs). These PMN-MDSCs exhibit a relatively mild suppressive capacity and primarily regulate tumor-specific immune responses, ultimately contributing to the establishment of tumor-specific T-cell tolerance. Notably, the differentiation of monocytic MDSCs (M-MDSCs) into macrophages and DCs is suppressed in these tissues. Conversely, within the TME, MDSCs display enhanced suppressive properties, with M-MDSCs being more abundant than PMN-MDSCs. Furthermore, M-MDSCs rapidly differentiate into TAMs in this context [ ], leading to robust tumor immunosuppression by expressing various cytokines and immunomodulatory molecules [ ].

2.1.5.

## NK/NKTs
NK cells are capable of rapidly killing neighboring cancer cells in a non-MHC-restricted manner [ ]. Natural killer T (NKT) cells, a subset of lymphocytes expressing both T cell receptor (TCR) and NK cell receptor, are further subdivided into two main categories. Type I NKT cells inhibit the differentiation of T helper type 2 (Th2) cells by secreting cytokines such as IFN-γ, while type II NKT cells suppress immune responses [ ]. In iTME, the activity of both NK cells and type I NKT cells is suppressed, indicating the potential impact of the TME on the function of these immune cells [ ].

2.1.6.

## DCs
In immune responses, the DCs system is APCs to effectively stimulate B and T lymphocytes. DCs include classical DCs (cDCs) and plasmacytoid DCs (pDCs), which are derived from myeloid cells, monocyte-derived DCs (MoDCs), and Langerhans cells (LCs). Currently, ample evidence has indicated that pDCs exert a negative regulatory role in the iTME. For example, pDCs induce Treg cells expansion through the expression of immunosuppressive factors such as TGF-β and inhibit the proliferation of CD4+ T cells by secreting granzymes [ , , ]. These findings provide a potential target for developing DCs-based immunotherapy strategies.

2.1.7.

## Neutrophils
Apart from their direct killing effect on tumor cells, neutrophils also promote the occurrence and development of tumors [ ]. Specifically, neutrophils can produce reactive oxygen species (ROS) to damage epithelial cell deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) that facilitates carcinogenesis. Additionally, neutrophils can also generate neutrophil elastase (NE) and prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) to promote tumor cell proliferation [ ]. More importantly, neutrophils can reshape the ECM through their biological activities to facilitate the migration and invasion of tumor cells [ ]. Meanwhile, neutrophils can also produce VEGF to stimulate the formation of new blood vessels, thus promoting tumor metastasis and invasion [ ].

2.2.

## Metabolite-mediated immunomodulation in the TME
2.2.1.

## Lactate
The level of lactate is ubiquitously elevated in many solid tumors’ TME, where it exhibits nutrition support and mediates multifaceted immunosuppressive effects [ ]. Emerging evidence indicates that lactate accumulation within the TME compromises the cytotoxic activity of effector T cells and NK cell-mediated immunity. Furthermore, lactate induces macrophage phenotypic switching towards the immunosuppressive M2 polarization state, collectively shaping an iTME [ ].
In this regard, therapeutic intervention targeting the lactate metabolic axis has emerged as a promising approach to reinvigorate antitumor immunity and overcome resistance to immunotherapeutic regimens. For example, pharmacological blockade of monocarboxylate transporter 4 (MCT4) effectively attenuates tumor-derived lactate extrusion, consequently restoring antitumor immune surveillance and potentiating immunotherapeutic efficacy [ , ]. Nevertheless, mono-therapeutic approaches targeting lactate metabolism via small-molecule agents demonstrate constrained outcomes. This is attributable to limited intracellular transport efficiency, pharmacokinetic profiles, and bioavailability. Beyond conventional small-molecule therapeutics, enzymatic strategies employing lactate oxidase (LOX) mediate the conversion of lactate into pyruvate and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). However, the clinical translation of LOX is hindered by inherent enzymatic instability in physiological conditions and inefficient cellular internalization [ ]. Therefore, nano-drug delivery systems demonstrate enhanced co-delivery efficiency for protein biologics and chemotherapeutics, representing a novel therapeutic paradigm to precisely modulate metabolic crosstalk between tumor and immune cells for reversing iTME [ ]. Emerging nanotherapeutic strategies have been developed to target lactate metabolism in the TME. These strategies include a nanoreactor for ultrasound-activated lactate depletion coupled with cuproptosis induction, as well as a nanocatalytic medicine that simultaneously degrades lactate and blocks pyruvate entry into mitochondria [ , ].These approaches demonstrate that precise modulation of tumor metabolite metabolism can effectively reprogram iTME for improved cancer therapy.

2.2.2.

## Glutamine
Glutamine, an essential metabolic substrate in the TME, fuels both malignant proliferation and immune cell activation. Tumor cells often exhibit higher glutamine uptake, indicative of their glutamine dependency [ ]. The tumor-infiltrating myeloid cells is predominantly composed of MDSCs and TAMs [ , ]. In hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) microenvironments, these myeloid populations undergo functional reprogramming toward an immunosuppressive phenotype, typified by granulocytic MDSCs (G-MDSCs) and M2-like TAMs. Such immunosuppressive adaptation compromises therapeutic responses to chemotherapy and immunotherapy, correlating with adverse clinical outcomes [ ]. The glutamine transporter inhibitor V-9302 specifically prevents glutamine uptake of triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) cells without affecting CD8+ T cells. This selective blockade increases glutathione production, enhancing CD8+ T cell's antitumor function. This evidence supports the “glutamine steal” hypothesis, where tumor cells deprive glutamine essential for antitumor immunity. Targeting tumor-specific glutamine metabolism may thus overcome immunotherapy resistance [ ]. Recent advances in nanomedicine have yielded innovative approaches to regulate glutamine metabolism within the TME. These include a sono-metabolic nanoplatform that synergizes ultrasound-triggered ROS generation with glutaminase (GLS) inhibition to boost T-cell responses [ ]. Another biomimetic nanosystem enables light-controlled co-delivery of glutamine metabolism inhibitor bis-2-(5-phenylacetamido-1,3,4-thiadiazol-2-yl) ethyl sulfide (BPTES) and the photosensitizer chlorin e6 (Ce6) to reprogram macrophage polarization [ ]. These strategies effectively remodel the iTME while preserving antitumor immunity by selectively interfering with glutamine metabolism.

2.2.3.

## Tryptophan
The tryptophan metabolic axis mediated by IDO1 and tryptophan 2,3-dioxygenase (TDO) has emerged as a promising therapeutic target in immunotherapy [ ]. Pathological upregulation of IDO1 in malignant cells and tolerogenic DCs drives multifaceted immunosuppression through kynurenine accumulation, which suppresses CTLs while promoting Treg cells differentiation [ ]. This pathway also generates quinolinic acid that activates forkhead box transcription factor O1 (FOXO1)/peroxisome proliferator activated receptor-γ (PPAR-γ) signaling, pushing macrophages toward M2-type polarization in glioblastoma (GBM) [ ]. Collectively, these findings underscore the therapeutic necessity of dual IDO1/TDO blockade to counteract the multifaceted immunosuppression by tryptophan metabolites. Cutting-edge nanomedicine approaches are now addressing tryptophan metabolic dysregulation within the TME. For example, a genetically engineered Clostridium butyricum (CB) (L-Trp CB) strain delivers tryptophan while simultaneously suppressing IDO1 activity [ ]. Moreover, a light-activatable nanoassembly can enable tumor-specific IDO degradation coupled with photodynamic immunomodulation [ ]. These strategies demonstrate that dual modulation of tryptophan availability and kynurenine production can effectively reverse IDO1-mediated immunosuppression in the TME.

3.

## NPs
The solid particles with a size of 1–100 nm, formed by the dispersion of drugs or the carrier excipients using the nanocrystallization technology are called NPs [ ]. NPs can be divided into nanocapsules and nanospheres according to their structures. The structure of a nanocapsule is membrane-shell type, and the structure of a nanosphere is skeleton type or core-shell type. Both of them can be dispersed in water to form a colloidal solution [ ]. Nanostructures can change the in vivo and in vitro properties of materials. On one hand, nanostructures have a larger specific surface area, and as the particle size decreases, more atoms are exposed to the interface. On the other hand, NPs may change the pharmacokinetic characteristics of drugs in vivo, thus significantly affecting the therapeutic effect [ ].
3.1.

## Commonly used carrier materials
It has been reported that the commonly used carrier materials for NPs include natural and synthetic polymer materials. Natural polymer materials mainly comprise albumin, chitosan, and gelatin [ ]. Albumin can be used as drug-binding sites due to the presence of more negatively charged amino acid residues in its structure. The main benefits of utilizing albumin stem from its exceptional compatibility with human cellular structures, its non-toxic profile, along with its biodegradable nature, which collectively preclude any immune responses [ ]. Chitosan consists of N-acetyl-d-glucosamine and d-glucosamine interconnected through β-1,4 linkages. The chitosan NPs carry a positive charge attributed to their amine groups, enabling them to adhere to negatively charged mucosal membranes. This adhesion facilitates the sustained release of encapsulated drugs. Consequently, the mucoadhesive characteristics of chitosan NPs are extensively utilized in oral drug delivery [ ]. Gelatin, a protein mixture derived from collagen through acid or alkali-catalyzed hydrolysis, exhibits considerable promise for drug delivery applications owing to its easy accessibility, robust stability, and extended in vivo storage. Notably, gelatin's amphoteric properties can influence mechanical strength and swelling dynamics. Therefore, the physicochemical profile of gelatin NPs can be tailored through the incorporation of various cross-linking agents. This modification enables gelatin to function as a versatile carrier matrix for encapsulating a wide range of particles, thereby accelerating the development of multifaceted nanoplatforms [ ].
Synthetic polymer materials are diverse and encompass several major categories. Polyester, a prominent group, includes a variety of substances like polylactic acid (PLA), polyglycolic acid (PGA), polylactic-co-glycolic acid (PLGA), and polycaprolactone (PCL). These polymers are widely used due to their biodegradability and compatibility with biological systems [ ]. Additionally, poly-alkyl-cyano acrylates (PACA) are noteworthy for their ability to release drugs more rapidly compared with other polymers. This characteristic makes it a valuable option in specific applications where quick drug release is desired [ ]. Amphiphilic block copolymers also play a crucial role in synthetic polymer materials. Notably, methoxy polyethylene glycol-polylactic acid (mPEG-PLA) and methoxy polyethylene glycol-polylactic-co-glycolic acid (mPEG-PLGA) combine hydrophilic and hydrophobic properties, enabling them to form micelles that can encapsulate and deliver drugs effectively [ ]. Lastly, thermosensitive materials can undergo discontinuous changes in their physical properties at specific temperature changes, making them suitable for controlled drug release applications where temperature regulation is feasible [ ]. Each of these polymer materials has its unique characteristics and applications, contributing to the diverse landscape of advanced drug delivery systems.

3.2.

## Preparation methods
The preparation methods of NPs include the dispersing method, polymerization, and surface modification of NPs. The dispersing method is suitable for the preparation of NPs with polymer materials, including in-liquid drying, high-temperature denaturation, chemical crosslinking, and salting-out condensation [ ]. Polymerization initiates with the monomeric material and proceeds to obtain NPs under certain physical and chemical conditions. The particle size, structure, and surface properties of the obtained NPs can be controlled by adjusting the conditions of polymerization reaction, but there may be residual unreacted monomers, initiators, or catalysts. Polymerization can be mainly divided into emulsion polymerization and interfacial polycondensation according to the different micro-positions of the polymerization reaction [ ]. The surface modification of NPs has been widely used to prolong blood circulation time and improve target enrichment [ ]. In recent years, a new strategy has been developed to encapsulate NPs with cell membranes (such as cell membranes of red blood cells, platelets, white blood cells, or cancer cells). The obtained biomimetic NPs can achieve specific delivery targets according to the properties of cell membranes. For example, erythrocyte membrane encapsulation can reduce macrophage phagocytosis of NPs. Platelet membrane encapsulation can selectively enrich NPs in thrombus sites. In addition, white blood cell membrane or tumor cell membrane encapsulation can prolong the blood circulation time of NPs and improve tumor enrichment [ , ].

4.

## Targeted nano-drug delivery systems
Targeted nano-drug delivery refers to the selective delivery of nano-drugs to targets. This delivery is specifically aimed at targeting tissues, organs, cells, or even specific cellular organelles by employing carriers, ligands, or antibodies. The primary objective of this delivery method is to maximize the concentration of nano-drugs in the target areas. Additionally, it aims to release drugs within the target areas at a controllable speed and manner. By achieving these objectives, targeted nano-drug delivery strives to enhance therapeutic effects and simultaneously minimize side effects [ ]. In the realm of nanomedicine, a diverse array of nanopreparations exists which facilitate this precise delivery. The NP-based targeted delivery systems within this category are particularly noteworthy. They have become the most commonly used technical approaches in nanomedicine to promote the precise target of active molecules to specific tissues and microenvironments [ ]. Targeted nano-drug delivery systems have extensive applications, not only in the treatment of various diseases but also in the diagnosis, sensing, and imaging of encapsulated drugs, thereby ameliorating disease progression [ ].
According to the different mechanisms of targeted drug delivery, targeted nano-drug delivery systems can be divided into two categories: passive targeted nano-drug delivery systems and active targeted nano-drug delivery systems ( ). Passive targeted nano-drug delivery systems exploit the modulation of NPs’ physicochemical parameters, including size, morphology, rigidity, and surface charge, to optimize biocompatibility with biological systems. For instance, NP size determines their propensity to extravasate into tissues with discontinuous vasculature, such as the liver and spleen. Correlating these parameters with pharmacokinetic behaviors (including circulation duration and organ-specific accumulation) informs rational design principles for NPs carriers [ ]. Active targeted nano-drug delivery systems are most commonly used in the treatment of tumors [ ]. In these systems, nanocarriers play crucial roles and are mainly divided into organic nanocarriers and inorganic nanocarriers. Organic nanocarriers primarily include metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), micelles, liposomes, microbubbles, and hydrogels. While inorganic nanocarriers encompass gold NPs, silica NPs, iron NPs, and other inorganic salt NPs [ , ] ( ).
 The evolution of targeted nano-drug delivery systems has profoundly influenced the field of modern medicine ( ). Since the report of controllable release polymer devices in 1964, research in this field has gradually laid a foundation. Subsequently, the discovery of the EPR effect in 1986 provided solid theoretical support for the specific accumulation of nanodrugs in tumors. In 1995, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved Doxil formulation marked a significant breakthrough in the clinical application of nanodrugs. Entering the 21st century, the research on targeted nano-drug delivery systems continued accelerating. In 2011, the development of cell membrane-cloaked NPs significantly enhanced the biocompatibility and stability of nanodrugs, providing possibilities for further clinical applications of nanodrugs. By 2019, the lipid NP messenger ribonucleic acid (mRNA) tumor vaccine (mRNA-4157) had entered the clinical trial stage, opening new avenues for tumor immunotherapy with this innovative technology [ ].
 In the field of tumor immunotherapy, targeted nano-drug delivery systems have demonstrated unique therapeutic potential by precisely targeting cancer cells, TME, and the peripheral immune system [ ]. Currently, numerous nanodrugs leveraging this targeted delivery mechanism are available on the market for tumor immunotherapy, exhibiting high targeting capability and the potential to minimize side effects. With the continuous progress of nanotechnology and deepening research, targeted nano-drug delivery systems are poised to play an increasingly critical role in tumor immunotherapy [ ]. Here, we summarize the currently available targeted nano-drug delivery systems for innate and adaptive immune responses ( ) [ , , , , , , , , , , , , , , ], aiming to provide valuable insights for further research in this promising field.
 
5.

## Targeted nano-drug delivery systems in regulating innate immunity
The innate immune system is a natural defense mechanism that has been naturally formed during the long-term evolution of organisms. Its core architecture covers tissue barriers, innate immune cells, and innate immune molecules, which jointly construct the first line of defense against external pathogen invasion [ ]. Innate immune system broadly encompasses a multitude of tissues and involves cellular entities derived from both hematopoietic and non-hematopoietic sources. Hematopoietic cells comprise a diverse spectrum of immune cell populations, such as macrophages, mast cells, neutrophils, eosinophils, DCs, and NK cells. These specific cellular subsets harbor germline-encoded recognition receptors, enabling them to be activated in response to inflammatory stimuli and subsequently differentiate into effector cells with limited lifespan, thus eliminating infectious agents. Conversely, non-hematopoietic components primarily include the skin and the epithelial linings of the gastrointestinal, genitourinary, and respiratory tracts [ ]. When the body encounters pathogen invasion, these innate immune cells can rapidly respond and initiate innate immune responses to eliminate pathogens [ ]. To enhance the cellular defenses, the innate immune system additionally comprises a humoral aspect, which consists of well-defined elements such as complement system proteins, lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-binding protein, C-reactive protein (CRP), along with various pentraxins, collectins, and antimicrobial peptides. These molecules not only directly participate in killing pathogens, but also strengthen the body's defense ability by regulating immune responses and promoting inflammatory responses [ ].
With the rapid development of nanotechnology, its application in enhancing the efficacy of the innate immune system has gradually attracted attention. Recently, various innovative targeted nano-drug delivery systems have been designed to achieve precise regulation and enhancement of innate immune responses.
5.1.

## Targeting macrophages
Based on biomimetic targeting and immunomodulatory synergy design, a size-switchable and dual-targeting nanodrug for TNBC has been developed. The key design embeds small-sized doxorubicin (DOX) loaded di-stearoyl-polyethylene glycol-phosphatidylethanolamine 2000 (DSPE-PEG2000)-modified poly(amidoamine) (PAMAM) dendrimer(DOX@P) within the large-sized phospholipid monolayer of imiquimod (R837)-encapsulated reconstituted high-density lipoprotein (rHDL), creating a pH-responsive cascade release system. In the acidic TME, imine bond cleavage triggers DOX@P release, enhancing DOX penetration into dense tumor cores. Meanwhile, the residual rHDL precisely delivers R837 to M2-like type TAMs via surface-conjugated M2 macrophage-targeting peptide (M2pep), promoting their repolarization into antitumor M1-like type. This design ingeniously integrates nanocarrier physical properties (size transition) with immunomodulatory function (TAMs reprogramming), synergizing chemotherapy and immunotherapy to convert “cold” tumors into “hot” tumors. TNBC exhibits an iTME with abundant TAM infiltration. This therapeutic design effectively reverses immune tolerance by reprogramming TAM phenotypes, though its efficacy may be constrained in tumors with low TAM infiltration. While the biomimetic properties of rHDL ensure system safety, potential membrane interactions from PAMAM dendrimers may pose long-term toxicity risks, necessitating further investigation of their metabolic pathways [ ].
Similarly, a blood-brain barrier (BBB)-penetrating NP system macrophage plasma membrane (MPMs) decorated DSPE-PEG-loaded infrared 792 (IR-792) NPs (MDINPs) for GBM therapy has been designed. This strategy mimics the natural immune chemotaxis of macrophages by integrating membrane proteins such as integrin α4 and macrophage-1 antigen (Mac-1). The integrin α4 on MPMs binds to vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1) on GBM cells, activating CAM-mediated endocytosis, while Mac-1 disrupts BBB integrity by regulating tight junction proteins to enhance penetration. This design integrates photothermal therapy (PTT) with near-infrared Ib (NIR-Ib) imaging via biomimetic immune cell targeting for therapeutic applications. Although both GBM and TNBC exhibit abundant TAMs in their TME, heterogeneity of GBM is primarily characterized by its unique anatomical barrier (BBB) and high invasiveness. Thus, MDINPs prioritize enhanced drug delivery rather than direct antitumor immune activation. The high photothermal effect of NIR-Ib compensates for its limited drug-loading capacity, while DSPE-PEG and MPMs ensure excellent biocompatibility. However, residual membrane proteins may trigger immunogenicity, necessitating long-term immunotolerance evaluation. A major limitation is its strong dependence on BBB integrity. Tumor vascular abnormalities or heterogeneous BBB disruption may impair NP accumulation [ ].

5.2.

## Targeting DCs
The integrated modular vaccination system cancer cell membrane (CM)-coated AS01 liposomes (CLPs)@recombinant keratin (RK) 84-T-based root layer (KTMN) (CLP@KTMN) was developed to address perioperative tumor recurrence by spatiotemporally separating immune activation and wound healing. The nanocarrier employs RK to construct a modular microneedle. The tip layer (RK-81-1Aα) rapidly mitigates inflammation and accelerates tissue repair in the dermis. While the root layer (RK-84-T) recruits DCs in the epidermis via CD205 receptor to enhance antigen presentation of CLPs. This spatial segregation mimics pathogen-immune interactions, leveraging keratin's innate immunomodulatory properties to coordinate DC migration and antigen uptake, thereby reshaping the TME. The efficacy of this modular microneedle in immunosuppressive tumors was validated using TNBC cell lines and patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models. Furthermore, targeting depth is constrained by microneedle height, potentially limiting efficacy in deeper or poorly vascularized tumors. While AS01 liposomes achieve effective CLP encapsulation with sustained release, scaling production of clinical-grade liposome requires further development. Despite keratin's inherent biocompatibility, AS01-mediated proinflammatory responses demand temporally/spatially optimized administration to circumvent immunosuppressive cascade activation [ ].
A parallel approach leverages molecular targeting of CD103+ DCs to systemically reprogram the TME. Current stimulators of IFN genes (STING) agonists for tumor immunotherapy are limited to localized delivery due to systemic toxicity and short half-life, which fails to address tumor heterogeneity. To overcome this, a systemically deliverable cationic liposomal nanoplatform encapsulating STING agonist ADU-S100 (SA) has been constructed. The liposomes are modified with WH peptide to target Clec9a, a C-type lectin receptor expressed on CD103+ DCs. This targeted delivery system specifically activates the STING pathway in CD103+ DCs, inducing type I IFN production and enhancing antitumor immunity. Importantly, this approach overcomes the limitations associated with intratumoral administration. This study evaluated the therapeutic efficacy and mechanisms in both “hot” (colon cancer model) and “cold” (melanoma model) tumors. When combined with anti-PD-L1 therapy, the treatment demonstrated enhanced tumor growth suppression in both models. Liposomal encapsulation enhanced SA intracellular delivery efficiency, and the targeting peptide promoted DC-specific uptake. However, clinical translation consistency may be compromised by heterogeneous Clec9a receptor expression across human DC subsets. Additionally, potential immunogenicity of the WH peptide requires further evaluation [ ].

5.3.

## Targeting neutrophils
Recently, a cisplatin-loaded nano-pathogen system was designed to eliminate residual microtumors after PTT by ingeniously utilizing the neutrophils in situ hitchhiking mechanism. The core design of this nanocarrier utilizes bacterial outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) to encapsulate NPs, creating a biomimetic structure bearing pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs). These PAMPs are specifically recognized by TLRs on neutrophil surfaces, activating their innate phagocytic function. By mimicking pathogen-immune system interactions, this design integrates nanocarriers with immunomodulatory mechanisms, which can simultaneously enhance drug delivery targeting and leverage inflammatory signals to guide neutrophil migration toward tumors. However, its targeting efficiency may be constrained by tumor size and vascular status. Moreover, the inflammation-dependent mechanism could limit its application in non-inflammatory tumors. The poly (ethylene glycol) methyl ether-block-poly(lactide-co-glycolide) (PEG-b-PLGA)-based nanocarrier demonstrates excellent drug encapsulation efficiency, enabling effective loading cisplatin. In terms of biocompatibility, the US FDA-approved biodegradable polymer PEG-b-PLGA ensures systematic safety. However, LPS in OMVs may induce transient inflammatory responses, warranting consideration of immune tolerance for long-term applications. The combination of these two therapies can exert a superb synergistic effect in breast and colon tumor models [ ].
Following a similar rationale, researchers have developed an inflammation-responsive nano-delivery system neutrophil-exosomes (NEs-Exos)/DOX utilizing neutrophil-derived exosomes, which leverages the natural chemotactic property of neutrophils for targeted glioma therapy. The system employs neutrophil-derived exosomes to encapsulate DOX through ultrasound-assisted loading, achieving high drug encapsulation efficiency while preserving native membrane proteins on the exosomal surface. In contrast to EPR-based or receptor-ligand targeting strategies, this platform exploits the inflammatory TME, utilizing cytokine-activated neutrophil chemotaxis to mediate BBB penetration and tumor targeting. Exosome membrane proteins CD63 and tumor susceptibility gene 101 (Tsg101) mediate immune cell interactions, attenuating immunosuppression via IL-33 downregulation. Regarding targeting specificity, NEs-Exos/DOX rely on neutrophil-derived inflammatory chemotactic signals for precise delivery, though this mechanism may be limited in low-inflammation or “cold” tumors. Ultrasound loading enables effective DOX encapsulation, but shows lower drug loading than synthetic nanocarriers, requiring improved drug-membrane binding strategies. While natural exosomes avoid synthetic carrier toxicity, DOX's systemic toxicity necessitates dose optimization. This approach shows promise for heterogeneous gliomas, though generalization to non-inflammatory subtypes requires validation [ ].

5.4.

## Targeting NK/NKT cells
A distinct strategy was employed to construct a simvastatin-loaded nano-delivery system simvastatin PLGA-MAN NPs. This system was further integrated with anti-PD-L1 antibody to enhance therapeutic efficacy in a hemi-splenic inoculated advanced-stage HCC model. In detail, simvastatin restores the stationary state of activated hepatic stellate cells (aHSCs) by stimulating Kruppel-like factor 2 (KLF2)-nitric oxide (NO) signaling pathway in liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSECs), thereby alleviating LSEC capillarization to return to the stromal microenvironment. In addition, simvastatin also upregulates C-X-C motif chemokine 16 (CXCL16) in LSECs to recruit NKT cells to inhibit tumor progression. MAN receptors are highly expressed on LSECs. MAN-modified NPs achieve active targeting via ligand-receptor binding, significantly enhancing hepatic drug accumulation. However, liver fibrosis heterogeneity may lead to uneven drug distribution, potentially compromising therapeutic efficacy. PLGA polymers effectively encapsulate hydrophobic simvastatin, offering biodegradability and biocompatibility. However, MAN modification may trigger complement activation or immunogenicity, thus requiring further long-term safety evaluation. [ ]. Similarly, another innovative approach combines immune targeting and microenvironment modulation. A bispecific nanosystem has been engineered using hematopoietic stem cell (HSC)-derived nanovesicles (Hn) functionalized with dual gripper ligands anti-PD-L1 and anti-NK receptor group 2 member (DNKG2D), and encapsulated with colony-stimulating factor (CSF). This design integrates targeted delivery, immune activation, and microenvironment modulation. The anti-NKG2D ligand activates endogenous bone marrow (BM)-resident NK cells by binding to their activating receptor. While anti-PD-L1 blocks immune checkpoint interactions and captures tumor cells, physically bridging NK and tumor cells to enhance cytotoxicity. CSF further promotes HSC differentiation, boosts memory T-cell generation, and restores bone homeostasis. Hn inherits BM homing receptors from parental HSCs to enable selective accumulation in the BM. However, the disseminated nature of hematologic malignancies and spatial heterogeneity in BM vascularization or ECM composition may lead to uneven drug distribution, potentially limiting efficacy in advanced or anatomically complex lesions. Notably, dual ligand conjugation may introduce steric hindrance, which may lead to compromised payload capacity or stability. Hn exhibits favorable biocompatibility with no significant toxicity in vivo. In contrast to solid tumors where stromal heterogeneity significantly limits drug penetration efficacy, hematologic malignancies present distinct therapeutic challenges due to the dissemination of malignant cells within a highly dynamic microenvironment. This bispecific targeting approach represents a promising therapeutic strategy for addressing these unique challenges in hematological malignancies [ ].

6.

## Targeted nano-drug delivery systems in regulating adaptive immunity
Adaptive immunity is a highly specialized immune response after encountering specific pathogens, characterized by specificity, tolerance, and memory. The adaptive immune response mainly comprises humoral immunity mediated by B lymphocytes and cellular immunity mediated by T lymphocytes [ ]. In the humoral immune response, B2 cells, also known as follicular B (FO B) cells, are the core participants. Upon antigen stimulation, B2 cells undergo a series of proliferation and differentiation processes. Ultimately, B2 cells can differentiate into plasma cells, which secrete specific antibodies and memory B cells to regulate the secondary immune response. The cellular immune response is mainly born by T cells, which are divided into three types: CTLs, T helper cells (Ths), and Treg cells. CTLs can specifically recognize endogenous antigen peptide-MHC-I molecular complexes to induce apoptosis of target cells by secreting perforin or activating the Fas-FasL/TNF-TNF receptor (TNFR) pathway. Ths which include subgroups such as T helper type 1 (Th1), Th2, and T follicular helper (Tfh) cells, can trigger the release of cytokines by recognizing and binding pathogenic antigens to initiate adaptive immune responses. Treg cells (such as nTreg and iTreg) ensure the appropriateness and persistence of immune response by suppressing excessive activation of other immune cells, thus maintaining the homeostasis of the immune system and preventing the occurrence of autoimmune diseases [ , , ].
Antigen presentation, being the initial step of the immune response, involves the antigen being ingested, processed, and displayed on the surface of APCs in the form of immunogenic peptides, followed by being recognized by immunocompetent cells [ ]. This process plays a pivotal role in detecting the invasion of pathogens (such as bacteria) or detecting mutations in malignant tumors [ ]. The antigen presentation system is mainly composed of APCs and T lymphocytes. APCs mainly include cells expressing MHC class II molecules such as DCs, monocytes/macrophages, thymic epithelial cells, and B lymphocytes, which are responsible for ingesting and processing antigens, and transferring antigen information to antigen-specific CD4+ T lymphocytes [ , ]. The antigen presentation process begins with APCs recognizing proteins on the surface of cancer cells [ ]. Subsequently, these proteins undergo internalization and degradation, which are then bound to MHC molecules and displayed together on the surface of APCs [ ]. The TCR on the surface of T lymphocytes can recognize the cancer cell protein peptides displayed on these MHC molecules, thus triggering the activation of T lymphocytes and subsequent immune responses [ ].
The rapid development of nanotechnology has extensively promoted the application of targeted nano-drug delivery systems in the induction and enhancement of adaptive immune response.
6.1.

## Targeting ICD
ICD is a specific type of RCD that ultimately triggers immune memory, distinguishing it from other RCD patterns which solely engage innate immune mechanisms or evoke active immunosuppression. ICD induces adaptive immunity against dead cells (usually apoptotic cells), and thus transforms the “cold” TME lacking immune response into a “hot” TME with immune activity. The successful execution of an ICD-driven adaptive immune response relies on the co-existence of antigenicity, adjuvanticity, and appropriate microenvironment conditions [ ]. The factors that cause ICD include chemotherapeutic drugs (DOX, cyclophosphamide, oxaliplatin, etc.), targeted anti-cancer drugs (cetuximab, crizotinib (Cro), cyclin-dependent kinase 4/6 inhibitors, etc.), oncolytic viruses, physical therapies (such as in vitro photodynamic therapy (PDT), and irradiation therapy) [ ], obligate intracellular pathogens (such as measles virus [ ]), and epigenetic modifiers (bromodomain inhibitors [ ], and histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitors, and DNA methyltransferase inhibitors [ ]).
Various targeted nano-drug delivery systems have been designed to strengthen cancer immunotherapy by stimulating ICD. The combination of small-molecule chemotherapeutic agents and immunotherapy demonstrates significant potential in suppressing breast cancer growth and remodeling the iTME by eliciting ICD to activate robust antitumor immunity. However, suboptimal drug bioavailability substantially limits therapeutic efficacy. Specifically, a DOX-based MAN nanogel has been developed for tumor-specific co-delivery of DOX for breast cancer treatment. Delivered DOX can induce a potent antitumor immune response by promoting ICD. Moreover, MAN released from nanogel has been shown to disrupt glycolysis and glucose metabolism to synergistically inhibit tumor growth. The nanogel preferentially accumulates at tumor sites via the EPR effect. It demonstrates high DOX encapsulation efficiency and favorable short-term safety profiles. In TNBC models, these nanogels exhibit potent therapeutic efficacy. And their MAN-mediated glycolytic interference mechanism suggests broad applicability to various tumor types with high glycolytic activity [ ].
Compared with conventional small-molecule chemotherapeutic agents, photoimmunotherapy (PIT) delivers precise tumor targeting with minimized collateral damage to healthy tissues and reduced adverse effects. Owing to the distinctive anatomical features of cervical cancer, this approach emerges as a promising next-generation clinical strategy. The synergistic combination of PDT and PTT, referred to as dual phototherapy, offers enhanced therapeutic benefits. A novel nanoaggregate with an acceptor-donor-acceptor (A-D-A) architecture has been constructed based on these rationales. Firstly, an A-D-A structured molecule, designated as DTPC-N2F, was designed and synthesized. To enhance its bioavailability, DTPC-N2F encapsulated DSPE-PEG5000 NPs (DNPs) were prepared via the nanoprecipitation method. Subsequently, these DNPs were coated with CM to form DNPs@CM. These nanoaggregates exhibit NIR responsiveness upon 980 nm laser excitation. Characterized by robust intramolecular charge transfer (ICT), the nanoaggregates display broad NIR absorption alongside fluorescence quenching, achieving both deep tissue penetration capability and a photothermal conversion efficiency. The optimized electronic structure facilitates efficient intersystem crossing (ISC) and enables multichannel ROS generation through distinct pathways: ground-state oxygen (3O2) conversion to singlet oxygen (1O2) and superoxide anions (O2•−), and catalytic hydroxyl radical (•OH) production. This dual enhancement of ICT and ISC confers potent combined PTT and PDT performance, ultimately inducing ICD in cervical cancer systems. The nanoaggregate demonstrates enhanced tumor-targeting capability through homologous cancer cell membrane coating. And its favorable hydration diameter and surface-negative charge promote EPR effect-mediated tumor accumulation. However, this study did not explicitly characterize drug loading capacity or release kinetics, which are critical for clinical translation [ ].
It has been demonstrated that oncolytic viruses can trigger ICD in tumor cells. Influenza virus (IAV) stands out among candidate oncolytic viruses due to three key attributes. First, it possesses strong immunostimulatory and immunomodulatory properties. Second, its genome does not integrate into host DNA, thereby precluding genotoxic risks. Third, IAV can be attenuated through established methods, while its oncolytic potency and immunomodulatory capacity are further enhanced via reverse genetic engineering techniques. A novel tumor nano-vaccine has been designed by coating PLGA NPs encapsulated MHC II epitope M30s with IAV-induced ICD membranes for melanoma treatment. The ICD membrane-modified nano-vaccine enhances APC recognition and uptake through surface-exposed calreticulin (CRT) and hemeagglutinin molecules. This nano-vaccine demonstrates exceptionally high encapsulation efficiency for M30s, combining with the multi-antigenic properties of ICD membranes to achieve potent antigen delivery. The US FDA-approved biodegradable PLGA material exhibits no significant organ toxicity in vivo. In melanoma models, this nano-vaccine induces robust ICD and demonstrates remarkable antitumor efficacy. However, in cervical cancer models, it primarily elicits NK cell-mediated nonspecific immunity, suggesting antigenic heterogeneity may limit its broad-spectrum applicability [ ].

6.2.

## Targeting CTLs
T lymphocytes play a pivotal role in mediating antitumor immunity, with CTLs serving as the central effectors capable of eliminating cancer cells in an antigen-specific manner. However, melanoma typically establishes an iTME that induces CTL exhaustion or apoptosis, thereby subverting antitumor immune responses. Calcium signaling is crucial for T lymphocyte activation, yet modulating calcium levels to control T cell activation in vivo remains challenging. To overcome these limitations, an innovative anti-PD-1 antibody coupled calcium carbonate (CaCO3) NPs have been designed for PD-1 directed endocytic delivery to CTLs. This nanoplatform potentiates calcium signaling cascades, simultaneously activating nuclear factor of an activated T cell (NFAT) and nuclear factor kappaB (NF-κB) pathways to reinvigorate CTL mediated tumoricidal activity. These CaCO3 NPs undergo receptor-mediated endocytosis into T cells, followed by gradual calcium release. The resulting intracellular calcium elevation triggers activation of NFAT and NF-κB pathways, particularly when phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) is surface-conjugated to the NPs. Animal studies demonstrate that PMA-loaded CaCO3 NPs enhance CTLs activation and proliferation, leading to improved tumor suppression without additional toxicity. While these CaCO3 NPs demonstrate superior drug-loading capacity and biocompatibility compared to traditional calcium ionophores, repeated long-term administration may pose risks of calcium homeostasis disruption. Additionally, their targeting efficiency depends on PD-1 expression levels, therefore the heterogeneous PD-1 expression among tumor-infiltrating T cells (e.g., low expression in naive T cells) could limit activation efficacy [ ].
STING agonists activate the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS)-STING pathway to stimulate both innate and adaptive antitumor immunity, with demonstrated therapeutic potential in preclinical and clinical studies. Although current delivery systems have significantly improved tumor targeting and T cell response to STING agonists, TNBC cells still evade immunity through multiple mechanisms. Research indicates that CTL-mediated cytotoxicity requires direct contact with cancer cells. During this interaction, mechanical force applied through immune synapses enhances perforin function, which is impaired by membrane cholesterol enrichment. In this regard, a novel redox-responsive methyl-β-cyclodextrin (MeβCD)-based supramolecular polyrotaxanes (MSPs) nanoplatform has been constructed based on the cholesterol-depleting properties of MeβCD. The amphiphilic diselenium-bridged axle polymer was used to encapsulate MeβCD through host-guest interactions, with two NIR fluorescent probes IR783 serving as terminal capping agents. The MSP and STING agonist dimeric aminobenzimidazoles (diABZIs) self-assembled into NPs, enabling co-release of both MeβCD and diABZIs in the redox TME. Upon STING activation in APCs by released diABZI, new T cell response is initiated. Concurrently, liberated MeβCD depletes membrane cholesterol to overcome cancer cell mechanical softness, thereby enhancing CTL-mediated cytotoxicity. This nanoplatform induces potent tumor regression and establishes long-term immune memory. The NPs preferentially accumulate at tumor sites via the EPR effect, and their biodistribution is trackable through the NIR probe IR783. However, the low drug-loading efficiency of diABZI may necessitate repeated administration to sustain therapeutic efficacy. Although the nanoplatform demonstrates favorable short-term safety, comprehensive assessment of long-term toxicity is required [ ].

6.3.

## Targeting Treg cells
The enrichment of Treg cells and low immunogenicity in the breast cancer microenvironment limit the efficacy of chemotherapy and immunotherapy. Moreover, free DOX and Cro fail to achieve effective concentrations at tumor sites due to rapid clearance and off-target toxicity, necessitating nanocarriers for precise co-delivery. Building upon these limitations, researchers have developed a delivery device to improve the antitumor activity of DOX. This device employed Cro polymerized prodrug carriers poly-(oligo ethylene glycol) (POEG)-b-PCro micelles, which served as Cro polymerized prodrug carriers for DOX. POEG-b-PCro micelles passively accumulate in tumor cells via the EPR effect following intravenous administration and are internalized by tumor cells. Subsequently, these micelles release DOX and cleaved Cro, and indirectly suppress Treg cell populations by augmenting tumor-specific CTL activity, thereby enhancing the overall antitumor effect. Despite enhanced accumulation, the absence of active targeting ligands limits penetration into hypovascular or high interstitial pressure regions, potentially failing to cover specific tumor subtypes. While POEG hydrophilic chains prolong circulation and reduce systemic toxicity, long-term safety requires further validation. This refinement in nano-drug delivery complements the existing strategies [ ].
Colorectal cancer (CRC) exhibits poor response to ICBs due to low immune cell infiltration, elevated immunosuppressive cells (such as Treg cells), and insufficient PD-L1 expression. Notably, CRC cells overexpress cyclin-dependent kinase 4 and 6 (CDK4/6), which critically regulate G1 to S phase transition. Leveraging these findings, the sequential responsive nano-proteolysis targeting chimeras (PROTACs) have been designed for precise intracellular delivery and enhanced degradation efficacy in CRC therapy. This sequential responsive nano-PROTACs enable precise PROTAC delivery via a pH/cathepsin B sequential response mechanism. During circulation, NPs accumulate in tumors via the EPR effect. In the acidic TME, they dissociate into monomers, facilitating penetration and cellular internalization. Intracellular lysosomal cathepsin B cleaves the Gly-Phe-Leu-Gly (GFLG) linker, releasing PROTACs to degrade CDK4/6. CDK4/6 degradation upregulates tumor PD-L1 expression and suppresses Treg cells proliferation, thereby remodeling the iTME and potentiating ICBs efficacy. Although its drug-loading capacity is moderate, enzyme-controlled release improves drug utilization efficiency. However, intratumoral heterogeneity in CRC (such as uneven metabolite distribution) may compromise pH-responsive activation. Additionally, enzyme-triggered drug release could induce localized lysosomal stress, necessitating further evaluation of long-term safety [ ].

## Targeted nano-drug delivery systems for regulating tumor metabolites
7.1.

## Targeting lactate
Elevated lactate levels in the TME not only promote tumor progression and metastasis but also induce immune evasion, frequently contributing to the failure of various therapeutic strategies. This underscores the therapeutic potential of lactate-targeted delivery approaches. Based on this, a semiconducting polymer nanoreactor has been developed to achieve synergistic immunotherapy through sono-activated cascade reactions that simultaneously regulate lactate metabolism and induce cuproptosis. The nanoreactor comprises a semiconducting polymer (sonosensitizer), LOX conjugated via a ROS-cleavable linker, and chelated cupric ion (Cu2+). Under ultrasound irradiation, the polymer generates singlet oxygen (1O2), which cleaves the linker to release LOX. The LOX then catalyzes lactate to pyruvate conversion, depleting TME lactate while generating H2O2. Simultaneously, Cu2+ in the TME undergoes reduction to cuprous ion (Cu+), which reacts with H2O2 via a Fenton-like reaction to generate hydroxyl radicals (•OH). These radicals further promote LOX release, establishing a self-amplifying cascade for sustained lactate depletion. The resultant lactate exhaustion alleviates the iTME (e.g., by reversing T cell dysfunction), while Cu+-induced cuproptosis triggers ICD, characterized by release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) including adenosine triphosphate (ATP), high mobility group box-1 protein (HMGB1), and CRT. These signals collectively stimulate DCs maturation and enhance CD8+ T cell infiltration, ultimately synergizing to potentiate antitumor immunity of both subcutaneous and deep-tissue orthotopic pancreatic cancer. The nanoreactor achieves tumor accumulation via the EPR effect, while ultrasound spatiotemporal control enables specific LOX release. The co-delivery design of LOX and Cu2+ enhances catalytic efficiency through cascade reactions. Experimental results demonstrate favorable blood compatibility, though long-term safety requires further evaluation. This system is specifically engineered for pancreatic cancer's highly immunosuppressive and lactate-rich TME. However, tumor heterogeneity may impact ultrasound penetration efficiency and uniform LOX/Cu2+ release. Collectively, this study presents a precisely controlled lactate depleting strategy for effective tumor immunotherapy [ ].
Although lactate depletion represents a promising therapeutic strategy, its downstream metabolite pyruvate can fuel cancer cell survival via the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle to sustain energy production. This metabolic bypass compromises the efficacy of lactate homeostasis reprogramming and undermines antitumor outcomes. To address this limitation, inhibition of pyruvate entry into mitochondrial respiration, with specific focus on preventing its integration into the TCA cycle, represents an attractive therapeutic strategy. A nanocatalytic medicine based on carbon-doping copper nitride enzyme (Cu3N–C NE) with enhanced LOX activity has been constructed. The Cu3N–C NE exhibits augmented LOX activity that effectively catalyzes lactate oxidation to pyruvate and remarkably reduces lactate level in the TME. This metabolic intervention reprograms the iTME, as evidenced by increased M1 macrophage polarization, suppressed M2 phenotype, and enhanced CD8+ T cell infiltration alongside DCs maturation. Cu blocks pyruvate entry into the TCA cycle and reduces ATP production by inhibiting the pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) complex. This triggers mitochondrial membrane potential collapse and TNBC cell apoptosis. Ultrasound stimulation further enhances LOX activity, initiating a cascade reaction that generates H2O2-derived ROS for synergistic therapeutic efficacy. While this nanocatalytic medicine achieves spatiotemporal specificity through EPR-dependent targeting and ultrasound regulation, tumor heterogeneity may compromise penetration and catalytic uniformity. Although no significant hematologic toxicity was observed, further evaluation of copper metabolism and potential long-term accumulation remains to be investigated [ ].

7.2.

## Targeting glutamine
Proliferating cancer cells in the TME are highly dependent on glutamine. As a primary source of both energy and biosynthetic precursors, glutamine is critical for tumor metabolism. Consequently, targeting glutamine metabolism, particularly GLS, has emerged as a promising strategy to expand the therapeutic window. 6-Diazo-5-oxo-l-norleucine (DON), a glutamine antagonist, inhibits tumor cell proliferation while promoting T-cell activation and expansion to enhance immunotherapy. However, its poor tissue distribution and systemic toxicity in normal organs limit clinical translation. Sonodynamic therapy (SDT), featuring non-invasiveness, high specificity, and deep-tissue penetration, is ideal for treating deep-seated tumors. To overcome these limitations, a novel titanium dioxide (TiO2)-Aurum (Au)@DON nano-composite was developed through conjugation of the metabolic reprogramming prodrug DON onto TiO2–Au Janus NPs. This sono-metabolic therapeutic approach demonstrates synergistic enhancement of antitumor immunotherapy efficacy in TNBC. The TiO2–Au Janus NPs serve as sonosensitizers that generate both type I and II ROS under ultrasound stimulation, leading to ICD induction and DAMPs release. This process promotes DCs maturation and CD8+ T cell infiltration. Concurrently, through inhibiting GLS, DON reduces nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate hydrogen (NADPH) and glutathione (GSH) production to disrupt tumor redox homeostasis and amplify ROS-mediated cytotoxicity. Furthermore, DON enhances T cell activation and memory T cell generation, thereby remodeling the iTME. Notably, a positive feedback loop emerges between ROS generation and DON-mediated metabolic modulation: DON depletes GSH to potentiate SDT efficacy, while SDT-induced ICD activates systemic antitumor immunity. The nano-composite demonstrates efficient tumor accumulation via passive targeting, achieving high drug loading capacity through covalent conjugation of the DON prodrug. The system enables tumor-specific drug release through disulfide bond cleavage in response to TME enzymes (esterase and proteinase K). Histopathological analysis revealed no significant damage in major organs, though notable hepatic accumulation was observed, potentially posing long-term metabolic concerns [ ].
Cholangiocarcinoma (CCA), arising from biliary epithelial cells, exhibits marked heterogeneity, posing a substantial therapeutic challenge. While targeted delivery of glutamine metabolism inhibitors offers promise, effective carriers remain elusive. PDT has emerged as a promising intervention for CCA due to its tunable light-induced damage, non-invasiveness, and minimal side effects. To address this conundrum, a biomimetic nanosystem has been developed by integrating mesoporous organosilica NPs (MSNs) with ROS-responsive diselenide bonds. This nanosystem enables controlled release of the glutamine metabolism inhibitor BPTES and the photosensitizer Ce6. Light-triggered ROS generation during PDT cleaves diselenide bonds, inducing MSNs framework disintegration and complete release of Ce6 and BPTES. This nanosystem suppresses GLS1 activity to reduce M2-type and CX3CR1+ macrophages infiltration as well as promote M1 polarization, thereby reversing the iTME. Concurrent ROS overproduction enhances CCA immunogenicity through tumor cell necroptosis. Surface modification with Arg-Gly-Asp (RGD) functionalized erythrocyte membranes improves biocompatibility, targeting efficiency (via integrin αvβ3 interaction), and 3D tumor spheroids penetration. While erythrocyte coating mitigates systemic toxicity, potential immunogenicity of allogeneic membranes requires further evaluation. Notably, heterogeneous expression of GLS1 and integrin αvβ3 in CCA patients may affect therapeutic consistency. Collectively, this strategy presents a promising approach for CCA treatment [ ].

7.3.

## Targeting tryptophan
Tryptophan is an amino acid that boosts CD8+ T cell activity. However, the TME often exhibits high expression of IDO1, which catalyzes tryptophan metabolism to deplete tryptophan and generate kynurenine, a metabolite that suppresses T cell function. Thus, simultaneously inhibiting IDO1 and increasing tryptophan availability can enhance T cell-mediated antitumor immunity. Conventional IDO1 inhibitors show off-target risks. To overcome this limitation, a genetically engineered high tryptophan-expressing L-Trp CB strain has been developed. This engineered bacterial strain demonstrates tumor-colonizing capability after systemic administration. L-Trp CB is a genetically engineered CB strain that overexpresses the tryptophan biosynthesis operon (trpEDCBA), enabling sustained tryptophan secretion and butyrate production. The released butyrate downregulates signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 (STAT1) and inhibits HDAC activity, thereby suppressing IDO expression in tumor cells and preventing tryptophan conversion into immunosuppressive kynurenine to alleviate TME immunosuppression. Meanwhile, secreted tryptophan enhances glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation in CD8+ T cells by directly activating the mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) pathway, leading to increased effector T cell proliferation, cytotoxicity, and memory differentiation. Furthermore, L-Trp CB increases tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells and reduces Treg cells through directly reprogramming the metabolism of CD8+ T cells. As an anaerobic bacterium, intravenously administered CB specifically colonizes hypoxic tumor tissues. Bioluminescence imaging confirms exclusive tumor localization, though intratumoral hypoxia heterogeneity may affect bacterial distribution uniformity. The engineered strain constitutively expresses tryptophan synthase via a strong promoter to achieve significantly elevated tryptophan secretion without compromising butyrate production, thus enabling dual delivery of both metabolites. While CB demonstrates inherent safety as a probiotic, its limited intratumoral survival duration (∼7 days) may constrain sustained therapeutic efficacy. Collectively, this synthetic biology approach potentiates tumor immunotherapy in TNBC models through dual mechanisms of IDO suppression and CD8+ T cell metabolic reprogramming [ ].
Compared to small-molecule inhibitors, PROTACs exhibit superior protein degradation efficiency and recyclable activity. However, their always-on bioactivity induced off-target effects pose daunting systemic toxicity risks, limiting clinical applications. Therefore, strategies that selectively deliver and activate PROTACs in tumor tissues for iTME reprogramming could overcome fundamental limitations of current immunotherapies. Based on this rationale, a light-triggered PROTAC nanoassembly (LPN) has been devised for photodynamic proteolysis of IDO. The LPN is self-assembled from a prodrug containing PROTAC, a cathepsin B (Cat-B)-specific cleavable peptide linker, and a photosensitizer, without additional carrier materials. Upon light irradiation, the photosensitizer generates ROS and induces tumor cells to release tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) and DAMPs, thereby promoting DCs maturation and effector T cell activation. Additionally, Cat-B triggers PROTAC release to degrade IDO via the ubiquitin-proteasome system, resulting in reduced tryptophan metabolism into kynurenine. This process reverses DC dysfunction, T cell inactivation, and the immunosuppressive effects of Treg cells. The LPN passively targets tumors via the EPR effect, and the Cat-B peptide ensures tumor-specific PROTAC activation, minimizing off-target effects. Its self-assembled architecture achieves superior drug loading compared to conventional nanocarriers. However, potential phototoxicity from residual photosensitizers warrants consideration. Collectively, this LPN-mediated combinatorial photodynamic IDO proteolysis strategy effectively suppresses tumor growth, metastasis, and recurrence in colon cancer [ ].

## Targeted nano-drug delivery systems for co-delivery
To address the complexity of tumor immunotherapy, combination therapeutic strategies leveraging nano-delivery systems have emerged as a powerful approach. These systems enable spatiotemporally controlled co-delivery of diverse therapeutic agents. For instance, a representative multifunctional nanoplatform has been developed by co-encapsulating ultrasmall phototherapeutic agent black phosphorus quantum dot (BPQD), chemotherapeutic paclitaxel (PTX), and immunomodulator PolyMetformin (PM) within hybrid membrane (cancer cell membrane and red blood cell membrane)-camouflaged liposomes for precise breast tumor targeting. The chemotherapeutic agent PTX directly kills tumor cells, while BPQDs induce ICD through photothermal/photodynamic effects, releasing DAMPs (CRT, HMGB1 and ATP) to promote DCs maturation and T cell activation. PM modulates tumor metabolism via adenosine 5′-monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK) pathway and regulates macrophage polarization. This triple combination synergistically remodels the iTME, with further enhanced efficacy when combined with anti-PD-L1 to reverse T cell exhaustion and sustain antitumor immunity. The hybrid membrane coating confers NPs with prolonged circulation and enhanced tumor-specific accumulation. While this platform enables co-delivery of multiple therapeutics, the complex structure of the hybrid membrane may limit optimal drug loading. However, although no significant organ toxicity was observed in vivo, long-term immunogenicity and chronic toxicity require further investigation [ ].
Similarly, a feasible combined chemoimmunotherapy strategy was developed by crosslinking carboxymethyl chitosan (CMCS)-derived polymetformin (PolyMetCMCS) with cystamine, yielding stimuli-responsive PolyMetCMCS nanogels (PMNGs). The disulfide bonds in cystamine enable PMNGs to respond to TME, as they are cleavable by the abundant GSH present in tumor tissues. The chemotherapeutic agent DOX was then loaded into PMNGs, followed by hyaluronic acid (HA) coating to enhance the overall biocompatibility and targeting capability of the nanogels. Owing to metformin's immunomodulatory properties, PMNGs effectively remodel the tumor immune microenvironment by reprogramming TAMs phenotypes, promoting DCs maturation, and recruiting intratumoral CD8+ T cells. The loaded DOX directly kills tumor cells, synergizing with immunomodulation to suppress postoperative melanoma recurrence. This strategy offers a promising solution to combat lethal melanoma relapse. The HA coating significantly enhances tumor accumulation through CD44 receptor-mediated active targeting. Notably, compared to other nanocarriers, this nanogel system demonstrates superior mobility, softness, deformability and hydrophilicity, facilitating efficient cellular uptake and deep tumor penetration. While the HA coating improves overall biocompatibility, potential immunomodulatory effects of PolyMetCMCS during chronic use require further investigation to assess long-term safety [ ].

## Targeted nano-drug delivery systems in regulating other immunomodulatory factors
In addition to the above approaches, new strategies are emerging, such as targeting peripheral immune organs (such as lymph nodes) and modifying the iTME.
Targeting peripheral immune organs has yielded promising results. The biopharmaceutical properties of small-molecule immunomodulators often result in poor lymphatic absorption. Consequently, direct lymph node delivery of these immunomodulatory drugs may offer significant opportunities to enhance bioavailability and reduce systemic side effects. The mesoporous polydopamine (MPDA) NPs polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP)-MPDA@R837, which are capable of delivering TLR7 agonist R837, profoundly enhance lymphatic drainage and effectively inhibit melanoma growth. The mesoporous structure of MPDA enables high-efficiency R837 loading, providing a drug reservoir for sustained immune activation. Moreover, the inherent biocompatibility of MPDA, combined with polydopamine (PVP)'s hydrophilic modification, significantly reduces cytotoxicity. This dual modification approach simultaneously minimizes nonspecific immune clearance and prolongs lymph node retention. Compared to free systemic diffusion of R837, nanocarrier-mediated localized drug release markedly enhances DCs antigen presentation capacity and activates CD8+ T cells. Furthermore, the photothermal effect of MPDA induces tumor cell apoptosis, releasing endogenous antigens. These released antigens synergize with R837 to potently enhance DCs cross-presentation, thereby establishing robust tumor-specific immune responses. The varying degrees of immune suppression across tumor regions may limit the universal applicability of PVP-MPDA@R837. Future designs are suggested to incorporate ICIs or dynamically modifiable surface ligands to better adapt to the heterogeneous TME. [ ]. This strategy highlights the potential of harnessing the immune system's own mechanisms to combat cancer.
The ECM barriers have also been addressed with innovative solutions. Traditional antiangiogenic therapy inhibits tumor growth by blocking tumor angiogenesis, but its efficacy is limited due to compensatory mechanism activation and pro-metastatic risks. As an alternative strategy, vascular normalization therapy optimizes the TME by pruning redundant vasculature, remodeling abnormal vessels, and improving vascular integrity, thereby enhancing the effectiveness of other tumor immunotherapies. Building upon the vascular normalization theory, a nano-drug delivery system, comprising vandetanib encapsulated within low-density lipoprotein (LDL) NPs (V@LDL NPs), was developed for the delivery of the VEGF receptor 2 (VEGFR2) inhibitor vandetanib. This system can reverse abnormalities such as hypoxia in the TME and substantially inhibit TNBC progression by improving tumor perfusion. Sustained VEGFR2 inhibition by controlled vandetanib release reduces pathological angiogenesis while enhancing vascular integrity. This vascular normalization improves oxygen perfusion and promotes CD8+ T cell infiltration to reverse the iTME. LDL NPs preferentially accumulate in tumor vasculature through EPR effect-dependent targeting. Their endogenous composition minimizes immune clearance risks [ ]. This biomimetic platform combines prolonged vascular normalization with excellent biocompatibility, making it particularly suitable for solid tumors with pronounced vascular abnormalities.
CAF-mediated fibrosis (physical barrier) and IDO1/kynurenine axis (biochemical barrier) cooperatively establish an iTME of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC). To address the dynamic immune evasion of PDAC, a peptide-drug conjugate (PDC)-based self-adaptive nanoregulator has been developed. This smart nanoregulator exhibits dynamic biphasic morphological transitions, initially transforming from spherical micelles into nanofibers, followed by subsequent reversion to spherical NPs. This nanostructure design enables spatially programmed delivery of therapeutic agents. CAF inhibitor (CAFi) is preferentially distributed to the ECM to disrupt tumor fibrosis. While IDO1 inhibitor (IDOi) is selectively targeted to tumor cells to alleviate IDO1/kynurenine axis-mediated T-cell exhaustion. The self-assembled micellar NPs exhibit fibroblast activation protein-alpha (FAP-α) recognition capability, triggering in situ morphological transformation from spherical micelles to nanofibers through poly(ethylene glycol) (PEG) shell shedding. This transformation enhances intratumoral retention of CAFi and alleviates CAF-mediated immune resistance. Under 671 nm laser irradiation, the photosensitizer integrated within the nanoregulator generated ROS, inducing a second-stage nanofiber to NPs transition through methionine oxidation in the peptide. This structural transformation enhanced tumor cell internalization of IDO1i, which was subsequently released in the reductive TME to disrupt the IDO1/kynurenine metabolic axis and prevent CD8+ T cell exhaustion. This nanoregulator enables dual targeting of the ECM and tumor cells. However, its efficacy relies on FAP-α expression levels, potentially limiting coverage across all CAF subtypes or tumor regions with low FAP-α. While PEGylation prolongs circulation time, long-term safety requires further validation. Overall, this PDC-based self-adaptive nanoregulator may offer a novel strategy to enhance immunotherapy for PDAC [ ].
In recent years, remarkable progress has been achieved in developing targeted nano-drug delivery systems, especially in exploring novel nanocarriers and loaded drugs. It has been reported for the first time that the antihypertensive drug, α2-adrenergic receptor (α2-AR) agonist, could promote the infiltration of T lymphocytes and macrophages to induce potent antitumor immune responses [ ]. Endogenous lymph node-targeted lipid NPs (LNP 113-O12B) are another breakthrough, which can efficiently deliver full-length proteins and peptide-based antigen-encoded mRNAs. LNP 113-O12B shows great potential as a universal platform for the next generation of mRNA vaccines [ ], highlighting the importance of targeted delivery in vaccine development. Finally, the self-assembling lipid nanodisks (LNDs) exhibit substantial potential for the delivery of immune drugs to solid tumors due to their robust tumor penetration and tumor cell uptake capabilities [ ]. These studies open up new directions for the design and application of targeted nano-drug delivery systems in the future.

10.

## Challenges and future directions
While targeted nano-drug delivery systems present great promise for tumor immunotherapy, pivotal challenges in safety, efficacy, and clinical translation must be addressed. Continued advancements in these areas may unlock the full potential of nanotherapeutics for clinical applications.
10.1.

## The limitations of targeted nano-drug delivery systems
While NP-based delivery systems show considerable potential in cancer immunotherapy, three critical limitations hinder their application: (1) systemic toxicity from unintended biodistribution and immune activation, (2) adverse effects on vascular and hemostatic systems, and (3) suboptimal therapeutic efficacy despite promising preclinical data. This section systematically examines these limitations and discusses emerging mitigation strategies.
10.1.1.

## Systemic toxicity
Despite significant advancements in nano-drug delivery systems in cancer immunotherapy, critical challenges persist, particularly systemic toxicity. The nanoscale dimensions of these carriers enable traversal of physiological barriers, yet concurrently elevate risks of unintended biodistribution and oxidative damage to cellular components, including genomic DNA, membranes, and organelles. Furthermore, intracellular delivery may provoke aberrant immune activation via interactions with pattern recognition receptors [ ].
Biodegradable lipid derivatives represent a promising strategy to mitigate risks associated with lipid-based NPs, demonstrating rapid plasma clearance and enhanced preclinical safety profiles. Surface engineering approaches, applicable to polymeric, lipid-based, and inorganic NPs, enable dynamic surface modification through non-covalent coatings that modulate NPs dispersion states and toxicity. For inorganic NPs, doping of NPs reduces toxic ions leaching by stabilizing reactive surfaces and suppressing ROS production through structural modification [ ].

10.1.2.

## Adverse side effects
Nano-drug delivery systems can induce diverse adverse side effects. These effects include vascular endothelial dysfunction, where NPs such as titanium dioxide disrupt intercellular junctions to increase vascular permeability and potentially facilitate metastatic dissemination. Hemostatic imbalances arise from the interaction of NPs with blood components. For example, silver NPs inhibit platelet adhesion by altering integrin conformation. Cationic dendrimers promote fibrinogen aggregation, leading to procoagulant activity. NPs also perturb microbial ecosystems, which may indirectly influence host immunity and metabolic homeostasis. Complement system activation, particularly via the alternative pathway, poses risks of hypersensitivity reactions [ ]. These findings emphasize the necessity of comprehensive preclinical assessments to delineate nanomaterial-host interactions and mitigate unintended adverse side effects.
To mitigate the side adverse effects of nano-drug delivery systems, interdisciplinary strategies emphasize rational design and biological interaction optimization. Biodegradable polymers and sub-10 nm ultra-small NPs minimize biopersistence through controlled hydrolysis and enhanced renal clearance, respectively [ , ]. In addition, biomimetic strategies utilizing cell membrane coatings or endogenous materials enhance biocompatibility [ ]. These integrated innovations collectively advance the development of safer nano-drug delivery systems with balanced therapeutic efficacy and biosafety profiles.

10.1.3.

## Suboptimal therapeutic response rates
Clinical trials demonstrate > 90% success rates in phase I trials, reflecting favorable safety of cancer nanomedicines. While phase II/III trials reveal > 80% failure rates due to insufficient therapeutic efficacy [ ]. The suboptimal therapeutic response rates of nano-drug delivery systems arise from multifaceted challenges, such as preclinical to clinical translation and biological interactions. Key limitations include inadequate predictive validity of preclinical models that fail to recapitulate tumor heterogeneity [ ]. Pharmacokinetic unpredictability stems from premature drug release, biomolecular corona formation altering targeting specificity, and inconsistent drug release kinetics between in vitro and in vivo conditions [ ]. Manufacturing complexities introduce batch-to-batch variability in critical quality attributes (size and drug loading, etc.) [ ]. However, insufficient patient stratification overlooks biological determinants of response [ ]. Furthermore, overly sophisticated nanocarrier designs compromise manufacturability without commensurate therapeutic gains, and regulatory challenges emerge from ambiguous classification of nanotherapeutics [ ]. These translational gaps collectively contribute to the discordance between promising preclinical results and clinical outcomes.
To address these challenges, approaches, such as developing clinically relevant disease models (organoids and organs-on-chips, etc.), have been developed to better recapitulate human pathophysiology [ ]. Dynamic characterization of biomolecular corona formation and drug release kinetics should be prioritized to improve pharmacokinetic predictability [ ]. Implementation of quality by design (QbD) principles can enhance manufacturing consistency and product quality [ ]. Companion diagnostics enables precise patient stratification based on biological determinants of response. Furthermore, early engagement with regulatory agencies facilitates optimized clinical trial design and clearer regulatory pathways for nanotherapeutics [ ]. These multifaceted strategies collectively aim to bridge the translational gap between preclinical and clinical efficacy.

10.2.

## Challenges of targeted nano-drug delivery systems in clinical application
The clinical translation of targeted nano-drug delivery systems faces three major challenges. First, PEG immunogenicity compromises therapeutic safety. Second, tumor heterogeneity driven by cancer stem cells (CSCs) fosters therapeutic resistance. Third, limited reliability of cancer animal models fails to recapitulate human tumor biology. Addressing these challenges requires interdisciplinary innovations to align preclinical expectations with clinical realities.
10.2.1.

## PEG immunogenicity
PEGylation has been a cornerstone of nanomedicine for over 25 years, exemplified by the clinical approval of PEGylated liposomal DOX (Doxil™). However, recent evidence highlights its association with immunological adverse events, leading to the discontinuation of certain PEGylated therapeutics. Notably, fatal infusion-related responses linked to PEGylation prompted the withdrawal of PEGinesatide (a functional analog of erythropoietin) from clinical use [ ]. Alternative approaches to evade immunogenicity include functionalizing nanomaterials with “don't eat me” proteins/peptides [ ] or employing biomimetic coatings strategies, such as NP camouflage using cell-derived membranes [ ].

10.2.2.

## Tumor heterogeneity
Tumor heterogeneity poses a formidable clinical obstacle by driving therapeutic resistance and malignant progression. CSCs, characterized by self-renewal capacity, tumorigenicity potential, and intrinsic therapy resistance, constitute a key cellular driver of this heterogeneity. CSC persistence is implicated in tumor recurrence, metastatic dissemination, and immunotherapy failure through mechanisms including immune evasion and microenvironment remodeling. Consequently, CSCs are an important challenge in the field of tumor immunotherapy [ ].
Differentiation therapy employs pharmacological agents to promote malignant cells differentiation toward mature phenotypes, thereby reducing stem-like state. However, tumor cell dedifferentiation enables dynamic re-emergence of CSCs from non-CSCs tumor cell populations. This bidirectional plasticity necessitates combined therapeutic regimens integrating differentiation inducers with chemotherapy agents to simultaneously target bulk tumor cells and CSCs, effectively addressing tumor heterogeneity at its source [ ]. Dual-drug sequential therapy (DDST) achieves synergistic therapeutic efficacy through coordinated use of a differentiation-inducing agent to reprogram CSCs into non-CSCs and a chemotherapy agent targeting non-CSCs. The success of this approach relies on precisely engineered temporal release kinetics, which sequentially disrupt CSC stemness before eliminating non-CSCs through hierarchical targeting mechanisms [ ]. Furthermore,a computational technique has been developed to map NP spatial distribution across heterogeneous tumor regions, achieving > 88% accuracy in predicting intratumoral NP localization. This proof-of-concept study demonstrates that tumor heterogeneity can be computationally characterized through biophysical structural patterns, enabling precise identification of NP accumulation. Such predictive model offers a promising strategy for engineering personalized nanocarriers to overcome heterogeneity-driven therapeutic resistance [ ].

10.2.3.

## Limited reliability and validity of cancer animal models
Animal models exhibit limited capacity to replicate critical features of human malignancy, including genetic mutations, uncontrolled proliferation, and metastatic spread. Such limitations significantly contribute to the translational gap between preclinical drug efficacy and clinical outcomes. For example, immune-deficient models cannot predict immune-related adverse effects in immunocompetent patients. Additionally, implanted tumors in animal models often exhibit substantial size discrepancies compared to spontaneous human tumors, further reducing their predictive value [ ]. Spontaneous tumor models and humanized PDX mouse models provide a more physiologically relevant platform to assess immune-related toxicities and therapeutic efficacy of nanotherapeutics [ ].

11.

## Conclusion
During the past decades, the application of nanomedicine delivery systems in immunotherapy has achieved remarkable progress. However, current research also faces many challenges, including the biocompatibility and safety of nanocarriers [ ], the complexity of immune escape mechanisms, and the optimization of delivery efficiency [ ]. To overcome these challenges, future investigations are urgently required to deepen the immune escape mechanisms, as well as optimize carrier design and drug-loading strategies to enhance therapeutic effects. In addition, the research on multimodal combination therapy also needs to be strengthened to fully exploit the potential of targeted nano-drug delivery systems. The close integration of preclinical and clinical trials will be key for accelerating the transformation and application of targeted nano-drug delivery systems in tumor immunotherapy.
Meanwhile, the intelligent design of targeted nano-drug delivery systems and the formulation of individualized treatment strategies will also become new research hotspots. Intelligent design is expected to achieve more precise drug delivery and release, while individualized treatment strategies can tailor the best treatment plan based on the patient's specific conditions. Looking forward, with the continuous advancement of nanotechnology and in-depth mechanism research of immune evasion, targeted nano-drug delivery systems are expected to play a more pivotal role in tumor immunotherapy, providing cancer patients with more efficient and safer treatment options.
