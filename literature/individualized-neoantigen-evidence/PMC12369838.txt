## Abstract
Cancer represents a major worldwide disease burden marked by escalating incidence and mortality. While therapeutic advances persist, developing safer and precisely targeted modalities remains imperative. Nanomedicines emerges as a transformative paradigm leveraging distinctive physicochemical properties to achieve tumor-specific drug delivery, controlled release, and tumor microenvironment modulation. By synergizing passive enhanced permeation and retention effect-driven accumulation and active ligand-mediated targeting, nanoplatforms enhance pharmacokinetics, promote tumor microenvironment enrichment, and improve cellular internalization while mitigating systemic toxicity. Despite revolutionizing cancer therapy through enhanced treatment efficacy and reduced adverse effects, translational challenges persist in manufacturing scalability, longterm biosafety, and cost-efficiency. This review systematically analyzes cutting-edge nanoplatforms, including polymeric, lipidic, biomimetic, albumin-based, peptide engineered, DNA origami, and inorganic nanocarriers, while evaluating their strategic advantages and technical limitations across three therapeutic domains: immunotherapy, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy. By assessing structure-function correlations and clinical translation barriers, this work establishes mechanistic and translational references to advance oncological nanomedicine development.
Keywords: Nanomedicine, Drug delivery, Tumor microenvironment, Cancer therapy, Immunotherapy, Chemotherapy, Radiotherapy

## Introduction
Cancer is a significant global public health issue and the second leading cause of death worldwide. Data from the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) indicate a continuous rise in cancer incidence and mortality rates. In 2022, approximately 20 million new cancer cases and 9.7 million deaths were reported globally. Similarly, the China National Cancer Center (CNCC) reported 4.82 million new cancer cases and 2.57 million cancer-related deaths in the same year.[ , ] Projections suggest that the annual global incidence of cancer will reach 35 million by 2050, a 77% increase from 2022, driven by factors such as demographic shifts, environmental pollution, and lifestyle changes.[ ]
Traditional cancer treatments, including surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy, remain the cornerstone of therapeutic approaches but exhibit notable limitations. Surgical resection is primarily effective for early-stage cancers; however, it often leaves residual tumor cells, increasing the risk of recurrence and metastasis while potentially damaging surrounding healthy tissues. Chemotherapy, local or systemic administration of cytotoxic chemicals, is complained about toxic side effects due to poor drug selectivity that impair normal tissue and organ functions.[ ] Radiotherapy also lacks specificity for tumor cells and can cause resistance, secondary malignancies, or chronic damage to normal tissues, such as pulmonary fibrosis and cardiac injury, particularly in radiation-insensitive tumors.[ ] As a result, many patients achieve limited benefits from these conventional modalities and irreversible side effects.
Emerging treatments, such as targeted therapy and immunotherapy, have introduced new possibilities in the treatment of various cancer types. For example, in nonsmall cell lung cancer (NSCLC)—the leading cause of cancer incidence and mortality—epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) gene mutations are observed in a subset of patients with lung adenocarcinoma, making EGFR-targeting therapies applicable; however, many patients eventually experience treatment failure due to resistance mutations, such as T790M.[ ] Moreover, the absence of actionable gene mutations in many patients with lung squamous cell carcinoma limits the applicability of targeted therapies. Immunotherapy for NSCLC is also constrained by a low response rate, with only 20% of patients achieving significant benefits.[ ] Long-term use of these advanced therapies is further challenged by resistance and treatment hyper-progression, with no effective solutions currently available. Similar issues are encountered in other cancer types, underscoring the urgent need for innovative therapeutic strategies that are precise, effective, low in toxicity, and economically accessible to improve survival rates and reduce the socio-economic burden of cancer.[ ]
Nanomedicine presents a transformative paradigm in oncological intervention through engineered drug delivery systems with electrostatic, covalent, and supramolecular interactions.[ , ] Therapeutic nanoplatforms encompass polymeric, liposomal, bioinspired, albumin-conjugated, peptide/DNA-engineered, and inorganic variants, with Figure schematically illustrating clinically validated formulations.[ , , ] These systems potentiate therapeutic outcomes through synergistic mechanisms: stabilization of circulatory payload integrity, tumor-selective accumulation leveraging enhanced permeation and retention (EPR) effects and ligand-mediated targeting, biodistribution-controlled toxicity mitigation, and spatiotemporal precision via stimuli-responsive release dynamics.[ ] This review synthesizes current developments in oncological nanomedicine, assessing therapeutic innovations, delineating technological constraints, and outlining strategic priorities for clinical translation.
 

## Tumor Microenvironment and Its Pathological Characteristics
The tumor microenvironment (TME) serves as a dynamic pathophysiological niche driving oncogenesis through intricate cellular and molecular cross-talk [Figure , central region]. The TME is pathologically defined by these cardinal features: hypoxia, gradients, persistent inflammation and functional immunosuppression. Tumorigenic expansion establishes hypoxic gradients through perfusion demand mismatch, stabilizing hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs) that drive vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)-mediated pathological angiogenesis and immunosuppressive signaling, which is clinically correlating with metastatic progression and poor survival. Preclinical studies validate HIF-driven metastatic dissemination, with genetic ablation suppressing tumor progression.[ ] Concomitant metabolic reprogramming generates acidic microenvironments via lactate overproduction, paralyzing cytotoxic T lymphocytes and natural killer (NK) cells. These pathophysiological adaptations collectively drive immune evasion and multidrug resistance to conventional therapies.[ ]
 The TME describes the complex ecosystem created by tumor cells that consists of a diversity of immune cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells and other stromal cells, embedded in a dynamic extracellular matrix (ECM).[ ] The non-cancerous cells and extracellular components form a supportive environment facilitating cancer cell proliferation, invasion, and metastasis through multidimensional interactions. In this dynamic system, cancer cells recruit and reprogram non-cancerous cells to remodel TME. Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) play a dual regulatory role in the TME. They are activated by factors released by cancer cells and subsequently secrete a range of factors that influence the TME, becoming one of the key drivers of tumor progression. CAFs can produce fibroblast growth factor (FGF), VEGF, and stromal cell-derived factor 1 (SDF1), which stimulate cell proliferation, angiogenesis, and metastasis.[ , ] In addition, CAFs secrete matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), a degradation enzyme of ECM. The family of MMPs play important roles in multiple processes of carcinogenesis and participate in the regulation of cell growth, apoptosis, angiogenesis, invasion, metastasis and immune evasion.
CAFs also secrete interleukin-6 (IL-6), which promotes the differentiation of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs). This process indirectly suppresses the activity of cytotoxic T cells which, in turn, weakens the body’s antitumor immune response.[ , ] Furthermore, CAFs produce transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β), which inhibits the maturation of dendritic cells (DCs), enhances the immunosuppressive microenvironment, and induces T cells to differentiate into regulatory T cells (Tregs). This event further suppresses the function of effector T cells, enabling the tumors to evade immune elimination and achieve sustained growth and metastasis. Moreover, cancer-associated adipocytes (CAA) are another stomal cell clusters converted by cancer cells and similarly secrete various cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors to create a pro-inflammatory environment associated with lipid metabolism that promoting cancer progression and immune suppression.[ ] These unique features of the TME offer critical entry points for the development of tumor-specific therapeutics.
Nanomedicine capitalizes on TME pathophysiological features through engineered platforms. Therapeutic breakthroughs include mRNA vaccines delivered via biocompatible lipid-polymer vectors enabling spatiotemporal gene expression, membrane-mimetic nanosystems leveraging endogenous biomolecular recognition for dual tumor targeting/biocompatibility, and DNA-based programmable architectures [Figure ]. Modular design of mRNA carriers provides nuclease resistance, immune evasion, and organotropism while activating coordinated innate/adaptive antitumor immunity.[ , ] Biomimetic variants demonstrate TME responsive cargo release (cytotoxins/immunomodulators) through pH/enzymetriggered mechanisms, enabling chemoimmunotherapy synergy with minimized off-target effects.[ ]
Co-delivery of chemotherapeutics and immunomodulators enables dual tumor and TME targeting. DNA-based platforms exploit nucleic acid programmability for conditionally activated payload release, achieving precise TME-specific delivery with minimal off-target effects.[ , ] TME-optimized strategies include: pH-responsive systems maintaining circulatory stability while releasing payloads in acidic niches;[ ] Arg-Gly-Asp (RGD)-functionalized nanoparticles targeting neovascular integrins for anti-angiogenic therapy;[ ] immune-targeted variants reprogramming cytotoxic lymphocytes to enhance antitumor immunity;[ ] and protease-activatable carriers remodeling tumor ECM to potentiate drug penetration.[ ]

## Mechanism of Nanomedicine Accumulation
Nanomedicines, with their advantages of highly targeted delivery, controlled release, and precise modulation of the immune system, can more accurately regulate the TME to achieve accurate antitumor effects while reducing damage to normal tissues. Nanomedicines accumulation in tumors achieved through the EPR effect and ligand-mediated targeting is significant for cancer diagnosis and therapy [Figure ].[ , ] Primarily due to pathological angiogenesis in the tumor tissues, their vascular structure of tumor differs from normal vessels. Larger endothelial gaps (100–2000 nm),[ ] reduced smooth muscle coverage, and impaired vascular tone regulation, collectively enhance vascular permeability and extravasation, facilitating the passive accumulation of nanomedicines in tumor tissues. Furthermore, solid tumors often exhibit impairment of lymphatic drainage, which prevents the nanomedicines from flowing back, thereby promoting their accumulation in the tumor region.[ , ]
 Doxil® (a liposome-encapsulated formulation of doxorubicin [DOX], developed by Sequus Pharmaceuticals Inc., USA) was the first nanomedicine approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) [Figure ]. Doxil® used polyethylene glycol (PEG)-coated liposomes,[ ] to reduce nonspecific protein binding in the serum, which significantly decreased its clearance by the reticuloendothelial system (RES), and extended the whole body circulation time. As a result, a much higher accumulation rate occurred in the tumor regions than that in free form. Iron oxide nanoparticles, which are used as contrast agent, can enhance tumor imaging contrast and increase tumor detection accuracy under the influence of EPR effect.[ ] These examples collectively demonstrate the remarkable advantages of passive targeting strategy by improving drug-delivery efficiency and therapeutic outcomes. By ingeniously exploiting the unique properties of the TME, drug carriers can more precisely localize to the tumor sites, and hence enabling action against lesions while minimizing damage to the normal tissues.
The EPR effect provides a foundation for the passive targeting and accumulation of nanomedicines in tumors. Beyond this, by modifying the surface of nanocarriers with specific targeting molecules (such as antibodies, peptides, sugars, or ligands), the nanocarriers can specifically bind to target receptors on cells or tissues [Figure ]. This action enhances drug accumulation at the target site, reduces toxicity to normal tissues, and improves therapeutic efficacy.[ , ] When compared with the traditional drug-delivery methods, active targeting techniques can significantly reduce drug distribution across non-target tissues and increase the drug concentration at the target site, thereby enhancing efficacy and minimizing systemic side-effects. For example, in an active targeting strategy, researchers modified the surface of nanoparticles with folic acid molecules, enabling specific binding to folate receptors overexpressed on ovarian cancer cells, which, in turn, improves the specificity of drug delivery to ovarian cancer cells.[ ] Similarly, Dutta et al[ ] used human EGFR 2 (HER2) antibody-modified nanocarriers to target HER2-positive tumor cells to achieve precise drug delivery. These studies demonstrate the significant advantages of active targeting strategies in enhancing therapeutic specificity and reducing drug exposure to normal tissues. In addition, Sindhwani et al’s research demonstrated that, beyond the EPR effect, nanoparticles could also enter the TME through endothelial cell-mediated endocytosis. This strategy, combining both passive accumulation through the EPR effect and active accumulation, effectively enhances the uptake of nanomedicines by tumor cells, thereby improving their antitumor efficacy.[ ]

## Types of Nanocarrier-Mediated Drug Delivery

## Polymer nanoparticles
Polymer nanoparticles are nanoscale structures composed of natural polymers, such as proteins and polysaccharides, or synthetic materials such as polylactic acid (PLA) and PEG.[ ] These nanoparticles exist in various forms, including polymersomes, polymeric micelles, dendrimers, and nanogels, each with distinct features and applications [Figure ].[ , ] Polymer micelles are self-assembled structures formed by amphiphilic block copolymers. They feature a hydrophobic core that encapsulates hydrophobic drugs and a hydrophilic shell that enhances water solubility. Dendrimers have a highly branched, tree-like architecture originating from a core molecule or monomer. Their surface functional groups can be chemically modified to attach drugs, targeting ligands, or fluorescent probes. Dendrimers offer precise control over properties such as mass, size, shape, and surface characteristics during synthesis. Polymer-drug conjugates involve the chemical attachment of drugs to polymer-carriers using bonds such as ester, peptide, or amide bonds. These bonds are designed to degrade under specific physiological conditions, such as varying pH levels or enzymatic activity, to release the drug molecules in a controlled manner.
 As schematically illustrated in Figure , the immunomodulatory lipid nanoparticle (LNPs-gel) nanohydrogel system integrates multiple functional components to achieve precise immune modulation in tumor tissues.[ ] In this system, ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA), incorporated into LNPs (LNPs with UDCA, defined as uLNPs) as a partial substitute for cholesterol, plays a crucial role in promoting the autophagic degradation of TGF-β1, thereby suppressing its immunosuppressive signaling. By downregulating TGF-β1 signaling, this process helps disrupt the immunosuppressive TME, thereby inhibiting Treg differentiation and diminishing their immunosuppressive activity. Meanwhile, the LNPs encapsulate circular RNA (circRNA/cRNA) encoding an IL-2-fragment crystallizable (IL-2-Fc) fusion protein. The unique circular structure of circRNA confers resistance to nuclease degradation and enables prolonged cytoplasmic translation, ensuring sustained IL-2-Fc expression in tumor cells. To achieve spatiotemporal control of drug delivery, the uLNPs are embedded in a pH-responsive nanohydrogel matrix. Administered through percutaneous injection into superficial solid tumors (e.g., melanoma), this composite system preferentially accumulates in immunosuppressive TMEs characterized by high Treg infiltration. In response to the acidic TME, the hydrogel undergoes pH-responsive degradation, enabling sustained LNP release. Following endocytosis by tumor or immune cells, the LNPs deliver circRNA cargo to the cytoplasm, where it is efficiently translated into IL-2-Fc. This bioactive cytokine enhances cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) activation and proliferation while reducing Treg infiltration, thereby reversing the immunosuppressive TME and boosting antitumor immunity.
 Polymer nanoparticles exhibit excellent biocompatibility, low toxicity, and high drug-loading capacity. They enhance drug stability and biodistribution while enabling targeted delivery and controlled release, making them a promising platform for advancing cancer therapy. In 2007, South Korea introduced Genexol-PM® (developed by Samyang Co., Seoul, South Korea), the first polymeric micelle formulation of paclitaxel, approved for treating NSCLC, breast cancer, ovarian cancer, and pancreatic cancer [Figure ].[ ] Despite recent advancements, polymeric nanomedicines face challenges that hinder their clinical application, including poor pharmacokinetic control, discrepancies between preclinical and clinical results, and difficulties in large-scale production. Addressing these challenges requires further research and optimization.

## Lipid nanoparticles
LNPs are composed of phospholipid molecules with hydrophilic heads that align along both inner and outer surfaces of the bilayer and hydrophobic tails that cluster in the bilayer’s center, and typically range from tens to hundreds of nanometers in size.[ ] The aqueous core of LNPs provides a protective environment for encapsulating hydrophilic drugs, such as small molecules, proteins, and nucleic acids which shield the drugs from degradation and facilitates their release upon reaching target cells. For lipophilic drugs, the lipid bilayer serves as the primary drug-loading region. The integration of lipophilic drugs into the hydrophobic tails of the bilayer enhances their stability, reduces their systemic toxicity, and enables the optimization of their release kinetics by adjusting the lipid composition. Doxil® was initially indicated for AIDS-related Kaposi’s sarcoma, and later approved for ovarian cancer and other solid tumors.[ ] The success of Doxil® established a foundation for the clinical application of drug delivery.
The 2023 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine is a significant milestone in lipid nanotechnology, which was awarded to Katalin Karikó and Drew Weissman for their discoveries in nucleoside base modification that enabled the development of mRNA-encapsulated ionizable LNP based vaccine for COVID-19.[ ] Unlike traditional liposomes, ionizable LNPs have demonstrated significant advantages in drug delivery and gene therapy with their unique micellar core structures optimized through formulation and synthesis parameters. Typically, ionizable LNPs comprise four key components: (1) Ionizable or cationic lipids: Form complexes with negatively charged nucleic acids and facilitate their release. (2) Phospholipids: Provide the structural framework of the LNP. (3) Cholesterol: Enhances particle stability and assists in membrane fusion. (4) PEG-modified lipids: Improve circulation time, increase stability, and reduce immune system clearance [Figure ]. Ionizable LNPs are particularly effective in encapsulating mRNA for protection from enzymatic degradation, high circulation stability, and efficient cellular delivery. They also minimize systemically nonspecific immune responses, significantly improving the safety of mRNA expression.
 The use of Ionizable LNPs has opened an efficient pathway for organ-specific targeting strategies. By incorporating cationic or anionic lipids, LNPs can selectively accumulate in different organs owing to their unique surface-charge.[ ] Cationic LNPs, with positively charged surfaces, can interact electrostatically with negatively charged tumor cell membranes, thereby achieving high efficacy in tumor treatment. For instance, DNA vaccines or anticancer drugs delivered by cationic LNPs have been demonstrated to effectively accumulate in tumor tissues effectively.[ ] In contrast, anionic LNPs with negatively charged surfaces, tend to accumulate in the Kupffer cells of the liver, which makes them ideal carriers for treating liver diseases.[ ] Dilliard et al[ ] developed selective organ targeting (SORT) nanoparticles by using different compositions and ratios of lipids. This strategy enabled SORT nanoparticles to interact with specific proteins in the blood, and hence overcoming liver accumulation and achieving RNA expression in extrahepatic organs such as the spleen and lung.
In clinical applications, the success of Onpattro® (developed by Alnylam Pharmaceuticals Inc., USA), the first FDA- and European Commission-approved small interfering RNA (siRNA) drug delivered through LNPs, highlights the therapeutic potential of LNPs in ilencing disease-related genes. Approved in 2018 for treating hereditary transthyretin-mediated amyloidosis (hATTR), Onpattro® marked a breakthrough in the application of siRNA therapies.[ ] In summary, LNPs are highly versatile drug carriers, applicable across a broad spectrum of therapeutics, from traditional chemical drugs and proteins to advanced nucleic acids and gene-editing tools. Their small size, structural similarity to biological membranes, excellent biocompatibility, and controlled release properties establish them as a cornerstone of precision medicine. Continuous innovation and surface modifications have enhanced their targeting ability, circulation time, and specificity, further solidifying their role as a powerful platform for developing safe, efficient, and novel therapeutic strategies.

## Biomembrane-based nanoparticles
Biomembrane-based nanocarrier-mediated drug delivery has become a focal point of research due to its excellent biocompatibility, low immunogenicity, and targeting properties. Tumor cells, which are easily cultured in vitro, express cluster of differentiation 47 (CD47) on their membrane surface, releasing “do not eat me” signals. CD47 interacts with signal regulatory protein α (SIRPα) on macrophages, preventing them from recognizing and phagocytosing tumor cells, thus leading to immune evasion. This prolongs the circulation time of nanoparticles in the bloodstream and enhances their homotypic targeting ability, increasing the selective accumulation of drugs in the TME. Tumor-secreted colony-stimulating factors also induce macrophages to differentiate into the tumor-promoting M2 phenotype. Based on this, Rao et al[ ] developed gene-engineered macrophage cell membrane vesicles (gCMs) overexpressing the SIRPα variant CV1, which encapsulated magnetic nanoparticles (MNs) to form gCM-MNs. CV1-expressing gCM-MNs competitively bind to CD47 on tumor cell membranes, reversing immune evasion. Under magnetic guidance, MNs accumulate in the TME, generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) to induce the polarization of M2 macrophages to the M1 phenotype, thus enhancing antitumor effects.
Red blood cells (RBCs), being the most numerous cells in the body, are highly accessible for utilization. Mature RBCs lack organelles and a nucleus, simplifying the process of extracting and purifying their membranes. The surface of RBC’s membrane contains proteins such as CD47 and C8bp which inhibit the phagocytic activity of immune cells, prolonging its circulation time to 120 days in vivo. Wang et al[ ] developed RBC-tumor cell hybrid membrane-coated hollow copper sulfide nanoparticles (DCuS@[RBC−B16] NPs), in which RBC membranes were fused with B16 melanoma cell membranes to create a hybrid membrane. The DCuS@[RBC−B16] NPs were loaded with DOX, resulting in prolonged circulation time and enhanced tumor targeting in photothermal/chemotherapy combination treatment [Figure , bottom left].
As critical mediators of hemostasis and thrombosis, platelet membranes demonstrate unique vascular targeting capabilities. Activated platelets release extracellular vesicles (EVs)—nanoscale membrane structures released by various cell types, classified by their biogenesis pathways into exosomes and microvesicles (generated during routine cellular activities) versus apoptotic bodies (produced during programmed cell death)—each performing distinct physiological roles. These vesicles demonstrate dual therapeutic capabilities through intrinsic targeting properties and cargo delivery functions. For instance, NK cell-derived exosomes carrying cytotoxic payloads exhibit tumor-suppressive effects in preclinical models.[ ]
Biomembrane-based nanocarrier-mediated drug delivery has demonstrated remarkable efficacy in both in vitro and in vivo therapeutic applications due to their excellent biocompatibility, precise active targeting, and prolonged circulation time. Given the unique properties of various biomembranes, drug delivery plays a crucial role in treating multiple diseases, offering new avenues for addressing refractory conditions. However, their development faces several challenges. Specifically, individual variability in biomembrane sources, along with the limited availability and blood type matching issues of red blood cell membranes, hinder large-scale production and clinical application. White blood cells and platelet membranes require activation, but platelet membranes exhibit significant variability in activation sensitivity, leading to processing instability. These challenges must be addressed through further research to accelerate the clinical translation of biomembrane-based nanocarrier-mediated drug delivery.

## Albumin nanoparticles
Human serum albumin (HSA), one of the smallest yet most abundant proteins in plasma, is a versatile nanocarrier for drug delivery and has attracted significant attention in materials science, nanotechnology, and biomedicine. Albumin is synthesized by liver parenchymal cells and constitutes 40–60% of total plasma proteins. It plays critical roles in maintaining plasma osmotic pressure, regulating blood pH, and transporting and storing various substances such as fatty acids, hormones, and ions. As a natural plasma protein with a long in vivo half-life, albumin exhibits excellent biocompatibility and hydrophilicity in drug delivery. Surface modification or drug encapsulation in albumin nanoparticles prevents rapid clearance (e.g., through liver and spleen filtration), thereby extending circulation time and promoting gradual accumulation in tumor tissues through the EPR effect. In addition, albumin can actively target tumor cells through its natural receptors, which are highly expressed on many tumor cells, facilitating internalization and enhancing drug uptake. Albumin can also be conjugated with other targeting molecules (e.g., antibodies, peptides) to further enhance tumor specificity. Moreover, the acidic TME of rapidly proliferating tumor cells may facilitate better distribution and stability of albumin nanoparticles.[ ]
The clinical impact of HSA nanotechnology was catalyzed by the 2005 FDA approval of Abraxane® (albumin-bound paclitaxel, developed by Abraxis BioScience, USA) for metastatic breast cancer [Figure ]. Subsequent label expansions to NSCLC and pancreatic cancer demonstrated its broad utility. By exploiting HSA’s tumor tropism, this nanoformulation achieves 33% higher intratumoral paclitaxel concentrations than conventional taxanes, significantly improving therapeutic indices [Figure and Figure ].[ ] The platform’s versatility was further evidenced by Fyarro® (approved for PEComa in 2021, developed by Aadi Bioscience Inc., USA ) and Nanozora® (developed by Ablynx in Belgium, approved for rheumatoid arthritis in 2022, Japan).[ ] These clinical milestones validate HSA’s superiority as a modular drug delivery scaffold. With emerging bioconjugation technologies, next-generation HSA nanocarriers are positioned to revolutionize targeted therapy paradigms.
 

## Peptide nanoparticles
Peptides, composed of short amino acid chains, are known for their structural diversity and biological activity. Found widely in nature, peptides play essential roles in numerous biological processes. By modifying their amino acid sequence and spatial conformation, peptide-based nanomedicines can be finely tuned for specific properties and functions. Under specific conditions, peptides can self-assemble into ordered nanostructures, such as nanofibers, nanotubes, and nanospheres, through noncovalent interactions. This self-assembly behavior can be leveraged to design drug carriers that encapsulate small-molecule drugs within these nanostructures, enhancing drug stability, improving uptake by tumor cells, and enabling controlled release. This strategy aims to increase therapeutic efficacy while reducing toxicity. For example, Soukasene et al[ ] encapsulated camptothecin (CPT) within peptide-based nanofibers, enhancing the drug’s water solubility by 50-fold. This nanofiber system successfully targeted tumor regions and enabled the gradual release of CPT, significantly improving therapeutic outcomes in breast cancer models.

## DNA nanostructures-based nanoparticles
Recent advancements in DNA origami have propelled the field beyond structural design toward functional innovation and therapeutic implementation. This technology harnesses sequence-specific Watson-Crick base pairing to guide the self-assembly of scaffold DNA strands into predesigned nanoarchitectures under precisely controlled biophysical conditions. The unique advantages of DNA nanostructures—unparalleled structural precision, inherent biocompatibility, and multifunctional adaptability—establish them as transformative platforms for precision drug delivery. Through sequence-programmable assembly, researchers can fabricate rod-like geometries, polyhedral frameworks (tetrahedrons, octahedrons, icosahedrons), and other complex morphologies with atomic-level accuracy. These nanostructures permit site-specific functionalization with targeting aptamers, therapeutic oligonucleotides, chemodrugs, and protein conjugates—a modular design strategy that enables combinatorial payload delivery while maintaining spatiotemporal control.[ ]
Tetrahedral DNA nanoparticles (TDNs) are a typical polyhedral nanostructure [Figure , bottom right]. Their functional groups or molecules can be modified in four ways: (1) Attached to the vertices; (2) mounted on the edge arms; (3) encapsulated inside the cage-like structure; or (3) integrated into the double-helix structure through conjugation. Fan et al[ ] fabricated an inhalable pH-responsive DNA tetrahedral nanostructure (CP@TDN) for delivering immunomodulatory cytosine-phosphate-guanine (CpG) oligonucleotides and programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1)-targeting antagonistic DNA aptamers. In the acidic TME, the C-base-rich pH-sensitive aptamer on the TDN preferentially adopts an i-motif structure. This conformational change prevents hybridization with the complementary DNA sequence derived from apt-PD-L1 (PD-L1-targeting antagonistic DNA aptamer), thereby unlocking the structure and releasing apt-PD-L1. The released apt-PD-L1 then binds to PD-L1 on tumor cell surfaces, triggering immune activation, including the activation of antigen-presenting cells (APCs) and the recruitment and activation of T cells, ultimately leading to the treatment of pulmonary metastatic tumors.[ ] In addition to pH-responsive sequence release, TDNs can also be released via nucleic acid- or enzyme-sensitive sequences.[ ] TDNs offer advantages such as simple self-assembly, stable mechanical properties, good biocompatibility, and resistance to nuclease degradation. They show promise for applications in biosensors, separation and analysis, bioimaging, and drug delivery. However, the potential for TDNs to cause adverse effects in vivo requires further investigation.

## Inorganic nanoparticles
Engineered inorganic nanoparticles are broadly categorized by core constituents into noble metals, metal oxides, and magnetic composites.[ ] These nanoplatforms possess engineered physicochemical properties—precisely tunable dimensions, controllable surface morphologies, and tailorable optoelectronic behaviors—that drive their adoption across precision medicine applications spanning diagnostic imaging, targeted drug delivery, and image-guided interventions. Noble metal nanoparticles, particularly gold nanostructures (AuNPs), demonstrate exceptional translational potential owing to their shape-tunable plasmonic properties (nanospheres, nanoshells, nanorods). Surface functionalization with tumor-targeting ligands enables precision drug delivery, while their plasmonic photothermal conversion capability permits localized tumor ablation.[ , ] In parallel, silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) exhibit potent antimicrobial activity through ionic release mechanisms, finding clinical utility in advanced wound care systems and medical device coatings.[ ]
Mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs) represent another key class, with their tunable pore architectures and high surface area enabling precise drug loading. These platforms successfully deliver diverse payloads—from small-molecule chemotherapeutics to biologics—while maintaining controlled release profiles.[ ] Superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPIONs), exemplified by the FDAapproved Ferumoxytol® (developed by AMAG Pharmaceuticals Inc., USA), demonstrate multifunctional capabilities as MRI contrast enhancers and magnetically guidable drug carriers. Their superparamagnetism enables three key clinical applications: (1) enhanced tumor visualization through T2-weighted signal modulation, (2) magnetic field-directed therapeutic accumulation, (3) stem cell tracking via magnetic particle imaging.[ ]
The unique properties and diverse applications of inorganic nanoparticles highlight their ability to improve diagnostic precision, enhance therapeutic efficacy, and minimize treatment-related side effects.

## Applications in Improving Cancer Therapy

## Applications in improving immunotherapy
The fundamental principle of tumor immunotherapy is to activate or modulate the immune system, enhancing its capacity to recognize and eliminate tumor cells. Immunotherapy leverages the innate characteristics of the immune system, such as its ability to specifically recognize non-self antigens and generate immune memory. By overcoming tumor cells’ immune evasion mechanisms, this approach effectively suppresses tumor growth, metastasis, and recurrence.
Nanomedicines address key challenges in tumor immunotherapy through synergistic mechanisms. First, by integrating the EPR effect with active targeting, nanodrugs enhance the selective accumulation of immunomodulators in tumors.[ , , ] Functionalization with targeting ligands, such as anti-cytotoxic T-lymphocyte antigen 4 (CTLA-4), prostate-specific membrane antigen (PMSA) aptamers, enables precise recognition of tumor-associated markers such as EGFR, HER2, and mucin 1 (MUC1).[ ] This promotes receptor-mediated endocytosis and reduces unintended T-cell activation in healthy tissues. Second, functionalized nanocarriers enhance immune responses through co-delivery strategies: (1) Antigen-presenting nanovectors (e.g., mannose receptor-targeting carriers) facilitate DCs’ antigen uptake and processing;[ , ] (2) coencapsulation of Toll-like receptor (TLR)/stimulator of interferon genes (STING) agonists enables the synchronized release of antigens and adjuvants;[ ] (3) PD-1/PD-L1-blocking nanodrugs restore T-cell cytotoxicity by inhibiting immune checkpoints. Third, stimuli-responsive nanoplatforms modulate the immunosuppressive TME through diverse mechanisms: (1) pH-/enzyme-responsive carriers enable site-specific drug release; (2) delivery of indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO)/colony-stimulating factor 1 receptor (CSF-1R) inhibitor delivery induces macrophage polarization toward the M1 phenotype;[ , ] (3) collagenase-loaded nanoparticles promote immune cell infiltration by degrading the ECM. These synergistic approaches effectively overcome both physical and immune-suppressive barriers, thereby enhancing the efficacy of cancer immunotherapy.

## Immune checkpoint inhibitors
Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) are among the most effective and widely used strategies in antitumor immunotherapy, demonstrating significant efficacy across various cancers. ICIs function by blocking immune-suppressive pathways, thereby restoring the immune system’s ability to recognize and attack tumor cells. Immune checkpoint molecules (e.g., PD-1/PD-L1, CTLA-4) serve as intrinsic regulators that prevent autoimmunity by protecting normal tissues. However, tumor cells exploit these mechanisms to evade immune responses, suppressing T-cell activity and enabling their unchecked proliferation. ICIs counteract this immune suppression by disrupting interactions between checkpoint molecules and their ligands, reactivating T cells to eliminate tumor cells. This approach enhances immune responses and has shown the potential to generate long-lasting antitumor immunity in some patients. Despite these benefits, the therapeutic efficacy of traditional ICIs is often limited by poor targeting, suboptimal delivery, and the emergence of resistance. For instance, the response rate for anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody monotherapy is only 20–30%, leaving a substantial proportion of patients unresponsive to PD-1-based treatments.[ ] Nanocarrier-mediated drug delivery offers promising solutions to these challenges. They can enhance the precision and efficiency of ICI targeting, improving outcomes. Furthermore, these systems enable the codelivery of complementary agents, such as siRNA, to inhibit immune checkpoints directly or modulate the tumor immune microenvironment.[ ]

## Targeting immune cells therapy
The immunotoxic TME consists of adequate blood supply, effective formation of tertiary lymphoid structures (TLSs), infiltration of effector T cells, and an immune-promoting cellular assembly, including APCs, NK cells, macrophages (with a higher proportion of M1 than M2 type), and effector B cells.[ , ] Conversely, an immunosuppressive TME is often surrounded by dense stromal components, such as fibrin, collagen, and laminin, and is characterized by hypoxia, acidity, and a high prevalence of CAFs.[ , ] In this setting, tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) typically show reduced cytotoxic T cells and an abundance of Tregs, MDSCs, and M2 macrophages, all contributing to immune suppression.
Efforts to target immune cells within the TME, particularly tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and Tregs, are rapidly advancing. Macrophages can polarize into two phenotypes: M1 macrophages, associated with pro-inflammatory and antitumor activities, and M2 macrophages, which promote immunosuppression, angiogenesis, and tumor progression. M1 macrophages, activated via the classical pathway, secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), facilitating adaptive immune responses and tumor cell elimination. High levels of iNOS lead to the production of nitric oxide (NO), which damages tumor cell DNA, proteins, and other biomolecules while enhancing immune activity.[ ] In contrast, M2 macrophages, activated through the alternative pathway, secrete anti-inflammatory cytokines and support tissue repair, angiogenesis, and tumor growth. TAMs in the TME predominantly exhibit the M2 phenotype, with their abundance often correlating with poor clinical outcomes. These M2 TAMs not only promote malignant behaviors directly but also indirectly suppress immune responses through their interactions with other immune cells in the TME. Notably, TAM polarization is dynamic, with interconversion between M1 and M2 states possible under specific conditions, making TAMs attractive targets for immunotherapy. For instance, Liu et al[ ] developed Ru@ICG-BLZ nanoparticles capable of repolarizing TAMs toward the M1 phenotype, thereby restoring their antitumor activity.
Ru@ICG–BLZ nanoparticles exhibit superior drug loading capacity and inflammation-responsive drug release behavior. Upon entering the TME, the released BLZ-945 inhibits the CSF-1/CSF-1R signaling pathway and significantly downregulates M2-associated markers in TAMs. The disruption of TAM reprogramming induced by tumor cells promotes a phenotypic shift of TAM from the tumor-promoting M2 type to the antitumor M1 type and improves the expression of iNOS and IL-12, markers of M1 macrophages, thereby enhancing the antitumor immune response. In addition, Ru@ICG–BLZ nanoparticles effectively induce photothermal ablation of solid tumors, with minimal damage to surrounding tissues. Similarly, immunotherapy targeting Tregs, which suppress antitumor immune responses, is gaining traction. Tregs inhibit the activity of effector T cells and NK cells through various mechanisms, and hence facilitate immune evasion and tumor progression. An increased Treg presence in the TME is frequently associated with poor prognosis. To counteract this, Sato et al[ ] employed near-infrared photoimmunotherapy (NIR-PIT) to deplete Tregs via CD25 targeting strategy and photothermal effect. This strategy successfully activated CD8+ T cells and NK cells, and restored localized antitumor immunity within the TME.

## Tumor vaccine
Tumor vaccines represent a promising approach in cancer immunotherapy, designed to enhance T-cell-mediated antitumor responses. These vaccines introduce exogenous antigens to stimulate the immune system, enabling it to recognize and target tumor-specific antigens effectively. The resulting immune response promotes the elimination of tumor cells and establishes long-term antitumor immune memory. Tumor vaccines mainly include cell-mediated vaccines, such as DC-based tumor vaccines, as stated in “Dendritic cell tumor vaccines”, and molecule-mediated vaccines, including peptide, mRNA, and DNA vaccines, as stated in “Peptide tumor vaccine”, “mRNA tumor vaccine”, and “DNA tumor vaccine”.

## Dendritic cell tumor vaccines
DC tumor vaccines use DCs to stimulate the immune system against tumors. As pivotal mediators between innate and adaptive immunity, DCs are key to initiating immune responses. They act as “sentinels”, capturing tumor-associated antigens (TAAs), processing them, and presenting these antigens to T and B lymphocytes, thereby activating T cells.[ ] Activated T cells can recognize and destroy tumor cells. Moreover, DCs migrate between lymphoid and nonlymphoid tissues, regulate inflammation, and direct lymphocyte homing via cytokine secretion, all of which are crucial for maintaining durable antitumor immunity.[ ] DC tumor vaccines are created by loading DCs with TAAs, after which the modified cells are reinfused into the patient. These DCs present the antigens in vivo, eliciting a tumor-specific immune response. This approach is highly targeted, stimulating immune activity against TAAs while minimizing damage to healthy tissues.
Clinically approved DC vaccines include Hybricell (approved in Brazil in 2004 for melanoma), DCVax-Brain (approved in Switzerland in 2007 for glioblastoma), and Sipuleucel-T (approved in the U.S. in 2010 for prostate cancer).[ ] These vaccines induce tumor-specific T-cell activation, frequently synergized with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) to amplify cytotoxic responses. A clinical study combining Neo-DCVac with PD-1 inhibitors demonstrated enhanced therapeutic efficacy in advanced lung cancer,[ ] with the vaccine restoring ICI responsiveness in progressing patients and achieving tumor control. Despite encouraging preclinical outcomes, DC-based tumor vaccines confront formidable translational barriers. Current Good Manufacturing Practice (cGMP)-compliant manufacturing requires rigorous logistics and clinical efficacy in advanced-stage tumors exhibit constrained objective response rates (ORR). Most DC vaccines remain confined to phase I development; scalable clinical translation remains premature. Research imperatives focus on advancing precision antigen-loading platforms and developing modular combination strategies with immune modulators to augment the therapeutic potential.

## Peptide tumor vaccine
Peptide vaccines constitute a precision immunotherapeutic modality employing synthetic epitopes of tumor-associated or tumor-specific antigens to elicit antigen-specific immunity.[ ] Administered peptides undergo APC-mediated processing (primarily DCs) and loaded onto major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules, which activate cognate T cell receptors. CTLs execute tumor lysis via perforin-granzyme-mediated cytolysis and Fas ligand (FasL)/Fas apoptotic pathways, while CD4+ helper T cells secrete Th1-polarizing cytokines (IL-2, IFN-γ) to amplify CTL effector functions and recruit innate immune effectors like NK cells. This coordinated immune attack suppresses tumor growth and metastasis. Despite precise epitope targeting, short-peptide formulations exhibit compromised immunogenicity and off-target MHC cross-presentation, inducing autoimmune reactivity against normal tissues expressing homologous epitopes.[ ] Long peptide constructs enable dual MHC class loading: MHC-II-restricted CD4+ T cell priming synergizes with DC-mediated MHC-I cross-presentation to activate tumor-reactive CD8+ CTLs, potentiating epitope spreading.[ ]
The clinical trial code-named ATALANTE-1 (NCT02654587) is a phase III randomized controlled clinical trial targeting HLA-A2-positive, ICI-resistant patients with advanced NSCLC. This trial evaluated the peptide vaccine Tedopi® (OSE2101, developed by OSE Immunotherapeutics SAS, a French biopharmaceutical company) against standard chemotherapy. The findings demonstrated that Tedopi® significantly improved median overall survival (OS) in patients with secondary resistance to ICIs (11.1 months vs. 7.5 months), with a favorable safety profile.[ ] In general, peptide tumor vaccines offer excellent adaptability, allowing for precise design and modification based on the specific antigen epitopes of various cancers. With mature chemical synthesis techniques, they facilitate large-scale, standardized production. The process is simple, easy to control, and cost-effective, providing an economically feasible foundation for widespread clinical application.

## mRNA tumor vaccine
In 2021, the FDA approved the first mRNA-based vaccine against coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), which expands the exploration and recognition of the potential of mRNA vaccines in cancer therapy. Advances in sequencing technologies enable the prediction of tumor-specific neoantigens through the identification of mutations in tumor exons and abnormal mRNA transcription or translation.[ ] mRNA vaccines introduce synthetic mRNA into cells and produce antigenic proteins transiently with the cell’s endogenous protein synthesis machinery. These proteins are cleaved into peptides through lysosomal pathways and then presented by MHC molecules to CD4+ and CD8+ T cells or recognized by B cells upon release. This process activates both cellular and humoral immune responses.[ ] Tumor cells often express antigens absent or minimally expressed in normal cells, while relative immune response can be highly specific by precisely designing mRNA to encode these antigens. This strategy further minimizes off-target effects and collateral damage to healthy tissues. Moreover, mRNA vaccines can induce immune memory and support long-term therapeutic responses.
mRNA has a short half-life and poor stability in vivo, which makes it prone to degradation by the ubiquitously present ribonucleases (RNases/cRNA). To address this, new structures such as circRNA or double-stranded RNAs (dsRNA) have been developed to enhance stability and expression efficiency.[ , ] LNPs are commonly used as carrier materials providing mRNA vaccines with properties like high stability, controllable safety, and efficient delivery to target cells. mRNA tumor vaccines offer several advantages over other vaccine types. First, mRNA vaccines are rapidly hydrolyzed and cleared by RNases, which prevents their accumulation in tissues other than the tumor and minimizing potential side effects. Compared to peptides, mRNA has smaller molecular weight and can simultaneously encode multiple peptides to produce a large quantity of relevant antigens. Unlike DNA vaccines, which can only be effective in cell nucleus, mRNA is typically translated in the cytoplasm and does not integrate into the host genome. This eliminates the risk of genome mutations and transgenic biosafety concerns, theoretically making mRNA vaccines much safer than DNA-based vaccines.
In 2023, Nature published the results of a clinical trial evaluating a personalized mRNA vaccine for pancreatic cancer. The study revealed that 50% of patients who received the autologous gene-based cevumeran vaccine developed specific T-cell immune responses. Notably, none of these patients experienced tumor recurrence during the 18-month follow-up period.[ ] Palmer et al[ ] investigated a personalized heterologous neoantigen vaccine combining chimpanzee adenovirus (ChAd68) and self-amplifying mRNA (samRNA) in conjunction with ICIs for patients with advanced metastatic solid tumors. The combination therapy was shown to be safe, well-tolerated, and capable of eliciting robust, durable, and functional immune responses in these patients.[ ] The success of mRNA vaccines in these trials highlights their therapeutic potential, addressing the limitations of traditional small-molecule drugs and antibody therapies. This progress underscores the transformative role mRNA vaccines may play in advancing personalized medicine and cancer treatment.[ , ]
As shown in Table , our study systematically analyzed enrollment-stage clinical trials of mRNA-based antitumor therapeutics registered on ClinicalTrials.gov since 2023. Analysis revealed a marked surge in mRNA drug development, with neoantigen-related therapies dominating 80% of current research efforts. Notably, China spearheaded the majority of these trials, demonstrating a distinct first-mover advantage in mRNA technology translation. Over the coming years, we anticipate groundbreaking advances in mRNA-based tumor therapeutics to emerge, potentially driving a paradigm shift in precision oncology.
 

## DNA tumor vaccine
DNA tumor vaccines use plasmids as carriers to deliver TAAs. These plasmids are engineered to encode one or more TAAs by incorporating TAA gene fragments into eukaryotic expression vectors. Once introduced into host cells, the recombinant DNA vector enables the expression of TAAs. This expression stimulates the immune system, particularly activating CTLs, which elicit a targeted antitumor immune response. Upon injection, the DNA vaccine undergoes transcription and translation within APCs, which then produce the corresponding tumor antigens. These antigenic proteins are processed and presented on MHC-I and MHC-II molecules, activating both CD8+ and CD4+ T cells, and initiating a specific immune response. DNA vaccines have been explored for the treatment of various cancers, including breast, prostate, and cervical cancer, though most are still in phase I/II clinical trials.[ ] Despite significant research, the clinical application of DNA tumor vaccines has been limited due to their poor immunogenicity. This challenge arises from the requirement for DNA to integrate into the host genome for expression. The variability in cell and nuclear membrane structures among individuals leads to inconsistent transfection efficiencies, further hindering the effectiveness of DNA vaccines.[ ] In addition, DNA tumor vaccines may induce immune tolerance mechanisms such as T-cell exhaustion, antigen tolerance, or the infiltration of immunosuppressive cells. There is also a risk of triggering autoimmune responses or unintended integration into the host genome. Moreover, the heterogeneity of tumor antigens complicates the design and application of DNA vaccines. As a result, further research and optimization of vaccine design and efficacy validation are necessary to improve their clinical potential.

## Applications in improving chemotherapy
Chemotherapy remains a cornerstone of systemic cancer treatment, primarily relying on cytotoxic drugs. However, traditional chemotherapeutics have several limitations, including poor selectivity, high toxicity, and systemic administration, which often harms healthy tissues and causes severe side effects.[ ] Furthermore, challenges such as tumor drug resistance and poor water solubility of many chemotherapeutic agents significantly reduce their clinical efficacy. The emergence of nanomedicines offers promising solutions to these limitations. Nanomedicines address issues inherent to small-molecule drugs, such as poor solubility, rapid metabolism, unstable absorption, and limited permeability. They also enable tumor-specific targeting through ligand modification, thereby enhancing therapeutic outcomes while minimizing off-target effects. Several nano-chemotherapeutic drugs have already gained clinical approval, showcasing their potential to improve traditional chemotherapy [Figure ]. Notably, as previously mentioned, Doxil® and Abraxane® are landmark examples of nanocarrier-mediated drug delivery that enhance chemotherapeutic efficacy [Figure ]. Doxil®, the first FDA-approved nanomedicine in 1995, is a liposome-encapsulated DOX used for the treatment of ovarian cancer, AIDS-related Kaposi’s sarcoma, and various solid tumors. By improving drug distribution in vivo, Doxil® significantly reduces the cardiotoxicity of DOX while enhancing its therapeutic efficacy against tumors.[ ] Abraxane®, which uses human serum albumin as a drug carrier, stably encapsulates paclitaxel within nanoparticles. This not only improves the water solubility of paclitaxel but also facilitates tumor-specific targeting.[ ] Yang et al[ ] developed a nanocarrier-mediated drug delivery system using exosomes derived from tumor cells through exocytosis to encapsulate porous silicon nanoparticles (E-PSiNPs) for targeted cancer chemotherapy. E-PSiNPs exhibit strong targeting capabilities toward tumor cells and cancer stem cells. Moreover, DOX-loaded E-PSiNPs (DOX@E-PSiNPs) enhance drug penetration into tumor vasculature and retention within tumor tissues, thereby improving chemotherapy efficacy.[ ] Nanocarrier-mediated drug delivery is constructed with nanotechnology, and carries conventional chemotherapy drugs. Through a dual mechanism of passive diffusion and active targeting, drug delivery facilitates precise drug accumulation in tumor tissues. This innovative approach not only significantly enhances the biocompatibility and solubility of the drug but also imparts a novel potentiation effect to conventional chemotherapy agents, while dramatically reducing side effects. It demonstrates a promising breakthrough in clinical applications, with the potential to reshape cancer treatment paradigms.

## Applications in improving radiotherapy and phototherapy
Radiotherapy is among the most widely used and effective cancer treatment modalities, utilized by over 50% of patients with cancer either as a standalone therapy or in combination with other treatments.[ , ] This localized approach employs high-energy ionizing radiation to target tumors, with two primary mechanisms: direct damage to cellular DNA via strand breaks and indirect production of ROS through interactions with water or oxygen molecules. ROS induce DNA strand breaks, protein carbonylation, and lipid peroxidation, leading to endoplasmic reticulum stress, mitochondrial membrane permeabilization, plasma membrane disruption, and subsequent activation of programmed cell death pathways (e.g., apoptosis, ferroptosis). Excessive ROS-induced depletion of glutathione (GSH), surpasses cellular antioxidant defenses, and immunogenic cell death (ICD) is initiated. ICD is characterized by the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and tumor-associated antigens (TAAs), which synergistically enhance DC-mediated antigen cross-presentation and stimulate macrophage-derived tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α secretion. These events collectively drive CTL infiltration, thereby reversing the immunosuppressive TME and establishing systemic immune memory against tumors.[ , , ] Radiotherapy is categorized into external beam radiotherapy (EBRT) and internal radioisotope therapy (RIT). However, its effectiveness is often limited by the low absorption of radiation by tumor cells, necessitating high radiation doses that can severely harm surrounding healthy tissues.[ ] Moreover, the hypoxic TME and high levels of antioxidants like glutathione (GSH) contribute to radiation resistance. GSH, with its potent ROS-scavenging properties, neutralizes ROS before they inflict damage, reducing the efficacy of radiotherapy.[ ] Developing radiotherapy sensitizers is crucial for enhancing tumor responsiveness, reducing required radiation doses, and minimizing side effects.
Radiotherapy sensitizers are broadly classified into metallic and nonmetallic formulations. Metallic sensitizers primarily exploit high-atomic-number materials, such as gold, gadolinium, titanium, hafnium, and tantalum [Figure , bottom left].[ ] Recent advancements have highlighted ferrite-based nanomaterials, such as Fe3O4 and Fe2O3, which catalyze intracellular H2O2 to produce highly reactive ROS, thereby enhancing tumor cell death and radiosensitivity.[ ] A notable example is NBTXR3, a hafnium dioxide nanoparticle formulation clinically approved for treating locally advanced soft tissue sarcoma [Figure ]. Administered intratumorally, NBTXR3 is activated by radiotherapy, triggering extensive tumor cell death and adaptive immune responses.[ ] Another promising development is AVPt@HP@M, a pH-responsive, tumor-targeting nanomaterial designed by Gong et al.[ ] This biomimetic nanoplatform is based on hollow polydopamine (HP) nanoparticles integrated with platinum nanoparticles (Pt@HP) and modified with cancer cell membranes (@M), enabling homologous targeting, pH-responsive drug release, and loading of apoptin (AP) and verteporfin (VP) in mesoporous shells. When combined with radiotherapy, AVPt@HP@M significantly boosts intracellular ROS levels, disrupts DNA repair mechanisms such as homologous recombination, alters purine metabolism, and enhances overall therapeutic efficacy.[ ]
Phototherapy is a noninvasive and highly efficient tumor treatment method that primarily encompasses photothermal therapy (PTT) and photodynamic therapy (PDT).[ ] PTT is an emerging cancer treatment that utilizes materials with high photothermal conversion efficiency, such as gold nanorods and carbon nanotubes, to convert light energy into heat upon exposure to specific wavelengths, typically NIR light. When these materials accumulate in or around tumor tissues, localized irradiation raises the temperature rapidly, inducing irreversible damage to cancer cell membranes and selectively ablating tumor lesions.[ ] PDT operates based on the photodynamic effect. Tumor sites are irradiated with specific wavelengths of light, which excite photosensitizers from their ground state to an energized state. This process generates cytotoxic ROS that damage cellular components such as membranes, mitochondria, and nucleic acids, ultimately inducing cancer cell death.[ ] Nanocarrier-mediated drug delivery significantly enhances the efficacy of phototherapy by improving the accumulation of photosensitizers in tumors and reducing off-target effects [Figure , bottom right]. For instance, Ini@PM HP, developed by Feng et al,[ ] enables tumor targeting following intravenous injection. It delivers a PARP inhibitor, disrupting DNA repair mechanisms and promoting tumor cell apoptosis. Furthermore, it facilitates in situ oxygen generation through reactions between photosensitizers and endogenous H2O2, alleviating tumor hypoxia and improving PDT outcomes. Additionally, with its high photothermal conversion efficiency, Ini@PM HP supports effective PTT under 808 nm laser irradiation.[ ] The development of nanomaterials has opened new possibilities for enhancing tumor phototherapy. A key area of future research will focus on integrating antitumor drugs and photosensitizers into multifunctional nanomaterials, enabling synergistic therapies that combine PTT and PDT for improved therapeutic outcomes [Figures and ].
 

## Conclusions, Ongoing Challenges, and Future Perspectives
Nanomaterials enable precise tumor targeting and therapeutic stabilization through inherent biosafety and biocompatibility profiles. They show considerable promise in translating scientific breakthroughs into clinical solutions by enhancing the efficacy of immunotherapy, mitigating chemotherapy-induced toxicity, and reducing radiation-related side effects. Indeed, novel therapeutic approaches using specific nanomaterials—such as PTT and PDT—have already been implemented in clinical practice.[ , ] However, the success of these strategies hinges not only on continuous advances in nanomaterial synthesis, but also on the progressive integration of bioengineering with clinical oncology.
Despite these advantages, the practical application of nanomaterials as drug carriers still faces notable limitations. Chief among these are concerns regarding their long-term stability, clinical efficacy, and safety profiles. Although many nanoparticles are engineered to evade immune recognition and prolong circulation, they may nonetheless provoke unintended immune responses or degrade into reactive byproducts. For example, cationic LNPs can exhibit cytotoxicity by disrupting cell membranes or triggering oxidative stress,[ , , , ] and nondegradable inorganic nanoparticles may accumulate in organs such as the liver or spleen, raising issues about chronic toxicity.[ , , ] In addition, questions remain about whether certain nanomaterials can traverse biological barriers or inadvertently integrate into off-target tissues, posing risks of systemic side effects.[ ] Cost-effectiveness and scalability also represent major hurdles;[ ] balancing broad-spectrum therapies, which inherently risk greater side effects, with highly specialized treatments targeted to smaller patient populations, remains a complex strategic challenge for both researchers and healthcare stakeholders.
From the perspective of the TME, tumor cells dynamically remodel their surroundings and orchestrate immune and stromal cells to favor tumor growth.[ , , ] Many nanomedicines aim to exploit these TME features, as exemplified by pH-sensitive release systems[ ] and carriers specifically targeting tumor-associated cells and stromal elements.[ , ] Other formulations address immunosuppression by modulating the functional states of T cells or DCs, repolarizing macrophages,[ ] or presenting tumor-associated or tumor-specific antigens.[ ] Working in tandem with TME research, these nanomedicine approaches seek to restore or enhance local anti-tumor mechanisms, thereby minimizing systemic toxicity and the risk of cytokine storms.[ ] We thus envision that nanomedicines will continue to flourish as core components of more localized and precise antitumor strategies.
Nonetheless, the potential influence of nanomedicines on systemic cancer therapies remains significant. The success of mRNA vaccines in combating infectious diseases, notably through LNP platforms, highlights the feasibility of integrating advanced nanotechnology for systemic immunomodulation. By encoding tumor-specific antigens, mRNA vaccines could potentially be adapted for the treatment of metastatic solid tumors, thereby expanding the reach of nanomedicine beyond localized regimens.[ , ] Since the immune system governs surveillance, clearance, regulation, and memory, both immunodeficiency and hyperactive immunity can heighten the risk of cancer. Consequently, leveraging nanomedicines to achieve balanced immune responses—for example, via prophylactic or therapeutic vaccines against viruses such as human papillomavirus (HPV) or Epstein–Barr virus (EBV)—constitutes a promising approach. Encouraging findings from various preclinical and clinical cancer vaccine studies reinforce the notion that nanomedicines have immense potential to enhance both the prevention and treatment of malignancies.
Future research must address three critical translational barriers in large-scale manufacturing, long-term biodynamic profiles, and healthcare economic viability to achieve broad clinical adoption. Strategic integration with next-generation therapies—notably CAR-T cellular platforms and CRISPR-Cas9 gene-editing systems—promises to establish multimodal regimens for metastatic containment. By aligning biomaterials innovations, tumor microenvironment reprogramming strategies, and clinical implementation challenges, nanomedicines are positioned to transform oncological care via precision-targeted systems coordinating locoregional eradication with systemic disease surveillance.
