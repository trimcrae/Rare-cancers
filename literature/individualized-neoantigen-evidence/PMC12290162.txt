## Abstract
Plasmid DNA vectors are emerging as a versatile antigen delivery platform for personalized cancer vaccines. Here, we report a comprehensive preclinical evaluation of a modular DNA cancer vaccine that targets antigen-presenting cells and encodes tumor-specific antigens (TSAs), including neoantigens and endogenous retroviral (ERV) antigens. To specifically direct the TSAs to APCs, the chemokine C-C motif ligand 19 (CCL19) was incorporated, enhancing the magnitude and persistence of antigen-specific CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell responses. This resulted in strong anti-tumor activity in multiple murine models. Delivery via clinically relevant DNA methods, including electroporation and needle-free injection systems, further improved immune responses compared to standard syringe/needle injection. Pharmacokinetic and toxicological analyses of the CCL19 component demonstrated rapid systemic clearance and an absence of adverse effects, supporting its suitability for clinical application. The platform exhibited a favorable safety profile across repeated administrations at clinically relevant and escalated dosing regimens, including in combination with immune checkpoint blockade. Furthermore, in vitro and in vivo evaluation of multiple antigens confirmed consistent protein expression and sustained immunogenicity, irrespective of antigenic composition, underscoring the platform’s robustness and translational potential for individualized cancer vaccine strategies. These findings demonstrate that the CCL19-targeted DNA vaccine platform is both immunologically potent and holds strong potential in personalized cancer therapy.
Keywords: CCL19, Antigen presenting cell targeted immunotherapy, DNA cancer vaccine, Neoantigens, Endogenous retroviruses (ERVs)

## Introduction
Cancer immunotherapy has emerged as a promising therapy for treating various types of cancer by harnessing the body's immune system to selectively target and eliminate tumor cells.
A primary objective of cancer immunotherapy is to stimulate a specific immune response targeting tumor antigens [ ]. In this context, DNA vaccination represents a straightforward and safe approach for eliciting the desired immune responses through in vivo expression of delivered antigen-encoding DNA constructs. DNA vaccines are engineered to deliver one or multiple genes including tumor antigens or immune modulatory elements, thereby inducing specific immune responses against the encoded targets [ – ].
An effective therapeutic DNA-based cancer vaccine should activate a strong tumor-specific cellular immunity while avoiding toxicity [ ]. However, therapeutic DNA-based cancer vaccines have historically demonstrated variable clinical results, largely attributable to suboptimal antigen selection, backbone plasmid design, and limited transfection efficacy of naked DNA administrated via standard needle injection [ – ]. Vaccine efficacy can be significantly improved not only by selecting appropriate tumor antigens but also by implementing strategies for improving antigen presentation and immunogenicity, such as new delivery systems aiming to improve DNA transfection, addition of molecular adjuvants that modulate the induced immune responses, optimized prime-boost dosing schedules, and combination with immune checkpoint inhibitors (CPIs) [ – ].
The personalized cancer therapy field has been working on finding effective cancer antigens where a vaccine can induce a potent and specific immune response against tumor-specific antigens (TSAs) [ ]. TSAs are unique to the tumor cells, absent in healthy tissue. TSAs include, among others, neoantigens, which are derived from non-synonymous mutations, resulting in novel protein sequences, and endogenous retroviral (ERV) antigens, which are ancient retroviral element incorporated in the human genome and reactivated in transformed malignant cells [ – ]. TSAs are presented by MHC molecules exclusively on the surface of tumor cells and not healthy cells, which constitute effective tumor targets for the induction of a cytotoxic T-cell response and tumor elimination [ , ].
Current optimized DNA vaccines often incorporate an immunomodulatory molecule alongside the antigens of interest. This molecular adjuvant can be encoded within the same plasmids as the antigens or co-delivered in a separate construct. These molecules may include cytokines, chemokines or co-stimulatory and signaling molecules. Cytokines and chemokines have been assessed for their potential as immune stimulators. Interleukin (IL)-2 and GM-CSF have historically been the most commonly used cytokines to improve immune response [ ], and chemokines such as CCL3, CCL5, CCL19 and CCL21 have been co-administered with DNA-encoded antigens for improved immune response [ – ].
Another common approach to improve DNA vaccine efficacy in the clinic is the use of delivery devices that improve DNA transfection of cells at the immunization site [ ]. Such methods include needle-free injection systems (NFIS) that deliver DNA into cells via a high-velocity, fine stream of fluid and electroporation (EP), which uses electrical currents to achieve the same effect [ – ].
Evaxion’s first-generation personalized DNA vaccine EVX-02, which encodes 13 patient tumor-specific neoantigens in the pTVG4 plasmid, was tested for safety and efficacy in a phase I clinical study for treatment of adjuvant melanoma. EVX-02, delivered with the Stratis NFIS, in combination with nivolumab was well tolerated and gave rise to robust T-cell responses in a phase I clinical study in resected melanoma patients (NCT04455503), demonstrating that delivery of patient-specific neoantigens is feasible with a DNA vaccine approach [ ].
To increase the potency of the DNA vaccine approach used in EVX-02, a next-generation DNA vaccine was developed, incorporating some of the above-mentioned approaches, including chemokine C-C motif ligand 19 (CCL19) as an APCt unit linked to the antigenic cassette through a human IgG3-derived dimerization domain, h1h4CH3, which generates a fusion protein product [ ]. CCL19 was chosen from a larger screening study aiming at finding a promising APCt motif for potentiating delivery of neoantigens in a DNA vaccine.
The APCt vaccine design for human use will contain human CCL19 as APCt, the dimerization domain h1h4CH3 and a personalized antigenic cassette encoding 13 TSAs. The antigenic cassette containing personalized TSAs is designed with Evaxion’s proprietary GxP-validated computational platform, AI-Immunology™. The platform identifies expressed neoantigens and ERV antigens matched to the patient’s HLA based on sequencing data of the patient´s tumor and healthy tissue and ranks them according to the predicted ability to induce a specific T-cell response. The 13 top-ranked TSAs are selected for the final antigen cassette, which encodes the 13 sequences for 27 amino acids (AA) per antigen as beads on a string connected by GC linkers. This antigenic cassette is synthesized and cloned into the final vaccine design containing CCL19 and the dimerization domain. This is the final vaccine product that is intended for the treatment of cancer patients to elicit a potent and specific immune response against the tumor.
This next-generation APCt DNA vaccine is intended to be administered in combination with CPIs with the aim of improving the clinical outcome of cancer patients receiving checkpoint inhibitors (CPI) as standard of care. Although CPIs have improved cancer treatment and are FDA-approved for many solid cancers, still a large fraction of patients who remain refractory to the treatment, which underscores the potential of cancer vaccines to synergize with this immunotherapy modality reaching to a larger patient population [ ].
Here we performed additional non-clinical studies in the BALB/c syngeneic tumor model using the APCt DNA concept, to bridge the gap between previous pre-clinical evaluation and clinical use by addressing preclinical safety evaluation, clinically relevant dosing schedules including vaccine boosters, as well as the therapeutic effect of combining the APCt DNA vaccine with CPIs.

## Methods

## Design of neoantigen and ERV encoding pDNA vaccines and tumor-specific antigen (TSA) selection (human + murine)
The DNA plasmids used in this study were derived from previously published vectors encoding five or thirteen 27-mer in silico predicted TSAs, neoantigens or ERVs antigens from the mouse tumor cell line CT26 [ , ] or human melanoma samples from the EVX-02 clinical trial (NCT04455503). The human neoantigen sequences were selected from human clinical derived melanoma samples sorted based on predicted presentation on BALB/c MHC molecules upon vaccination of mice. These TSAs were linked by (glycine-serine (GS))5 as poly-epitopes on a string and are referred to as Neo5, Neo13, Erv13 or Neo5/Erv8.
The CT26 neoantigens were selected using Evaxion’s computational model PIONEER™. PIONEER™ takes as input whole-exome sequencing (WES) and RNA sequencing of tumor samples and WES of a matched healthy sample to identify and rank neoantigens. PIONEER™ ranks the neoantigens based on the integration of a series of neoantigen-specific features that are required for an effective vaccine target [ ]. One of the features is represented by prediction of neoantigen presentation on the subject’s MHC molecules. PIONEER™ prioritizes neoantigens sequences that are expressed in the tumor and predicted to contain both an MHC class I and a class II ligand, enabling the induction of both CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell responses. A similar antigen presentation prioritization is employed by ObsERV™ for ranking ERV antigen-derived sequences [ ]. In this study, either 5 or 13 TSA epitopes were encoded in the mCCL19-CH3 plasmid. For a more detailed description of the in silico TSA prediction and immunotherapy design, see Viborg et al. [ ], for the selection of Neoantigen and Garde et al. [ ] for the ERV selection.
Next, the APC-targeting pDNA constructs were designed with three main components: an APC-binding molecule, a dimerization module consisting of hinge 1, hinge 4 and the CH3 domain (h1h4CH3) from human IgG3, and the poly-neoantigen unit consisting of five or thirteen TSAs. Each module was fused by glycine–leucine (GL). The sequences of the APC-binding molecule, including the selection process of the chemokine CCL19, are described in Barrio-Calvo et al. [ ].
All DNA insert sequences were optimized for codon adaptation in mice, GC content, repeated sequences and mRNA-free energy using previously described tools [ ]. The DNA inserts were synthesized and subcloned into the expression vectors pTVG4 using EcoRI and NotI sites and upscaled by Aldevron (US) as research-grade DNA.
The four human melanoma plasmid designs were synthesized by Aldevron, and the sequences were verified by Sanger sequencing. The different gene inserts (human model antigen cassettes containing 13 neoantigens of 27-mer length and spaced by GC linkers) were inserted into a pTGV4 plasmid backbone containing the APCt cassette comprising hCCL19 and a h1h4CH3 dimerization domain. For expression detection, an N-terminal 6 × His-tag was introduced. To enhance product expression from the inserts, the neoantigen pDNA designs were upscaled at Cobra Biologics (UK) as clinical-grade DNA.

## DNA vaccine formulation and immunization schedule
The DNA plasmids for research and clinical use were prepared with the co-block polymer poloxamer 188 (P188) as described by Viborg et al. [ ]. The majority of the in vivo experiments were performed with a standard needle injection intramuscularly (i.m.) in both hind leg muscles (tibialis anterior, 50 µl in each leg) where the DNA was co-formulated with P188 (Kolliphor®, BASF) in 1xPBS to a final concentration of 3% P188.
For testing clinically relevant delivery devices, pDNA was prepared in PBS and delivered either by electroporation (EP) using the AgilePulse device (BTX, Harvard) immediately after i.m. injection or by a modified version of the Tropis® NFIS (PharmaJet, Golden, CO, USA) which has been altered to deliver 50 µl of naked pDNA dissolved in PBS intramuscularly into mice. Both EP and Tropis® delivery methods were employed for clinically relevant dosing schedules and yielded similar results.

## Tumor cell line and tumor challenge
The syngeneic colon cancer cell line CT26 from BALB/c mice (ATCC, #CRL2638) was grown in RPMI media (Gibco, #72400-021) with 10% heat-inactivated fetal calf serum (FCS) (Gibco, #10500-064). The HEK293T cell line (ATCC, #CRL-1573) was maintained in DMEM media (Merck, #D6546) with 10% FCS, 1% GlutaMAX (ThermoFisher, #35050061) and 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin. The CHO-K1 cell line (ATCC, #CCL-61) was cultivated in RPMI media with 10% FCS and 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin. All cell lines were incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO2, following the supplier’s guidelines. All cell lines were routinely checked for mycoplasma.
As previously described, tumor challenges were performed. On Study Day 0, mice received either 5 × 105 or 2.5 × 105 CT26 cells subcutaneously (s.c.) in their right flank in serum-free media. Tumors were measured three times a week with a caliper and calculated using the formula: tumor volume = (π/6)*(diameter1*diameter2)^(3/2). Experiments were terminated when most of the tumors in the control groups reached a 15 mm diameter in any direction. Mice were euthanized upon reaching humane endpoints (e.g., 15% loss of body weight or tumor ulcerations). Missing data for longitudinal tumor sizes were handled using Last Observation Carried Forward (LOCF).

## Safety scoring/evaluation of well-being of the animals
To ensure the safety and well-being of the animals, a comprehensive evaluation was conducted. This included monitoring key parameters such as injection site reaction (ISR) scores, biopsy analysis, bodyweight monitoring and observation of injection site skin inflammation. The ISR score was used to quantitatively assess localized responses and detect any potential adverse effects. Biopsy analysis provided detailed insights into tissue responses, aiding in the identification of microscopic changes. Continuous monitoring of bodyweight served as a systemic indicator, ensuring the well-being of mice under extreme dosages. This thorough assessment aimed to guarantee the safety and tolerance of the mCCL19-CH3-Neo13 platform.

## Blood and serum collection
Blood samples were collected from the tail veins of mice at various time points throughout the study. The serum was separated by centrifugation and stored at − 80 °C for further analysis. These samples were used for various assays to monitor immune responses and biomarker levels. Whole blood was used for multimer staining of the C1 neoepitope, while serum was used for multiplex analysis (MSD) analysis.

## Multimer staining
To monitor CD8+ T-cell immune recognition of specific vaccine epitopes in the blood of mice, we used the MHC class I multimer staining methodology, as described previously [ ]. In particular, we used PE-conjugated MHC tetramers comprising H-2Kd molecules loaded with the restricted minimal epitope KFKASRASI derived from the vaccine epitope C1 (Tetramer Shop, cat H-2Kd (KFKASRASI), from hereon: C1 multimer, 5 µl per sample). Tail vein blood was collected in EDTA-coated tubes (Sarstedt, #20.1278.100) and then transferred to deep, 96-well plates (Sigma, #575653). Cells were incubated with FcR-blocking anti-CD16/32 (Biolegend, #101301, 1:50) for 10 min at 4 °C. Prior to use, multimer reagents were centrifuged at 3300 g for 5 min and then added in each blood sample before incubation at 37 °C for 15 min. Cells were then surface stained for CD3e (FITC, Biolegend #100305, 1:800), CD4 (PE-Cy7, BD #552775, 1:600) and CD8 (BV786, BD #563332, 1:250) for 30 min at 4 °C. Finally, cells underwent one-step fixation/red blood cell lysis (eBioscience, #00-5333-57) before acquisition on FACS Celesta. For the determination of MHC multimer + CD8 + T-cells, FlowJo software (version 10.8.0) was used. The gating strategy is performed as previously described in Viborg et. al. (all, lymphocytes, single cells, live cells, CD3e+ , CD8+ and C1 multimer) [ ].

## Cytokine multiplex analysis
A cytokine multiplex analysis was performed using a mesoscale discovery (MSD) custom mouse U-plex assay (K15069M, MSD) to screen multiple cytokines (TNF-α, KC/GRO, IL-6, MCP-1, IL-12, IL-2, IL-17, IL-1b, IL-6 and IL-10). The assay was performed according to manufacturer’s instructions with samples not diluted. MESO QuickPlex SQ 120 (MSD) at the Flow Cytometry and Single Cell Core Facility at UCPH was used to read the plates. Serum samples were collected in series consisting of four samples collected before, 6 h, 24 h and 7 days after immunizations. MSD Discovery Workbench software was used to determine cytokine concentrations using a 4-parameter logistic nonlinear regression analysis of the standard curve. Values lower than the limit of detection for each cytokine were set to 0.

## Spleen isolation
At the end of the studies, spleens from 5 to 7 mice, as specified in figure legend, were removed and placed in RPMI with 10% FCS (Gibco, #10500–064), broken down into single cells using GentleMACS processing (Miltenyi Biotec, C-tubes, #130-096-334 and Dissociater, #130-093-235), and frozen.

## Peptide production for re-stimulation assays
Synthetic TSA neoantigen or ERV: 27-mer peptides, matching the TSAs encoded in the pDNA constructs, were produced by GenScript G New Jersey, USA or Biosynth (Switzerland), at a purity above 85%. The 27-mer peptides have the mutated AA in the middle position. The dried peptides were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (Merck, #D8418) at 10 mg/mL for peptide re-exposure.

## Peptide re-stimulation and enzyme-linked ImmunoSPOT (ELISPOT)
T-cell reactivity against vaccine-encoded epitopes was measured ex vivo using the ELISPOT assay, as described previously [ ]. In brief, 5 × 105 of viable splenocytes suspended in R10 medium [RPMI 1640 (Fisher Scientific #72400021) + 10% FBS (Fisher Scientific #10500064)] were plated on an ELISPOT plate (Merck Millipore #MAIP4510) coated overnight with 5 μg/mL anti-murine IFN-γ capture antibody (BD #51-2525KZ). Splenocytes were re-stimulated with synthetic vaccine-corresponding 27-mer peptides, peptide pools or left unstimulated (R10 medium only).
Cells were stimulated for 20 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2. IFN-γ produced by peptide-specific T-cells upon reactivation and captured on an anti-IFN-γ capture antibody was visualized through the sequential addition of 2 μg/mL biotinylated anti-IFN-γ detection antibody (BD #51-1818KA), Streptavidin–Horseradish Peroxidase (HRP) (BD #557630) and AEC chromogen substrate (BD #551951) as per the manufacturer's protocol. Following overnight drying, the ELISPOT plates were imaged using an ELISPOT reader (Cellular Technology Ltd.), and the bound IFN-γ was quantified as spot forming units (SFUs) per 1 × 106 splenocytes.

## Peptide re-stimulation and intracellular cytokine staining (ICS)
The assay was conducted as described previously [ ]. To characterize neoantigens and ERV-reactive CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell responses induced by DNA vaccination, 2 × 106 viable splenocytes in single-cell suspension were plated in round-bottomed, 96-well culture plates (Corning #3799) in RPMI 1640 medium (Fisher Scientific #72400021) supplemented with heat-inactivated FBS (Fisher Scientific #10500064). Cells were stimulated with synthetic peptide pools containing CT26-specific neoantigens or ERV epitopes corresponding to vaccine contents or with individual vaccine epitopes (peptide pools at 5 μg/ml final concentration of each peptide). As negative controls, five samples were incubated with media only (no stimulation). As positive assay controls, two samples were stimulated with leukocyte activation cocktail (LAC, BD #550583). Protein transport was inhibited by the addition of brefeldin A (GolgiPlug BD #555029) and monensin (GolgiStop BD #554724) 2 h after the initiation of stimulation, followed by overnight incubation at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Cells were then washed and incubated with the FcR-blocking anti-murine CD16/32 antibody (Biolegend #101301, 1:50) at 4 °C for 10 min, followed by staining for murine CD3e (FITC, Biolegend #100305, 1:800), CD4 (PE-Cy7, BD #552775, 1:800), CD8 (BV786, BD #563332, 1:250) and viability dye (GloCellTM Fixable Viability Dye, Stem Cell Tech #75010, 1:1000) at 4 °C for 30 min. Cells were then fixed (eBioscience #00-8222-49) at 4 °C for 20 min and permeabilized (eBioscience #00-8333-56) before staining for intracellular IFN-γ (BV650, BD #563854, 1:1050) and TNF-α (BV421, BD #566287, 1:1050) at 4 °C for 30 min. Stained cells were acquired on FACS Celesta (BD), and frequencies of cytokine-producing CD4+ and CD8+ T-cells were determined using FlowJo software (version 10.8.0). Samples with less than 1000 counts of CD8+ cells were excluded from the final analysis. The gating strategy was performed as previously described in Viborg et al. (all, lymphocytes, single cells, live cells, CD3e+ , CD4+ or CD8+ , and a split plot of TNF-α and/or IFN-γ) [ ].

## Expression screening assays

## pDNA transfection
HEK293T cells (#CRL-1573, ATCC) were seeded in tissue culture-treated 6-well plates (cat#140675, ThermoScientific) in a density of 0.25 × 106 cells/well. Transient transfection of plasmid DNA (1 µg total) was mediated by using Lipofectamine 2000 (ThermoFisher, #L3000015) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For subsequent analyses either the supernatant or cells were collected 48 h post-transfection for protein and total RNA extraction.

## CCL19 sandwich ELISA
Detection of human CCL19 in supernatant of transfected HEK293T cells was performed using a human CCL19 DuoSet sandwich ELISA (R&D systems, #DY361). hCCL19/MIP-3β antibody pairs were used to capture and detect fusion proteins. Plates were developed using DuoSet ELISA Ancillary Reagent Kit 2 (R&D Systems, #DY008) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and colorimetric signal was measured using the ELISA plate reader Epoch2 (BioTek).

## RNA isolation
Total RNA was extracted from transfected HEK293T cells using the Qiagen RNAeasy kit (#74104, Qiagen) following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentration and purity was assessed using a NanoDrop One spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher).

## cDNA synthesis
For complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis 1 µg of total RNA was reverse transcribed using the Taqman reverse transcription kit (#N8080234, ThermoFisher) according to manufacturer´s instructions using random primers.

## RT-qPCR
Real-time quantitative PCR was performed using the FastStart SYBR green master mix (#04673484001, ThermoFisher) on a Real-time qPCR system (PCR max Eco 48). The housekeeping reference GAPDH (fw 5′GTCTCCTCTGACTTCAACAGCG 3´and rev 5´ ACCACCCTGTTGCTGTAGCCAA 3´) and the gene-specific primers were used in a final concentration of 0.3 µM. The following cycling conditions were used: denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s, annealing at 60 °C for 30 s, extension at 72 °C for 30 s, for 40 cycles. Relative gene expression was analyzed using the delta Ct method with GAPDH as the reference control.

## SDS-PAGE and immunoblotting
Total protein lysates were prepared from transfected HEK293T cells by lysing cells in RIPA buffer (#89900, ThermoFisher) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (#A32953, Pierce) on ice for 15 min. The cell lysates were centrifuged at 13.000 pm for 10 min at 4 °C (accuSpin Micro 17, FisherScientific) and the supernatants collected. Equal amounts of protein lysates were resolved on an SDS-PAGE gel gradient 3–15% (#4568093, Bio-Rad) and transferred onto a 0.45-µM PVDF membrane (#88518, ThermoScientific) using semi-dry transfer (Power Blotter Statin, Invitrogen). Membranes were blocked with 1 × Pierce milk blocking solution (#37587, ThermoScientific) for 2 h at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against 6 × His-tag (0.1 µg/ml, #MA5-33032, ThermoFisher). For signal development membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (#HF008, Novus Biologicals, dilution 1:1000) for 1 h at room temperature and Pierce ECL substrate (#10095983, ThermoScientific). Chemiluminescence imaging was performed using the iBrite FL1500 system (ThermoScientific).

## Statistical analyses
GraphPad Prism 10 for Mac OS X was used for graphs, statistical analyses and tools. Data were subjected to the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test for normality (alpha = 0.05). Parametric data were analyzed by ordinary ANOVA with Sidak’s multiple comparison correction. Non-parametric data were analyzed by the Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s multiple comparison correction. For the tests described above, the following levels of statistical significance were applied: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.001, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001.

## CCL19-augmented vaccination promotes early tumor-specific T-cell response onset and improves therapeutic efficacy
We have previously demonstrated that the APCt vaccine design encoding mCCL19 and a dimerization unit linked to a neoantigen cassette (hereafter referred to as mCCL19-CH3-Neo13) induces superior T-cell responses and anti-tumor efficacy compared to a cancer DNA vaccine encoding an identical antigen cassette containing 13 neoantigens (C1 to C13) identified in CT26 tumor cells (hereafter referred to as Neo13) but lacking the APCt component [ ].
To gain deeper insight into the specific stages of the immune response modulated by the APCt component focusing on the timing and magnitude of the vaccine-induced antigen-specific T-cell response, we performed a dose titration study in the CT26 tumor model in BALB/c mice. The two DNA vaccine constructs (mCCL19-CH3-Neo13 and Neo13) were administered at three different doses of 1, 2.5 and 5 μg of DNA (Fig.  a). The two vaccines were compared by assessing tumor growth inhibition, kinetics of antigen-specific T-cell induction and the frequency of activated neoantigen-specific T-cells at study endpoint (Fig.  ).
 Mice were immunized weekly five times from Study Day -13 up until Study Day 15 followed by CT26 tumor cell inoculated s.c. on Study Day 0 (Fig.  b). Both DNA vaccines reduced tumor growth dose dependently. The group immunized with the APCt vaccine mCCL19-CH3-Neo13 developed significantly smaller tumors than those immunized with the Neo13 vaccine at corresponding doses (Fig.  c, d). A mCCL19-CH3-Neo13 dose as low as 1 μg obtained a significantly improved anti-tumor effect as compared to the Neo13 vaccine at the same dose. A dose of 5 μg of Neo13 was required to mediate a comparable anti-tumor effect as for 1 μg of mCCL19-CH3-Neo13 which substantiates the addition of the APC-targeting unit increasing the potency of the DNA vaccine up to fivefold (Fig.  d).
To facilitate a longitudinal assessment of the vaccine-induced T-cell response, blood samples were collected on Study Days 1, 8 and 13. The circulating neoantigen-specific CD8+ T-cells were quantified using MHC I multimers loaded with the minimal epitope corresponding to C1, the first neoantigen in the antigen cassette (Fig.  a).
Immunization with 1 µg of mCCL19-CH3-Neo13 induced C1-specific T-cells (2.5% C1-positive CD8+ T-cells) already at Study Day 1 at levels significantly higher than in mice treated with Neo13 (< 0.5% C1-positive CD8+ T-cells) at the same time point and dose (Fig.  e). Mice treated with 5 µg of Neo13 did not show C1-specific T-cells in circulation above the background level until Study Day 8 (Fig.  f). These levels of induced T-cells correspond with the observed anti-tumor effect shown in C. At Study Day 13, the C1-specific T-cell levels were still significantly higher for mice receiving the APCt vaccine design as compared to Neo13 (Fig.  g) at matching doses.
At the study endpoint (Study Day 22), functional antigen-specific T-cells were assessed by measuring cytokine-producing CD4+ and CD8+ T-cells following ex vivo stimulation of splenocytes with the corresponding vaccine epitopes. Comparable frequencies of antigen-specific CD4+ T-cells were detected in samples from mice treated with the CCL19-CH3-Neo13 vaccine and the Neo13 vaccine for all three doses (Fig.  h). A tendency toward increased frequencies of antigen-reactive CD8+ T-cells induced by CCL19-CH3-Neo13 vaccine was observed as compared to the levels induced by Neo13. However, this was only significant for the groups dosed with 2.5 μg, suggesting that the differences in immune responses elicited by both plasmids are restricted to the time onset as their magnitude even out over the course of the study (Fig.  i).
Altogether, the multimer staining and ICS data indicate that the superior antitumor effect observed with the APC-targeted vaccine could be attributed to the earlier onset of antigen-specific T-cells, induced by mCCL19-CH3-Neo13, with CCL19 as the APC-targeting unit.

## The antigen-specific T-cell response of the APCt DNA vaccine is potentiated by DNA delivery devices
To evaluate DNA vaccine delivery devices for clinical use, we investigated FDA-approved DNA delivery systems, including an NFIS and electroporation (EP)-assisted immunization, reported to enhance in vivo DNA transfection and expression [ ]. The spring-powered NFIS uses a high-velocity stream of fluid to propel molecules that would normally not cross a cell membrane, such as DNA, directly into the cytoplasm and nucleus [ ]. EP relies on short electric pulses, which open the cell membranes temporarily and allow DNA molecules to reach the nucleus [ , ]. We tested the two types of delivery systems in preclinical models: in vivo EP using a BMX device and the modified Tropis® NFIS adapted for use in mice and compared them with standard needle injection of the DNA vaccine formulated with the poloxamer P188 (Fig.  a).
 Mice received weekly immunizations five times on Study Days −13, −6, 0, 8 and 15 followed by s.c. challenge with CT26 tumor cells on Study Day 0. Splenocytes were isolated at termination on Study Day 21 for further T-cell analyses (Fig.  b).
EP-assisted immunization and Tropis®-mediated delivery of mCCL19-CH3-Neo13 prevented tumor growth in all 13 mice; the standard needle administration at the tested dose of 5 µg mCCL19-CH3-Neo13 inhibited tumor growth in 12 of 13 mice which did not lead to conclusive data for the potential enhancement of the induced anti-tumor effect by introducing a delivery device (Fig.  c, d).
In contrast to the tumor data, the analyses of reactive T-cell responses revealed that EP-assisted DNA immunization and Tropis® injection significantly increased the frequency of functional neoantigen-specific CD4+ T-cells (up to 2.5%) (Fig.  e) and CD8+ T-cells (up to 12%) (Fig.  f) when compared to the treatment group immunized via standard needle injection i.m. (approx. 1% and 2.5%, respectively). The levels of functional T-cell responses in mice immunized with the two tested delivery devices were comparable for both CD8+ and CD4+ T-cells (Fig.  e, f). Even though the magnitude of the immune responses is improved by the assistance of the delivery devices, the breadth of the response did not seem to be significantly affected when using EP, as observed by the recall response from neoepitope-specific CD8+ T-cells to the individual antigens from the cassette (Fig.  g). Given the comparable outcomes from EP and Tropis® in anti-tumor effect and immunogenicity the two systems are used interchangeably throughout the study as indicated in the individual figure legends. EP was chosen for the therapeutic setup, as the needle sizes are better optimized for murine dosing.

## Efficacy of the APCt DNA vaccine is potentiated when combined with CPI treatment
Next, we tested whether combining the mCCL19-CH3-Neo13 DNA vaccine with the immune checkpoint inhibitor anti-PD-1 mAB could potentiate the anti-tumor efficacy and the induction of antigen-specific T-cells. For this study, we selected an immunization schedule that usually exhibits a limited anti-tumor effect to observe any potential additive or synergistic effects of the two immunotherapies, as monotherapy would otherwise lead to complete tumor control.
Mice treated with the mCCL19-CH3-Neo13 vaccine plus anti-PD-1 mAb had smaller accumulated tumor sizes over time, calculated as AUC, compared to the tumors of mice administered with no DNA and control mAb; however, the differences were not statistically significant (Fig.  b, c). Combining mCCL19-CH3-Neo13 with anti-PD-1 antibody did not significantly improve the antitumor effect as compared to mCCL19-CH3-Neo13 alone when evaluating the accumulated tumor sizes over the 28 Study Days (Fig.  b).
 On Study Day 15, circulating neoantigen-specific CD8+ T-cells recognizing the C1-neoantigen were detected at similar levels in both groups treated with mCCL19-CH3-Neo13 (Fig.  d). As the first antibody dose was administered only two days earlier (Study Day 13), a pronounced difference in the T-cell response at this early timepoint was not anticipated. At the endpoint on Study Day 28, functional antigen-specific cytokine-secreting CD4+ T-cells were observed in neoantigen-stimulated splenic samples. Although the difference between the combination group (mCCL19-CH3-Neo13+ a-PD-1 mAb) and the mCCL19-CH3-Neo13+ control antibody group did not reach statistical significance, a trend toward an additive effect was evident. Importantly, the combination treatment with a-PD-1 mAb resulted in significantly higher levels of CD4+ T-cells compared to anti-PD-1 monotherapy (Fig.  e). A similar pattern was observed for CD8+ T-cells: Both vaccine-treated groups showed functional antigen-specific responses, with the combination group exhibiting significantly elevated levels relative to the a-PD-1 monotherapy group, though not significantly different from the mCCL19-CH3-Neo13+ control antibody group (Fig.  f). These data suggest that co-administration of the mCCL19-CH3-Neo13 vaccine with PD-1 blockade may enhance the induction of functional antigen-specific CD4+ and CD8+ T-cells.

## Safety evaluation of APC-targeted DNA vaccine including DNA delivery devices and booster dosing regimen
Following the demonstration of the improved potency of the CCL19-carrying vaccine, we next assessed the safety profile of the APCt DNA vaccine. Given that the first-generation DNA vaccine EVX-02, which encodes 13 patient-tailored neoantigens, has been proven safe in humans, and that the dimerization domain has also been tested in clinical studies [ ], we did not anticipate adverse events related to the DNA plasmid, the TSAs or the dimerization domain. Consequently, our safety assessment focused on identifying any potential adverse effect associated with the inclusion of CCL19 in the vaccine. The safety assessment was focused on three parts: pharmacokinetics, safety pharmacology and toxicology.

## Pharmacokinetics
Classical pharmacokinetic studies (absorption, distribution, metabolism, secretion) are not conventionally performed for personalized cancer therapies. However, as systemic long-term exposure to the translated protein product containing the chemokine CCL19 could potentially lead to unwanted side effects triggered by CCL19-mediated engagement of the CCR7 chemokine receptor, we conducted a biodistribution study to investigate the secretion of mCCL19-CH3-Neo13 protein product into the bloodstream and its clearance from i.m. DNA vaccine-immunized mice.
To specifically detect vaccine-derived CCL19, we utilized a vaccine plasmid encoding human CCL19 (hCCL19) and used an ELISA detecting hCCL19 differentiating the vaccine-derived hCCL19 from endogenous mouse CCL19. Prior studies have demonstrated that hCCL19 bind and activate the murine CCR7 receptor with comparable affinity as the mCCL19, and therefore we expect downstream effects, transport and clearance of hCCL19 to be comparable to mCCL19 [ , ].
The hCCL19 backbone plasmid was used as a positive control as the simpler DNA fusion product (without the Neo13 antigen payload) encodes a shorter protein product with a simpler folding and thereby a higher protein expression, as seen in transfected culture cell lines, which is also expected for in vivo transcription. This was shown in HEK293 cells transfected with either hCCL19-CH3 or hCCL19-CH3-Neo13 DNA. Secreted protein levels in the conditioned media were quantified using the hCCL19 ELISA. The simpler construct, hCCL19-CH3, resulted in higher detectable levels of hCCL19 protein compared to the full-length hCCL19-CH3-Neo13 vaccine (Fig.  a).
 To maximize in vivo transfection levels in mice and thereby likelihood of detection of the translated protein product in circulation, EP-assisted immunization with 50 μg of either the full-length APCt vaccine, hCCL19-CH3-Neo13 or with a DNA construct, hCCL19-CH3 was conducted. Analysis of serum from immunized mice using hCCL19 ELISA showed detectable levels of hCCL19 by day 3 post-immunization in mice that were immunized with hCCL19-CH3 encoding DNA. These protein levels persisted for up to 14 days before declining to background levels (Fig.  b). In contrast, no detectable hCCL19 was observed in the serum of mice immunized with the hCCL19-CH3-Neo13 vaccine, most likely due to the lower expression levels (data not shown). From this we conclude that the vaccine protein product hCCL19-CH3-Neo13 either never reaches circulation or in levels below detection. If the latter, the protein is cleared within 14 days as seen for the positive control hCCL19-CH3 product.

## Safety pharmacology
Safety pharmacology, as conducted for therapeutic drugs, with effects on cardiovascular, respiratory, central nervous system and other central organs, is not typically done or required for personalized cancer immunotherapies. However, to investigate whether systemic spill over of CCL19 could lead to any unwanted acute reaction we administered mice systemically with high doses of recombinant hCCL19. To evaluate (1) the acute effect of high levels of CCL19 in the blood stream and (2) how long CCL19 remains in circulation, we administered 1 μg recombinant hCCL19 protein to mice via intravenous injection. When observing the mice closely from time of injection to 24 h post-injection with specific focus on behaviors such as activity and responsiveness, body posture and fur coat condition. No signs of distress were observed in the mice after intravenous (i.v.) administration of hCCL19 protein (data not shown).
To investigate the clearance kinetics of recombinant hCCL19, longitudinal blood samples were collected for serum hCCL19 quantification via ELISA analysis. The data revealed that hCCL19 was cleared from the blood stream following first-order kinetics, reaching non-detectable levels within two hours and exhibiting a half-life of less than 1 h (Fig.  c).
Taken together, the absence of any adverse effects in mice dosed with high concentration of pure recombinant hCCL19, combined with the undetectable systemic presence of the vaccine protein product hCCL19-CH3-Neo13, suggests that the inclusion of CCL19 in the vaccine construct is unlikely to pose any risk related to systemic exposure.

## Toxicology
We previously performed a dose escalation study for mCCL19-CH3-Neo13 with the vaccine dose ranging from 5 to 500 μg formulated with P188 and administered i.m. to determine the maximum tolerated dose [ ]. The vaccine was well tolerated at all doses, with no observable impact on animal well-being, establishing 500 μg as No Observed Adverse Effect Level (NOAEL) indicating a promising safety profile. A second objective of this study was to find the dose of the vaccine that would elicit the highest level on Neo13 specific T-cells. The data showed that both CD4+- and CD8+-activated neoantigen-specific T-cell responses reached a maximum magnitude at a dose of 50–100 μg, indicating no further immunological benefit with higher vaccine doses. Based on this, the maximum effective dose was considered to be 100 µg per mouse of the mCCL19-CH3-Neo13 DNA vaccine [ ].
In extension of this, a GLP repeated-dose toxicology study was performed in accordance with current GLP standards to assess any systemic toxic effects induced by the maximum effective dose of 100 µg per mouse of the mCCL19-CH3-Neo13 DNA vaccine co-formulated with P188 upon repeated i.m. injection as compared to control mice immunized with vehicle only (P188) (Fig.  ).
 During the study, blood cytokine levels and general clinical conditions were assessed. Evaluation of animal well-being included observation of body weight (BW) changes, food consumption and body temperature. Additionally, ophthalmological examination, hematology (peripheral blood) and blood chemistry were conducted in the live phase. Organ weights were determined, and micropathological and histopathological examinations were performed on multiple organs at endpoint. Antigen-specific T-cell induction was verified in splenocytes of satellite groups terminated over the course of the study (Fig.  ). In total, 165 male and 165 female 8- to 9-week-old BALB/c mice received mCCL19-CH3-Neo13 injections every two weeks for 14 weeks, with the last administration on the first day of Week 15 and necropsy the day after. In order to evaluate the reversibility of any potentially observed effects during the treatment phase, the mice were further monitored for a recovery period of three weeks (Fig.  a).
The intramuscular administration of mCCL19-CH3-Neo13 given once every two weeks for 14 weeks at 100 μg/mouse was well tolerated with no effect on the appearance and behavior of the animals. No mortalities and no mCCL19-CH3-Neo13-related clinical or injection site observations were reported. There were no effects on BW, food intake, body temperature or following ophthalmic investigations (data not shown).
To inform on systemic changes in cytokine levels upon repeated vaccination, a set of pre-defined cytokines was measured. A transient increase in circulating TNF-α (Fig.  b) and KC/GRO (Fig.  c) levels was observed 6 h after intramuscular administration of mCCL19-CH3-Neo13, returning to baseline by 24 h. This cytokine response was not observed in the control group receiving P188 alone, indicating that the effect was likely attributable to the DNA component rather than the poloxamer P188.
Antigen-specific T-cell induction in splenocytes was used as a surrogate measure for successful vaccine delivery and was measured in satellite groups terminated over the course of the study as indicated in Fig.  a. The vaccine-epitope-specific double-positive CD8+ and CD4+ T cells were detected from Study Day 42 and peaked at day 78 at 2.5% and 0.6%, respectively, confirming the delivery of the vaccine to the mice (Fig.  d, e).
Based on the data from the GLP study, it was concluded that mCCL19-CH3-Neo13 was well tolerated and did not cause any adverse changes at local or systemic level in mice, demonstrating a favorable safety profile of the DNA vaccine technology. The results are summarized in Table  .
 We next evaluated the long-term immunogenicity and safety of repeated doses of including booster immunizations mCCL19-CH3-Neo13 using the Tropis® NFIS.
We know from prior studies by Viborg et al. [ ] that adding a booster immunization for a DNA vaccine not only restores the magnitude of the T-cell response but significantly elevates it, as compared to the T-cell response achieved in and post the priming phase. Therefore, this study was designed to assess the safety of the response induced by booster vaccines as well as the added potency of using a DNA delivery device. We chose to use a high dose of 50 µg of the APCt DNA vaccine to challenge any potential safety concerns of boosters as well at delivery device, both potentiating the T-cell magnitude.
This approach was compared to mCCL19-CH3-Neo13 co-formulated with P188 and administered with standard needle injection and to untreated controls (Fig.  a).
 Mice were primed with weekly doses of mCCL19-CH3-Neo13 for eight consecutive weeks, followed by a booster dose on Study Day 161 and a second booster on Study Day 259. Throughout the study, the immune response was assessed by monitoring circulating C1-specific CD8+ T-cells, serum was collected for cytokine measurements, and satellite groups were sacrificed across the experiment as described in Fig.  a for T-cell analysis in splenocytes. The results show that mCCL19-CH3-Neo13 delivered via the Tropis® device or standard needle injections induced a comparable immune response onset as measured by C1-specific circulating CD8+ T-cells. The response became detectable by Study Day 15 following two immunizations, increased significantly by Study Day 30 and peaked at Study Day 37 during the priming phase. At this time point, comparable frequencies of antigen-specific CD8+ T-cells (approximately 5–8% C1-specific CD8+ T-cells) were detected in both groups. Booster immunization on Study Day 160 led to a threefold increase in neoantigen-specific T-cells, reaching approximately 14–20% in both treatment groups, and these elevated levels remained comparable between the groups (Fig.  b). Following the first booster, C1-specific CD8+ T-cells gradually declined over the subsequent 2.5 months from Study Day 175 to Study Day 248. At this point, mice received a second booster immunization on Study Day 258, which further enhanced the vaccine-induced immune response, resulting in C1-specific CD8+ T-cell frequencies of approximately 18–25% in both treatment groups. By the final time point (Study Day 368), frequencies remained robust, ranging from 15 to 20% (Fig.  b).
The immune response was further evaluated by assessing the induction of functional CD4+ and CD8+ T-cells in satellite groups terminated on day 160 and day 258 as well as upon termination of the study end at day 374.
The delivery of mCCL19-CH3-Neo13 by the Tropis® device seems to give rise to higher frequencies of neoepitope-reactive CD4+ and CD8+ T-cells in the satellite groups (Fig.  c, d). Especially the CD4+ T-cell levels seemed higher in the mice receiving the Tropis® delivered vaccine at the first satellite group analyzed at day 148. This was more noteworthy on Study Day 248 almost 90 days after the first booster vaccine giving rise to 1% antigen-reactive CD4+ T-cells as compared to 0.3% for standard needle-administered vaccine. The CD4+-specific T-cell remained higher for the Tropis® until endpoint (Fig.  c). The reactive CD8+ T-cells analyzed across the satellite groups at day 160, 258 and 374 had comparable levels across the two administration techniques as well as over time.
To summarize, both groups exhibited long-lasting reactive T-cells capable of recalling the antigen in the antigen cassette of the vaccine.
Neither group experienced weight loss or behavioral impairment from the booster, indicating that the vaccine was safe (data not shown).
Given that one of the few mCCL19-CH3-Neo13-related findings in the GLP study was a transient systemic cytokine peak immediately after dosing, we performed a cytokine multiplex analysis to evaluate the serum levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines after i.m. immunization with mCCL19-CH3-Neo13. Samples were collected prior to dosing and at 6 h, 24 h and 7 days after first vaccine dose, the eighth (final) dose of the priming phase and around the two booster immunizations (Fig.  a).
As expected, a transient spike in the serum levels of KC/GRO and TNF-α but also IL-6 and MCP-1 (Fig.  e, f, g and h, respectively) was observed 6 h after dosing, returning to baseline within 24 h. This response was likely activation of the innate immune system by the CpG-rich DNA plasmid itself, rather than by CCL19, as our prior data showed that in vivo expression of the hCCL19-CH3 DNA construct resulted in detectable serum CCL19 only after 24 h ( ).
Furthermore, the serum cytokine levels did not further increase over time with repeated dosing (Fig.  e, f, g and h) indicating a low likelihood of cytokine-mediated longer-term toxicity.
A transient increase in cytokine levels was observed immediately after dosing with the P188-formulated vaccine, which was higher than that seen with Tropis® delivery; however, this effect rapidly subsided.
Collectively, the data suggest that the dosing schedule and Tropis®-mediated delivery of the vaccine provides a safe approach for advancing the APCt DNA vaccine, as it elicited durable T-cell responses without significantly impacting systemic cytokine levels.

## The Antigen Cassette of the APCt DNA Vaccine is interchangeable with consistent efficacy
After confirming that mCCL19-CH3-Neo13 induced a strong T-cell response against the encoded antigens and was well tolerated at high doses, we investigated how varying the TSA composition in the DNA plasmid affected expression efficacy and immunogenicity. Therefore, DNA plasmids with antigen inserts of the same length but with different sequences were designed to mirror the expected variability in a potential clinical trial with personalized neoantigen vaccine potentiated by the CCl19 unit.
To translate the findings from the preclinical experiments to human designs, four DNA constructs were synthesized each containing an individual set of 13 human melanoma neoantigens (hM 1–4). The four different neoantigen cassettes were inserted into the hCCL19-CH3 plasmid backbone. The neoantigens were selected by the AI-Immunology™ model PIONEER™ using sequencing data from human tumor samples.
We assessed the four personalized melanoma hCCL19-CH3-Neo13 constructs for neoantigen cassette transcription, protein expression and the secretion of the expressed fusion protein as these steps are instrumental for stimulation of APCs in the injection site tissue (Fig.  a).
 First, to confirm the complete transcription of the ectopically expressed neoantigen inserts, quantitative PCR analysis (qPCR) was conducted. The results demonstrated that all neoantigen cassettes were expressed in the hCCL19-CH3 plasmid backbone, with no significant differences in transcriptional levels. This confirms that the vaccine vector is suitable for interchanging neoantigen cassette designs independent of their sequence composition (Fig.  b).
Furthermore, to investigate secretion of the fusion protein expressed from the CCL19-CH3-Neo13 plasmid, the supernatant from the in vitro cultured HEK293T cells after transfection with the different vaccine designs was analyzed using a CCL19 ELISA. The ELISA results confirmed that the CCL19 part of the fusion protein was expressed and secreted from the cells into the supernatant in detectable amounts (Fig.  c). The secretion levels were comparable across the different hCCL19-CH3-Neo13 plasmid designs containing different neoantigen cassettes, respectively (Fig.  c).
To determine whether the transcribed mRNA successfully translated into protein, total protein was isolated from the transfected HEK293 cells. Cell lysates were subjected to SDS-PAGE and analyzed by immunoblotting with an anti-His-tag antibody. The results confirm that all measured transcripts were translated into proteins of the expected protein size, approximately 50 kDa, corresponding to the monomeric form. Additionally, lower molecular weight bands were also visible, most likely representing proteolytic degradation products generated during sample preparation. As absolute protein concentrations were not determined, protein levels were assessed qualitatively. Therefore, while differences in detected protein levels were observed between the four tested plasmid designs, definitive conclusions regarding their relative expression efficacy could not be drawn, and, neither could expression levels be correlated with secretion levels measured by CCL19 ELISA (Fig.  c).
Lastly, the ability to induce an immune response to the four hCCL19-CH3-Neo13 designs was tested in vivo in a murine immunization study. These BALB/c mice were used as in silico analysis revealed the presence of murine MHC (i.e., H-2d) ligands within the majority of the human neoantigens encoded in the plasmids.
Immunization of mice with the hCCL19-CH3-Neo13 encoding human neoantigens elicited an antigen-specific T-cell response, demonstrated by peptide pool restimulation and IFN-γ ELISpot to detect antigen-specific T-cells. This supports the observation that the APCt vaccine platform can effectively induce an immune response against multiple different sets of neoantigens introduced in the antigen cassette (Fig.  e).
To summarize, this demonstrates that the APCt DNA vaccine platform can effectively express, secrete and maintain the integrity of antigenic products, independent of the antigenic payload making it suitable for individualized/personalized vaccine designs.

## Combinations of different types of TSAs can be incorporated into the APCt DNA Vaccine to broaden eligible patient population
Previous analysis reveals that not all patients with solid cancers harbor enough tumor mutations and thereby inadequate neoantigens for effective vaccination. This limitation has prompted us to explore alternative antigen sources, such as non-conventional antigens derived from the dark genome, as potential complements to neoantigens in a vaccine setting. Combining both antigen types could broaden therapeutic coverage, including for low-/medium-TMB cancers, where few high-quality neoantigens are typically identified.
We previously demonstrated that DNA vaccination with non-conventional antigens derived from ERV sequences could induce antitumor immunity in the CT26 mouse tumor model [ ]. To evaluate whether neoantigens and ERV-derived antigens can co-exist in an anti-tumor vaccine without interfering with each other immunogenicity, we incorporated both antigen types into the cassette of the CCL19 vaccine platform and tested their ability to induce T-cell-mediated tumor killing in the CT26 model.
Mice were immunized with a CCL19-CH3-Neo5/Erv8 vaccine, encoding five neoantigens (C1–C5) from the original CCL19-CH3-Neo13 construct and eight in silico identified ERV antigens or vaccines containing either neoantigens alone (CCL19-CH3-Neo5) or ERV antigens alone (CCL19-CH3-Erv8) (Fig.  a). Similar anti-tumor results were observed all three groups, independent of vaccine antigen composition.. This indicates that multiple different TSA cassettes, even when comprising different antigen classes, can mediate robust anti-tumor efficacy (Fig.  c).
 To further investigate the interaction of the two classes of antigens encoded in the same vaccine, we addressed the vaccine-induced immune activation. First, circulating neoantigen-specific T-cells were assessed by monitoring C1-specific CD8+ T-cells in blood of mice immunized with either neoantigen alone (CCL19-CH3-Neo5) or a combination of ERVs and neoantigens (CCL19-CH3-Neo5/Erv8). On Study Day −1, both mice immunized with CCL19-CH3-Neo5 and CCL19-CH3-Neo5/Erv8 showed a significant increase in C1-specific T-cells compared to negative controls (Fig.  d) and these levels were maintained through Study Day 14 (Fig.  e).
Following study termination, splenocytes from each treatment group were analyzed to assess immune recognition of the individual neoantigens and ERV antigens. The samples were re-stimulated with either pooled or individual peptides corresponding to vaccine-encoded antigens. Mice treated with CCL19-CH3-Neo5/Erv8 showed strong response to the pooled peptides (Fig.  f). Deconvolution of the response to understand the contribution of individual epitopes revealed that five of the neoantigens and five of the ERV antigens in the vaccine gave rise to detectable activated T-cells, both in their individual vaccines but also in the vaccine with combined neoantigens and ERV antigens (Fig.  f). The data suggest that neoantigens and ERV-derived antigens can be co-delivered in a vaccine and do not compete for immunogenicity, as the individual T-cell levels for each epitope are comparable whether the antigens are delivered as ERVs alone (CCL19-CH3-Erv8), neoantigens alone (CCL19-CH3-Neo13) or a combination (CCL19-CH3-Neo5/Erv8).
Taken together, these results demonstrate effective tumor control and a strong immunogenicity induced by the CCL19-CH3-Neo5/Erv8 construct. Our findings show that the antigenic cassette can be successfully expanded to include both neo- and ERV antigens, highlighting the versatility and therapeutic potential the APCt DNA vaccine for personalized cancer treatment.

## Discussion
DNA vaccines offer several advantages over other vaccine platforms, including thermostability, ease of manufacturing, cost-effectiveness and a favorable safety profile. These advantages have led to successful approvals of DNA-based vaccines for veterinary use and, more recently, the approval of a human DNA vaccine for SARS-CoV-2 [ ]. Their stability and limited cold-chain requirements make DNA an attractive modality for both prophylactic and therapeutic vaccine applications, including personalized cancer vaccines [ ].
Encouragingly, early clinical data support the use of personalized DNA vaccines in cancer therapy. In a Phase I study, EVX-02, a personalized neoantigen DNA vaccine administered in combination with a-PD-1 therapy, induced robust neoantigen-specific T-cell responses in patients with resected melanoma, with all patients remaining relapse-free at last follow-up [ ]. Similarly, clinical-stage personalized vaccines, including Nykode’s APC-targeting and Geneos’ Interleukin-12 enhanced personalized neoantigen DNA vaccines, have demonstrated safety and immunogenicity [ , ]. These findings highlight the potential of DNA vaccines in personalized immunotherapy. A critical determinant of therapeutic efficacy in cancer vaccination is the induction of an early and robust tumor-reactive T-cell response, particularly crucial in patients with advanced or high-risk disease. Key parameters such as delivery technology, dosing strategies, antigen selection and APC targeting significantly influence vaccine efficacy. Advances in electroporation and NFIS (e.g., Stratis®, Tropis®) enhance DNA uptake and immunogenicity. At the immunological level, strategies that direct antigens to APCs via chemokines such as GM-CSF, CCL3 or CCL19 have shown promise in improving T-cell priming [ , , , ].
Building on these observations, we developed a next-generation DNA vaccine incorporating CCL19 as an APC-targeting domain to improve the delivery and presentation of tumor-specific antigens. To further enhance uptake and immunogenicity, we tested the vaccine in combination with electroporation or the Tropis® injection system. Both delivery methods significantly improved vaccine-induced immune responses. Notably, Tropis® delivery of the mCCL19-CH3-Neo13 vaccine, using a booster regimen, resulted in durable long-term immunity without observable adverse effects or sustained changes in systemic cytokine levels, supporting the safety and effectiveness of this delivery strategy.
The developed vaccine encodes TSAs, including neoantigens and ERV antigens. Our preclinical results demonstrated that CCL19-mediated APC targeting significantly improved anti-tumor efficacy, induced stronger antigen-specific T-cell responses and enabled a fivefold dose reduction compared to non-targeted DNA vaccines. Additionally, T-cell responses were detected earlier, consistent with more efficient engagement of APCs.
Importantly, safety was confirmed in GLP toxicology studies and biodistribution analyses. No systemic toxicity was observed, and the vaccine-encoded CCL19 fusion protein showed limited systemic presence and transient expression. Furthermore, intravenous administration of recombinant CCL19 was well tolerated, confirming the safety of this chemokine in vivo.
Tumor antigen selection remains central to cancer vaccine design. Historically, cancer vaccines focused on tumor-associated antigens (TAAs), self-antigens aberrantly expressed in tumors, such as HER2, NY-ESO-1 or MAGE family proteins [ ]. While early-phase clinical trials demonstrated that TAA-based vaccines are safe and immunogenic, late-phase trials showed limited clinical benefit, likely due to immune tolerance and off-tumor expression [ ]. Sipuleucel-T, targeting the TAA PAP, remains the only approved cancer vaccine to date and demonstrated a 28.3% reduction in mortality in advanced prostate cancer [ ].
TSAs including neoantigens and ERVs are highly specific to tumor cells and thus offer greater potential for effective immunotherapy. Neoantigen vaccines have shown clinical promise, as demonstrated in the KEYNOTE-942 Phase IIb trial [ ], where the combination of Moderna’s mRNA-4157 (V940) and pembrolizumab significantly prolonged recurrence-free survival in high-risk melanoma patients, reducing the risk of recurrence or death by 44% compared to pembrolizumab alone. Similarly, our data support the use of APC-targeted DNA vaccines in combination with checkpoint inhibitors as a combinatorial approach, as evidenced by our preclinical study with the DNA vaccine and a-PD-1 blockade.
Moreover, the inclusion of ERV antigens could broaden the clinical applicability of personalized vaccines, particularly in patients with low tumor mutational burden (TMB). Since ERV expression does not correlate with neoantigen load or TMB, these antigens represent valuable targets that may extend the benefit of DNA vaccines a wider range of cancer patients [ ].
In conclusion, we present a next-generation DNA vaccine platform incorporating CCL19 as an APC-targeting chemokine, enabling potent immune priming and efficient delivery of tumor-specific antigens. This platform improves immunogenicity, maintains a favorable safety profile and supports the development of personalized cancer vaccines targeting both neoantigens and ERVs. The data presented here provides a strong foundation for clinical translation.
